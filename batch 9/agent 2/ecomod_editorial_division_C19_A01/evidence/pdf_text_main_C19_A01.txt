A rising biocapacity total can mask ecological-capital
land drawdown: a two-land model
Amin Abaee a, ∗
a Independent Researcher, Tehran, Iran
Abstract
Biocapacity combines land area and productivity in an aggregate annual ac-
count. We examine the information retained by this total using a conserved-
area two-land model with delayed ecological regeneration, demographic re-
sponse and an imposed quality constraint. Conversion increases the model
account when the receiving land class has the higher per-hectare contribution,
even as ecological-capital land declines. In a representative simulation, bioca-
pacity increases 39%, capital-land area decreases approximately 46%, and its
modeled account contribution decreases 51.72%. Sharp static bounds quan-
tify how independent land-area and quality observations restrict the compat-
ible allocation set. Integrated contribution budgets distinguish conversion,
maintenance and retirement, restoration, quality and fast-yield eects. In
the 100-year xed-demand simulation, conversion contributes 0.547925 gha,
while the net account increase is 0.231310 gha; other contributions largely
oset conversion. Budget closure improves under timestep renement. The
reduced xed-demand equality relation reaches the minimum-area boundary
without a fold or critical slowing; this result is specic to that comparison.
Separately, National Footprint Accounts Edition 2026 values for 19612023
show world biocapacity increasing 24.86%, biocapacity per person decreasing
52.71%, and production footprint per person increasing 16.73%. Cropland,
forest and built-up accounts contribute +24.06, −5.95 and +7.44 percentage
points, respectively, to total biocapacity change. These are annual account
changes rather than physical-stock observations. The results support report-
ing constituent accounts alongside independently measured ecological assets.

1. Introduction
In ecology, carrying capacity describes the population a resource base
can sustain. Many models treat it as a xed ceiling, in part because it
is dicult to estimate (Cohen, 1995). If resource use changes the basis of
future production, however, a xed ceiling may not capture that change. An
aggregate production-based measure may increase despite a decline in the
area of a constituent land class.
The National Footprint and Biocapacity Accounts (NF A) report biolog-
ical supply as biocapacity and human demand as the Ecological Footprint.
Both are expressed in global hectares (gha), which adjust area for biological
productivity relative to the world average in a given year; the annual basis
is implicit (Global Footprint Network, 2009, 2019). Because a global hectare
combines area and productivity, a change in the total does not by itself
show whether yields, area, or the mix of land uses changed. Distinguishing
these mechanisms requires information on the constituent areas and their
per-hectare contributions.
In composite-indicator theory, this ambiguity is called compensation : a
gain in one component can oset a loss in another (Nardo et al., 2008; Becker
et al., 2017; Fischer et al., 2022). The aggregate remains useful, but reading
it alone can conceal component changes. Related critiques discuss the inter-
pretation of the Ecological Footprint and biocapacity accounts (Blomqvist
et al., 2013; Giampietro and Saltelli, 2014; van den Bergh and Grazi, 2015;
Galli et al., 2016).
Most humanenvironment models summarize nature as an aggregate
stock or a unied feedback. Examples include the World3 simulations (Mead-
ows et al., 1972, 1974, 2004), models of Easter Island (Brander and Taylor,
1998; Anderies, 2000), and coupled Earth-system frameworks (Motesharrei et
al., 2016; Donges et al., 2017; Calvin and Bond-Lamberty, 2018; Henderson
and Loreau, 2018). Other work debates whether human carrying capacity is
xed, variable, or useful as a concept (Cohen, 1995; van den Bergh and Ri-
etveld, 2004; Simon, 1996; Meyer, 1994; Meyer and Ausubel, 1999; Dasgupta,
2021); global demographic assessments often project population separately
from environmental constraints (United Nations, 2022). Land-use studies,
meanwhile, document conversion among forests, grasslands, wetlands, and
2
cropland (Haberl et al., 2007; Krausmann et al., 2013; Erb et al., 2017).
These studies motivate an analysis of the information retained by aggregate
biocapacity when land classes dier in yield and regeneration.
Annual biological provision and the ecological assets supporting that pro-
vision are distinct quantities.
Analogies. To make the accounting issue concrete, imagine an orchard with
fast-provisioning ground, such as intensively managed beds, beside slower-
renewing ground with trees and living soil. Annual harvest is a ow ; trees,
roots, and soil are assets that support future ows. In our model, a shortfall
can trigger a transfer from ecological-capital land to fast-provisioning land.
If the receiving class has a higher annual contribution per hectare in that
scenario, the conversion term adds to B at the margin while capital-land
area Ac falls; other ows can oset that increment. This is a conditional
model mechanism, not evidence that NF A accounts measure physical stocks
or that a particular real-world conversion occurred. Replanting may restore a
later harvest, but it is not a one-for-one measure of the condition or recovery
of the distinct assets represented by the model's capital-land class. The
analogy separates current output from the state of its productive base; it
does not assert that the classes map directly to NF A categories.
A second analogy separates observed service from structural condition:
an elevator rated for ten people might continue operating while carrying four-
teen, but the ride itself does not establish whether repeated overloading has
changed the cable's safety margin. Continued service is not a measurement
of structural condition, nor does the analogy predict inevitable failure. Re-
lated material-ledger work discusses a broader owstock masking problem;
here we study its composition-specic form in a biocapacity model (Abaee,
2026c).
Research questions. This paper addresses three questions:
1. The masking dynamic: Under what conditions can conversion raise
total model biocapacity while Ac declines, how long can this divergence
last, and can the aggregate identify the land shift?
2. System trajectory: Under the model's stated assumptions, does
drawdown involve a fold, hysteresis, or critical slowing, or does the
reduced comparison follow another path?
3
3. Limits and recovery: What limits drawdown under xed demand,
and what modelled process allows capital-land area to recover?
Core contributions. We make three contributions:
 We construct a dynamic two-land model that conserves total area, rep-
resents capital-land quality separately from area, and includes delays
in ecological regeneration and demographic response.
 We derive the condition under which a conversion term raises aggre-
gate biocapacity while Ac falls, quantify sharp static identication in-
tervals under independent observation packages, and compute signed
integrated contribution budgets with renement and closure checks. In
a reduced xed-demand comparison, the balance relation reaches the
area oor without a fold or critical slowing; realized paths in the de-
layed model remain history-dependent. These are conditional model
results, not general claims about observed ecosystems.
 We decompose NF A Edition 2026 account values for 19612023 and
examine a separate cropland-area/yield proxy. The account series show
a compositional change, not a census of physical ecological stocks.
Finally, we derive ve conditional predictions and specify their observa-
tion requirements and rejection criteria (SI S11). The imposed-cap compar-
ison is an internal consistency test rather than an ecological generalization.
The model parameters are representative rather than tted, so simulations
illustrate conditional mechanisms rather than historical forecasts. Aggregate
biocapacity is therefore most informative when read alongside its component
accounts.

Core system feedbacks. The equations permit two conditional feedbacks:
1. Compositional-yield feedback. When fast land contributes more
per hectare than capital land ( bf > Υc(q)), conversion raises B at the
margin while reducing Ac. With endogenous population, higher B can
raise delayed carrying capacity K and later population and demand,
potentially creating another shortfall. Maintenance, retirement, qual-
ity change, and the other model terms also aect B; this is a model
feedback, not an empirical causal nding.

2. Decityield feedback. A shortfall adds to D, while repayment

reduce B and deepen the shortfall. The state D is the model's leaky
decit measure, not a direct measure of nutrient depletion, aquifer over-
draft, or other physical processes.
2.2. Parameter selection and empirical grounding
Table 1 summarizes the baseline parameter values and their evidence sta-
tus. Parameters are representative rather than econometrically tted; the
model is designed to isolate structural mechanisms, not to generate precise
historical forecasts. To keep the model tractable, it consolidates forests,
soils, wetlands, and other slow-renewing systems into one eective capital-
land class, although their extent, condition, yields, and recovery dynamics
dier substantially. Published forest, sh-stock, and soil studies use heteroge-
neous recovery denitions and document system-specic responses (SI S5.5)
(Poorter et al., 2016; Hutchings and Reynolds, 2004; Neubauer et al., 2013;
Poeplau et al., 2011). We do not ret a common recovery curve or compute
harmonized t50 or τr values from those records here. They are measurement
and future-modeling precedents only; they do not estimate ρc or τg, validate
the present generic quality equation, or identify its parameters.
Two data-based quantities provide context for cropland but do not cali-
brate the model: the NF A cropland account value per unit area and a sepa-
rate cereal-yield/cropland-area proxy (SI S5). Their boundaries do not map
one-to-one onto model fast land, and neither is a tted value of bf or a direct
measure of ecological capital. Finally, the baseline assumes bf − Υc(q) > 0.
This is an explicit conditional modeling regime for examining when a short-
term contribution premium can mask a change in land composition, not a
universal empirical claim.

2.4. Numerical implementation
Fixed-demand intervention.. In the controlled xed-demand runs, we over-
ride E = eP with E(t) = ¯E and set Rrc = 0 . Population still follows its
10
displayed demographic equation, but no longer determines demand in that
intervention. Thus xed demand and restoration-o are two explicit scenario
settings, not consequences of demographic equilibrium. Endogenous-demand
restoration runs retain E = eP and the stated restoration gate.
We integrate the delay equations by the method of steps with explicit
rst-order Euler updates. Current states are read at the preceding node;
time-dependent inputs and delayed values are evaluated at the new time,
with linear interpolation of the delayed q and B histories. The delayed quality
velocity is projected before each update, followed by clipping to Kq as a nite-
step safeguard. Reserve area is reconstructed from total-area conservation,
the positive-part operator is exact, and the stated bounds on Ac, Af , q, P, D,
and K are enforced. The reserve threshold is monitored rather than pro-
jected, and the optional fast-land ceiling is inactive. Baseline demonstra-
tions and threshold bisections use ∆t = 0 .05 yr; broader drawdown sweeps
use 0.1 yr, with selected thresholds rechecked at 0.05 yr.
Pre-run histories cover the full longest active delay window,
[− max{τg, τp}, 0], with q(s) = q0 and B(s) = B0 calculated from the
initial state. In the matched technology-wave experiment, both runs share
the no-wave prehistory; the active Tb(0) changes current B(0), and later
wave-adjusted B values enter the delayed carrying-capacity input. SI S3

3.1. When conversion raises the total as capital land declines
State versus annual readout.. The term bG,cGc(q) values a logistic growth

to 1, whereas Υc falls from 0.05576 to 0.05 gha ha −1. This is an intentional
growth-ow observation rule, not evidence that improved ecological condition

the direct-yield component is larger in this case.

We call a trajectory with rising B and falling Ac a composition mask . A

3.4. Recovery requires an explicit, gated restoration ow
Conversion moves land from capital to fast use. The model has no route
back to capital land unless restoration Rrc is active; fast-land retirement
returns land to reserve instead. Lowering population, footprint, or shortfall
therefore does not, by itself, reverse earlier conversion. With χr = 0, Ac stays
at its post-conversion level.
In the projected delayed-quality run ( Ac0 = 1 .0 ha, P0 = 2 .0 cap,
χr = 0.10 gha −1 yr −1, T = 400 yr), Ac falls to about 0.398 ha and then recov-
ers to Ac(T ) = 0.677 ha. The recovery fraction is Rc = (Ac(T )−Adegc )/(Ainitc −
Adegc ) = 0.46, matching the delayed-quality recovery table in the Supplemen-
tary Information (SI Table S5; ∆t = 0 .1 yr). Rening this same case to
∆t = 0 .00078125 yr gives min Ac = 0 .401217 ha and Ac(T ) = 0 .678295 ha;
recovery remains partial (Section 3.6; SI S10).
The two restoration gates operate under dierent conditions. Surplus-
gated restoration is inactive during a shortfall, but may continue after a
surplus opens while population adjusts. Reaching a specied endpoint there-
fore requires a target-limited rule (SI S4.4). Decit-gated restoration acts
during shortfall, when supply is already insucient. At the tested rates, con-
version outpaces restoration and drawdown continues. The result depends on
timing and rates; neither gate is universally preferable. SI Table S6 reports
all nine gate and conversion-freeze policies at three timesteps.

The model yields ve conditional predictions, with an operational test
map in SI Table S18 and sampling and uncertainty rules in SI S11. Aggre-
gate B alone cannot test them: the composition mechanism requires inde-
pendent observations or defensible proxies for Ac, fast-land area, and yield;
testing delay, recovery, and demand-threshold predictions also requires rele-
vant time series and intervention or demographic data.
P1. Conversion can raise the total under a specic condition. In
the baseline xed-demand run, ˙Ac < 0 while ˙B >0 for a nite interval
(Fig. 1a). Conversion adds to B exactly when ∆bconv > 0, although
other ows, quality, debt, technology, and restoration determine the net
change. With baseline shares σf = σc = 1, conversion requires demand
to exceed total biocapacity ( RB = E/B > 1). The reserved-share ex-
tension can permit conversion when RB ≤ 1, but that is not a baseline
result. A composition mask is rising B with falling Ac; a liquidation
mask is steady E with falling Ac. The two patterns may overlap but
are not interchangeable. The sign of ∆bconv describes conversion's con-
tribution, not necessarily the net change in B.
P2. The duration depends on model rates, not a universal horizon.
The slow return rate λ1 (along with τconv, µ, ηf , and bc/bf ) sets the
timescale; ∆bconv and the initial shortfall aect the size of the change.
In the baseline, most conversion occurs within one to two e-folding times,
when the remaining gap shrinks by a factor of e.
P3. The reduced model has no fold-type early warning. Return time
does not grow as demand approaches the ceiling, and drawdown depth
changes continuously with demand (Fig. 1c). The model instead signals
that Ac is approaching its minimum. It predicts no fold-related warning
before that boundary.
P4. The baseline 20/40-year comparison is saturation-induced. At
q(s) = 0 .9, ρc = 0.08 yr −1 and qmax = 1, the projected quality reaches
its imposed ceiling at 125/9 ≈ 13.8889 yr, before either τg = 20 or 40 yr
elapses. Those quality paths and matched outcomes therefore coincide;
the τg = 5 yr case is distinct. The nite comparison does not establish
general delay robustness (SI Table S2).

4. Discussion
4.1. What the two-land model shows
The two-land model yields three results relevant to humanenvironment
models (Schaefer, 1954; May, 1973; Meadows et al., 1972, 2004; Wackernagel
and Rees, 1996; Brander and Taylor, 1998). First, conversion can add to
total biocapacity while Ac falls. Whether B rises overall also depends on
other land ows and quality change; Proposition 1 gives the exact condition
for the conversion term. The technology boost is capped, while debt depends
on the magnitude and duration of shortfall (Proposition 3).
Second, under the reduced xed-demand assumptions ( q = qmax, D =
0, technology boost o, and no restoration), the equality balance relation
declines steadily with Ac until it meets the area oor. It does not fold back.
The absence of a fold or critical slowing applies to this equality relation, not
to every trajectory or to the separate surplus no-conversion family. With
one-way conversion and no return ow, Ac remains below its pre-overshoot
level after conversion stops, although it no longer declines at the equality
point (Table 6). Third, recovery requires an explicit return ow; its eect
depends on whether the restoration gate opens during a shortfall or a surplus.
SI S7 reports a separate one-stock comparator, not a result for the two-
land state Ac. Its full delayed trajectory (SI Fig. S1), xed-demand masking
example (SI Fig. S2), selected regeneration-delay trajectories (SI Fig. S3),
and nite-grid endpoint classication (SI Fig. S4) use comparator-specic
states and parameters. At the illustrated no-debt settings its equilibrium re-
lation is monotone; these runs establish neither a fold nor a universal critical
delay or collapse mechanism in the two-land model.
These results also clarify when to expect common early-warning indica-
tors. Rising autocorrelation and variance are associated with approaching
a fold (Scheer et al., 2001). This reduced model reaches a minimum-area
boundary instead, so those indicators need not rise rst. Their absence would
not establish safety. The relevant model signal is the path of Ac, which the
aggregate alone cannot reveal.

4.2. Measurement implications
Biocapacity in gha is a value-weighted total, not an inventory of land
classes. A 20% increase cannot distinguish modest gains across all compo-
nents from a large gain in one that osets losses elsewhere. Proposition 1(iii)
formalizes the limit: even with xed per-hectare yields, the total and the
conserved-area constraint leave multiple feasible allocations among fast, cap-
ital, and reserve land.
A related transition-assessment study denes a linear compensatory op-
erator and compares perfect substitutability with less-substitutable CES al-
ternatives (Abaee, 2026b). The model total B is also a linear sum, but the
companion analyzes a dierent, nite-plan setting. There, the compensatory
protocol accepts states even when no single available plan meets every compo-
nent oor (compensatory-acceptance result in Abaee 2026b). The diagonal-
gap analysis in Abaee (2026b) identies an exact interval of diagonal-gap
states on its witness-dened region that each positive elasticity falsely cer-
ties as safe; thus no positive-elasticity member is uniformly safe on that
region. If feasibility is required throughout a transition, falling below a land-
class minimum remains a violation even if the endpoint later recovers.
For the NF A, these ndings concern account composition, not physical
stocks. Section 3.7 reports the Edition 2026 aggregate, per-person and com-
ponent changes; their contrast shows why an aggregate and its constituents
answer dierent questions. None of those account values measures forest
biomass or condition, soil carbon, wetland extent, sh biomass, or another
physical stock. In particular, a decline in Forest biocapacity does not es-
tablish a decline in forest biomass, and Built-up land is an NF A accounting
class, not ecological capital (Lo et al., 2026; Borucke et al., 2013; Blomqvist
et al., 2013; Giampietro and Saltelli, 2014; Galli et al., 2016; van den Bergh
and Grazi, 2015).
The model baseline likewise illustrates an accounting mechanism rather
than an observed global trend (Fig. 1a). With restoration o, one-way con-
version leaves Ac below its starting value after the shortfall closes; it then
remains at the lower balance level rather than declining indenitely. This
conditional result is distinct from the NF A evidence.
We assembled no common-boundary global physical-stock series for these
components and therefore report no physical-stock composite or global de-
cline nding. SI Table S12 is an unpopulated measurement specicationnot
an inventorywith candidate stock, extent, condition, and annual-ow mea-
sures and the requirements for comparing them; it contains no endpoint

values. Until spatial and temporal boundaries, units, uncertainty, overlap,
valuation or normalization, and permitted substitution are specied, these
unlike measures should remain a nonadditive vector. SEEA Ecosystem Ac-
counting also reports ecosystem extent and condition separately from ecosys-
tem services and monetary presentations (United Nations et al., 2021).
The external cropland-area and cereal-yield series provide an illustrative
proxy, not direct measurements of the model's land classes. The proxy is
anchored to the 1961 NF A cropland share; cereal yield is a productivity
indicator, not an all-crops measure, and the area and account boundaries do
not match by construction. External cropland area Af is in hectares and
index-scaled yield ˆbf in gha ha −1, so X = ˆbf Af and C emp = B − X are
account amounts in gha. The residual can absorb boundary and calibration
dierences, other account components, and conversion-factor changes. It is
neither Υc(q)Ac nor a physical-stock measure. Physical interpretation would
require independent, boundary-consistent measures of area, condition, yield,
and regeneration, together with a validated mapping and valuation. Forest
area could inform one part of the eective capital class, but cannot represent
the entire class or its condition.
4.3. Policy controls and limits
The conversion-rate law in Eq. (5) preserves the capital-land oor from
safe initial states. For y = Ac − Aminc ≥ 0, 0 ≤ uc ≤ y/τconv and Rrc ≥ 0
imply ˙y ≥ −y/τconv, hence y(t) ≥ y(0)e−t/τconv. The oor is approached
asymptotically; a stop is an operational safeguard rather than an addi-
tional continuous-time guarantee. A ceiling on fast-land area constrains Af ,
whereas the capital-land minimum concerns Ac; the two restrictions are dis-
tinct. Constraint preservation also diers from state identication. The
constant choice uc = 0 preserves the capital-land oor without observing Ac,
but does not generally meet a provision objective. Policies that depend on
remaining capital-land area require observations or valid bounds for Ac be-
yond aggregate B. Joint provision and area objectives therefore need to be
assessed under specied admissible controls and state information.
The restoration gates also behave dierently. Surplus-gated restoration
is inactive during a shortfall; decit-gated restoration acts during the short-
fall and can reduce provision further. This comparison echoes the distinc-
tion between protective and mobilizing institutional feedback in related work
(Abaee, 2026a). With a common reserved share, σf = σc = σ < 1, con-
version is inactive while E ≤ σB. With separate shares, the condition is

not to spatially protected area. Half-Earth, by contrast, is a spatial con-
servation target (Wilson, 2016). The connection is only the abstract idea
of reservation: the model neither maps protected areas nor evaluates that
proposal. Robustness to disturbances and parameter uncertainty remains
untested.
4.4. Relation to prior work
The ledger companion distinguishes weak and strong sustainability as op-
erating regimes according to whether material loops close at throughput. In
its terms, a positive weighted sum ranks outcomes under weak sustainabil-
ity, whereas componentwise adequacy is the strong-sustainability predicate
(Abaee, 2026c). The model total B is an annual biocapacity-account total.
The link is an analogy about compensation, not an identity with the com-
panion's material-ow ledger or a complete sustainability classication. The
assessment companion studies aggregate measures and typed transition crite-
ria in a separate nite-plan setting (Abaee, 2026b); the governance compan-
ion studies institutional feedback (Abaee, 2026a). The delays here represent
ecological regeneration and demographic response, not institutional review.
4.5. Limitations
Several limitations set the scope of the results. Ecological-capital land
combines unlike assets, so its single quality state, projected regeneration
rule, composition diagnostic, and demand ceiling are eective model quan-
titiesnot estimates for forests, soils, or wetlands. The quality index is
uncalibrated; qmax = 1 is an imposed admissibility bound, and ρc and τg
are scenario parameters rather than estimates from recovery studies. Most
other parameters are representative rather than tted. A cross-section cannot
identify bc, bG,c, and ρc separately. Recovery records from dierent systems
and external measurement frameworks are precedents for future ecosystem-
specic measurement and modeling only; they do not validate the present
equation or parameter values. The records describe regeneration responses,
not the delay τg. We hold the per-person footprint constant to isolate feed-
backs among land, biocapacity, and demand. A changing footprint would
require another model extension.
The composition pattern depends on demand and on the mechanisms in-
cluded here; under the stated assumptions, it is bounded. The exact shortfall

switch makes stability at S = 0 one-sided. Drawdown thresholds are re-
ported as proximity bands because they depend on tolerance and time step.
We x debt repayment at η = 0.05 yr −1; the zero-repayment limit ( η → 0),
which removes the debt equilibrium, is not analyzed. NF A accounts combine
area and yield and therefore cannot identify them separately. Finally, the
current-quality comparator Gc(q(t)) is retained only as a sensitivity check; it

initial-population threshold by at most 0.001 cap (SI S3).
5. Conclusions
Aggregate biocapacity alone generally does not identify changes in its
underlying land allocation. In the two-land model, conversion raises total B
at the margin when fast-land contribution per hectare exceeds capital land's
eective value ( bf > Υc(q)), while Ac falls if restoration is o (Proposition 1).
Other model terms determine whether total B rises overall. This is a con-
ditional accounting mechanism, not an empirical census of global physical
stocks.
The reduced xed-demand equality relation reaches the specied
minimum-area boundary without a fold or critical slowing. This is a
statement about that relation, not the separate xed-demand surplus
no-conversion family or every trajectory of the delayed model. Realized
paths remain history-dependent: when shortfalls subside and restoration
is o, Ac remains below its pre-overshoot level, but it does not continue
to decline at the balance point. In the representative baseline run, total
model biocapacity rises 39% while ecological-capital area falls 46.16%, its
per-hectare quality term Υc(q) falls 10.33%, and its modeled contribution
Υc(q)Ac falls 51.72%.
Independent yield and land observations turn the static non-identication
claim into explicit compatible-area intervals; the example narrows from
[0.05, 1.658163] to [0.606061, 0.8] ha when fast-land area is independently
known. The integrated budgets quantify conversion alongside the osetting
channels with measured nite-step closure errors. Neither result identies
physical capital from NF A account totals or establishes robustness to other
delay histories.
Separately, NF A Edition 2026 world account values rise 24.86% between
1961 and 2023, while biocapacity per person B/P falls 52.71% and the pro-

duction footprint per person rises 16.73%. Cropland contributes +24.06 per-
centage points, Forest biocapacity −5.95, and Built-up land +7.44 as an ac-
counting class. These normalized annual account values are not observations
of physical stocks or model Ac.
No common-boundary global physical-stock series was assembled, so we
report no composite or global physical-stock decline nding. The SI measure-
ment specication sets out candidate measures and comparison requirements;
it is not an empirical inventory. Physical measures with dierent units and
boundaries cannot be combined into one stock total without explicit treat-
ment of uncertainty, overlap, valuation or normalization, and allowed substi-
tution. The auxiliary proxy residual C emp = B − ˆbf Af is neither a physical-
capital measure nor the model contribution Υc(q)Ac; a future comparison
would require independent observations and a validated mapping.
Component accounts and independent physical observations provide com-
plementary information and should be reported alongside the aggregate. Re-
turning to the orchard metaphor, a ledger of annual harvest alone cannot tell
whether the trees, roots, and soil that support future harvests have changed.
That physical record requires independent, comparable observations; it can-
not be inferred from the account total. A harvest total is not a census of the
orchard.
