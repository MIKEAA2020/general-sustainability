**When institutions, not just nature, take too long to respond**

Delays are a well-known source of trouble in managing renewable resources such as fish stocks. Most research has focused on nature’s own lags—how long it takes fish to mature, or populations to rebound after being harvested. This paper examines a different delay: the time that elapses between noticing that a stock is declining and actually changing harvesting rules.

The analysis uses a simple three-part model: the size of the harvested stock, a running “memory” of how badly it is falling short of sustainable levels, and the current intensity of harvesting effort (kept within realistic bounds). Two institutional response styles are compared. Under a *mobilising* rule, a worsening shortage triggers even more harvesting effort—think fleet expansion or extra licences when catches drop. Under a *protective* rule, managers instead try to hold harvest to a cap, easing pressure as the shortage signal grows.

The two rules produce strikingly different outcomes. With the mobilising rule, a delay of intermediate length can actually *stabilise* the system. There is a window of delay times (a few years to well over a century in the examples studied) inside which the lag itself damps oscillations; delays that are either too short or too long make the stock swing wildly. With the protective rule the result is simpler and stronger: the system remains stable no matter how long the delay.

When managers instead review the stock only at fixed intervals (for example once a year) and then freeze their decision until the next review, the two rules again behave in opposite ways. Annual reviews keep a protective system stable. Annual reviews of a mobilising system make it unstable; stability returns only when reviews are spaced further apart (roughly every six-and-a-half years). An apparent instability around two years turned out to be an artefact of the numerical method, not a real feature of the governance process.

The same modelling approach applies to any renewable resource that is periodically assessed and then managed by institutional rules, not just fisheries. Historical timelines, such as the northern cod fishery—from yearly assessments, through the 1992 moratorium, to the 2024 reopening—show that these institutional delays unfold over decades.

The central message is straightforward. Biological lags matter, but they are not the whole story. The *form* of the institutional response (whether it ramps effort up or holds it back) and the *timing* of that response together decide whether management stabilises a stock or drives it into collapse. How often managers review the stock is itself a powerful control knob, and it works in opposite directions under the two rules.

### Revised Abstract

Delays are well-known triggers of boom-bust cycles and collapse in renewable resources like fisheries. Traditionally, researchers have focused on biological lags, such as the time it takes for young stock to mature. This paper investigates a different, often overlooked source of instability: **governance delay**—the lag between detecting a resource decline and implementing a policy response. 

Using a generalized model linking resource biomass, institutional memory, and harvest effort, we contrast two fundamental management strategies:
1. **A mobilizing rule**, where falling yields prompt increased extraction effort or subsidies to maintain harvest levels; and
2. **A protective rule**, where catch is systematically reined in toward a sustainable cap as deficits emerge.

Our analysis reveals that the structure of the policy rule decisively shapes resource stability:
* **Protective policies are inherently resilient:** Reining in catch whenever a deficit appears maintains steady stock levels across virtually any administrative delay. 
* **Mobilizing policies inherently destabilize:** Aggressively chasing declining yields creates systemic instability. Counterintuitively, while zero delay and very long delays produce severe cycles and collapse risks, a moderate bureaucratic lag acts as a temporary buffer, stabilizing the system within a narrow window.
* **Review cadence matters:** When managers assess stocks periodically (such as through annual audits) rather than continuously, the rules diverge further. Annual reviews keep protective policies steady, but destabilize mobilizing policies—revealing that how often an agency meets to revise quotas is itself a critical control lever.

Reflecting real-world timelines, such as the decades-long management cycles of the northern cod collapse and recovery, these findings show that ecological biology alone does not dictate resource collapse. The timing, review cadence, and design of institutional feedback are equally decisive in determining whether a resource system thrives or collapses.
