**Title:** Aggregate Indices and Transition Safety: A Quantifier-Order Separation Between Scalarized and Coordinate-Wise Feasibility

Dear Editors,

I am submitting the manuscript **"Aggregate Indices and Transition Safety"** for consideration in the *Journal of Multi-Criteria Decision Analysis*, in the special issue **"Better Decisions for a Better Tomorrow: MCDM Approaches for Sustainability, Risk, and Complex Systems"**.

**What the paper is about.** The paper asks an acceptance question about composite indices: when can a weighted aggregate certify that a *transition* — a management plan carried out over time, under disturbances, with several criteria required to hold along the entire path — actually satisfies its criteria? Two acceptance semantics are compared. Under *weight-adaptive* acceptance, every weighting of the index licenses some plan, possibly a different plan for each weighting. Under *common-plan* acceptance, one plan must serve every weighting. The paper proves that these two semantics disagree on an open region of an explicit worked example: every nonnegative weighting accepts some transition, no single transition is accepted by all, and the floors are breached path-wise by every available plan. The failure is not in the weights — at any fixed trajectory the aggregate is lossless — but in the quantifier order of the acceptance rule.

**What is established.** The separation is characterized exactly: per-weight licensing thresholds (the weight-sensitivity intervals of the acceptance verdict); a closed-form rescue threshold (the budget that converts impossibility into a staged transition); a finite-menu geometric theorem (the acceptance gap of any finite plan menu is the part of the convex hull of the plans' required margins that no single plan dominates); and the blend-collapse theorem — convexifying the plan menu closes the gap exactly, while discrete time-sharing does not. The results connect directly to the journal's literatures: the compensability debate (the static compensatory-potential analyses of MCDA methods classify aggregation at a fixed profile; the separation here acts one level up, in the acceptance protocol), the weights-versus-importance distinction, and weight-space robustness of recommendations.

**Fit with the special issue.** The three themes of the issue name the paper's three constituencies. *Sustainability*: the two acceptance semantics are the weak- and strong-sustainability doctrines; the worked example is realized as a fishery transition anchored to a regulated biomass limit reference point. *Risk*: all statements are robust (worst-case disturbance tubes); the two protocols are the here-and-now versus wait-and-see distinction of adjustable robust optimization, with the assessment weight in the role of the uncertain parameter. *Complex systems*: the analysis is a viability-theoretic study of transition safety — path-wise constraints, regime structure (rescuable versus impossible states), and a common-shock analysis of when the separation survives correlated disturbances.

**Method and verification.** All results are stated precisely and proved in an appendix. Every number in the worked example is re-derived in exact rational arithmetic by deposited verification code (figshare, DOI 10.6084/m9.figshare.33764023), whose checks are enumerated in the manuscript. The paper makes no empirical claim about any particular resource system; a terminology table maps its central objects onto the working vocabularies of multi-criteria decision analysis and operations research, control and viability theory, and environmental modelling and governance. The benchmark is presented as an explicit decision-aiding context — the decision maker (a resource authority), the decision alternatives (the management schedules), the criteria (the binding floors evaluated path-wise), and the preference weights — so that the theory is motivated by the decision context it serves, as the journal's editorial policy requires of theoretical papers.
