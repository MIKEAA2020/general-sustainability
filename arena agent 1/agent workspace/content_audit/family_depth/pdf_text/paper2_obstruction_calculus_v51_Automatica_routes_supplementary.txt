Theorem 1 (finite-horizon soundness and completeness) . An initial belief B0 admits an
observation-based policy that keeps every compatible trajectory in V for N steps if and only if
B0 ∈ W N .
Proof. (⇒) Induction on N . The case N = 0 is B0 ⊆ V , i.e. B0 ∈ W 0. If a policy is safe
for N steps, its first action a is admissible at every compatible state, so a ∈ U B(B0); for every
possible observation y, the successor belief Post(B0, a, y) is safe for N − 1 steps under the same
policy, so by induction Post(B0, a, y) ∈ W N −1; hence B0 ∈ Pre(WN −1) = WN .
(⇐) Backward induction. If Bk ∈ W N −k = Pre( WN −k−1), choose the witness action ak ∈
U B(Bk); after the observation yk the successor belief Bk+1 = Post(Bk, ak, yk) ∈ W N −k−1, and
the recursion continues. By construction every reachable state over N steps lies in V. □

Proposition 3 (epistemic emptiness by admissibility — a minimal construction) . There exists
a system with Viab(V; U, πperf ) = V ⁄ = ∅ such that, for a non-injective observation map O, every

outside ER ViabI(V): the epistemic kernel over the fibre-induced initial beliefs is empty. The
mechanism is admissibility rather than a hidden mode: the state-dependent control set of the
construction leaves a single common action at the merged belief — one that is outward-drifting
at the boundary state — so the emptiness is the common-action obstruction (Theorem 2’s safety
case) at minimal size, produced by the control-set primitive rather than by information; the
hidden-mode instance is Example 1.

Complete proof of the selector principle (main text, Section 8). Let π witness B ∈
ER ViabI(V). (i) Admissibility. The policy π is admissible by the definition of epistemic viability,
so its review selection a = π(B) satisfies a ∈ U (x) for every compatible x ∈ B — an admissible
policy never selects an action outside the state-dependent control set at a reachable state —
whence a ∈ U B(B). (ii) Safety. Every trajectory compatible with B and governed by π
over the review interval [tk, tk + ∆] lies in V; under the sampled-hold semantics the union of
these segments over compatible initial states is exactly Reach[0,∆](B, a), giving the inclusion
Reach[0,∆](B, a) ⊆ V . (iii) Recursive viability. Let B′ = τ (B, a, y) be any post-observation
belief reached at the next review. Every x ∈ B′ lies on a trajectory compatible with the
observation record through tk + ∆ that stayed in V so far and is governed beyond tk + ∆ by
the continuation of π; that continuation is admissible, information-adapted, and keeps every
such trajectory in V — otherwise π would fail to keep every compatible trajectory of B in V
on [0, ∞). Hence π|B′ witnesses B′ ∈ ER ViabI(V), which is (iii). Induction on the review
index now shows that all four response correspondences — U B, RB
V , Atube at the review length,
and the recursive predecessor — are nonempty at every reachable belief, each containing π’s
review selection; by contraposition, a certificate that any one of them is empty at a reachable
belief certifies nonviability. The finite-system converse is the backward induction of Theorem 1
applied to the review-time belief recursion. The continuous-time converse is the backward-
recursion completion recorded in Section 6.5 of the main text; it is cited there and not re-derived.

dnk → d ∈ D(x), continuity of F and of qj gives qj(F (x, u, d)) = lim k qj(F (x, unk , dnk )) ≤ 0,

safe. Now suppose ⋂
x∈B RV (x) = ∅. Each member of the family {RV (x)}x∈B is compact and
convex in the Euclidean space U ⊆ Rm. If every finite subfamily had nonempty intersection,
the finite-intersection property of the compact sets would give nonempty total intersection —
contradiction; so some finite subfamily RV (x1), . . . ,RV (xN ) has empty intersection. By Helly’s
theorem in Rm (Helly, 1923), a finite family of convex sets in Rm with empty intersection
contains a subfamily of at most m + 1 members with empty intersection; the corresponding
x1, . . . , xℓ, ℓ ≤ m + 1, are the required witness. The converse direction is immediate — the
witness sets are members of the family, so their empty intersection implies the full one.

exactly this failure — and the Helly route does not apply; the witness bound then holds for the
convexified (relaxed) safe-control sets, a weaker statement consistent with the reading of the
relaxation in Section 3.

S2. The suﬀiciency landscape (full)
5. The Suﬀiciency Landscape
The suﬀiciency results against which the obstruction calculus is defined are standard; they are
stated here for contrast, and proofs appear in the cited sources.
(a) V eliov’s output-feedback condition. Veliov (1993) considers the same problem as
Section 3 (a tube in the state space under incomplete and inexact measurement) and gives a
suﬀicient condition for the existence of an output-feedback regulation map: a set-valued feedback
of the measurement under which all trajectories starting from the graph of the tube remain in it.
Under perfect measurement his condition reduces to the classical viability condition (Haddad,
1981). The complementarity with this paper is exact: Veliov’s theorem certifies existence; the
obstruction calculus certifies nonexistence; a problem that satisfies neither requires a stronger
observer or a finer observation structure.
(b) The estimation-tube programme. Cardaliaguet, Quincampoix, and Saint-Pierre
(2007) pass from imperfect information in the measurement space to perfect information in an
estimation space of sets of compatible states: the value functions of the two problems coincide,
and the estimation-space problem admits a Dini-derivative characterization. The reduction
is exact for value functions; what it does not supply is a common prescription . The exact
relationship is stated in Section 6.3: carried out under the set-membership semantics, the
estimation-space solution propagates the belief under a single control signal, so its controls
are common controls and joint admissibility is built into the estimation-space problem’s own
admissibility. Pointwise selections taken without that joint-admissibility check need not be
jointly admissible; the common-action obstruction (Theorem 2) is the certificate that no jointly
admissible selection exists at the information state in question.
(c) Observer-and-buffer transfer. When a full-information feedback exists with a strict
inward margin, and an observer supplies exponentially convergent estimates, output feedback
of the estimate preserves safety on a buffered subset: if Kζ is a compact controlled-invariant
subset of the interior of the kernel, the state feedback u = k(x) is Lipschitz with constant Lk,
the observer satisfies ‖ˆx(t) − x(t)‖ ≤ M e−λt‖ˆx(0) − x(0)‖, and the initial estimation error is
small enough that LkM ‖ˆx(0) − x(0)‖ is absorbed by the margin, then Kζ is viable under output
feedback (observer-to-viability transfer with safety buffer; the standard separation argument
of observer-based control (Sontag, 1998)). Similarly, eroded kernels: if K is robustly invariant
under full-state feedback with a strict inward margin on ∂K , and estimation and implementation
errors are bounded by ζ, then an eroded set K−cζ is invariant under output feedback for a
sensitivity constant c > 0 (erosion absorbs the error; see the buffer constructions in the literature
cited in Section 1.3). These results bound the suﬀiciency side: under margins and convergence,
output feedback can work; the obstruction calculus of Sections 3–4 states the conditions under
which it cannot.
(d) The linear substitution alternative. For the finite linear model in which a resource-
typed system must meet a demand vector through specified substitution pathways, exactly one
of the following holds (Farkas, 1902; Gale, 1960): (i) there exists a non-negative pathway vector
a ≥ 0 satisfying the linear substitution constraints; or (ii) there exist multipliers α, β, γ ≥ 0
such that
α⊤R + β⊤E − γ⊤Q ≥ 0 componentwise, γ ⊤sreq > α ⊤x + β⊤e.
The second statement is a certificate that the specified substitution pathways cannot meet de-
mand within the typed resource and capacity bounds; writing all constraints as Aa ≤ ρ, the
pair is the Farkas lemma alternative. The multipliers are a separation certificate, not univer-
sal exchange rates: nonlinear, nonconvex, path-dependent, spatial, or irreversible technologies
require their own feasibility analysis, and an elasticity fitted near one operating point cannot
establish global substitutability. This is the feasibility-side complement of the observation-side
12
obstructions of Sections 3–4: where the present paper’s certificates bound what information
can achieve, the substitution alternative bounds what material pathways can achieve, and the
same infeasibility logic — exhibit the separating multiplier rather than assert impossibility —
applies to both.
A worked comparison: barrier, estimation-tube, and obstruction certificates on
one system. The two-floor system of Section 3.4 makes the division of labour concrete. Two
states x1 and x2 share one observation; the single control u ∈ [0, 1] must satisfy u ≤ 0.4 at x1

Barrier certificates. A barrier certificate certifies safety from a scalar function whose zero
level set separates the unsafe region from all trajectories under some state feedback. Here there
is no state to feed back — the observation merges x1 and x2 — so an admissible barrier would
have to be constant on the fibre {x1, x2}, and no such function can separate the two floors’
incompatible requirements. The programme therefore reports “no barrier found,” a verdict
that does not distinguish “unsafe” from “unsafe for the class of barrier functions tried. ”
Estimation-tube reduction. Lifting to the estimation space replaces the merged observation
by perfect information about the belief {x1, x2}. The belief-state viability problem then has
empty value — no common control keeps both floors safe — so the estimation-space kernel is
empty. The reduction returns the verdict exactly, but as a black box: it does not isolate which
feature of the observation is responsible.
Obstruction certificate. The common-action obstruction (Theorem 2) fires with the explicit
Farkas certificate λ = (1/2, 1/2) for the stacked system u ≤ 0.4, −u ≤ −0.6, with λ⊤A = 0 and
λ⊤b = −0.1 < 0 — the infeasibility margin 0.1 of Section 3.4. The sparse witness of Section 3.4

observation separating x1 from x2 restores one-step viability. (The general polyhedral statement
and its checkability discussion are the complete-discussion block of S1.) This is the division of
labour the paper relies on — the barrier programme and the estimation-tube reduction certify
that the kernel is empty; the obstruction calculus certifies why, and names the observation

Complete proof of the exact two-phase decomposition (main text, Section 7). Write
a policy for the two-phase model as a blind part (u0, . . . , uK−1) ∈ ΠB followed by an adapted
part, a map from the revealed state to a continuation. (i) ⇒ branchwise necessity: branches
evolve independently under the blind control, and viability requires V-membership at every

is not window-surviving. (ii): if some branch lands outside R Viab(V) at the reveal under every
window-surviving blind control, then upon that branch’s realization no adapted continuation is
viable, and the policy fails; conversely, if some window-surviving blind control lands every branch
in R Viab(V), the policy consisting of that blind part and, after the reveal, the kernel-witnessed
continuations from the revealed state is viable: after the reveal the information is complete, and
membership in the full-information kernel is exactly the existence of a viable continuation. This
proves the equivalence. For the consequence, over a class on which the window certificates are
complete, silence of the pair is equivalent to clause (i): each certificate fires only on witnessed
window failure (common-action at one step; timing by exit before the horizon), so if (i) holds
neither fires, and if (i) fails the complete window certificate fires. Given silence, viability holds
exactly when clause (ii) holds for some window-surviving blind control, and nonviability, when
it occurs, is exactly the failure of clause (ii) for every window-surviving blind control: the
post-observation recourse mode.

A.3 Example (insuﬀicient post-observation recourse). A three-state system shows
the mode of Section 6.5(ii) concretely. Take V = {x1, x2, x4} with x3 /∈ V a violation, actions
A = {a, b}, and no disturbance. Transitions: x1 maps to x1 under a and to x4 under b; x2
maps to x4 under a and to x2 under b; x4 maps to x3 under both actions, so x4 lies in V but
outside the kernel. The observation merges x1 and x2 at the first step and is exact thereafter.
The root belief B0 = {x1, x2} is one-step viable: action a keeps both states in V (reaching
x1 and x4), as does b (reaching x4 and x2), so the common-action certificate is silent and no
exit occurs in the first step. After the first observation, however, the trajectory is at x4 for
one of the two possible initial states (under a it is x2, under b it is x1), and x4 has no safe
action, so that branch exits at the second step regardless of what the policy learns. Hence
B0 /∈ W 2 although B0 ∈ W 1: the failure is a post-observation recourse failure, witnessed only
by the recursion (Theorem 1) — the exit certificate is silent at the root because both x1 and
x2 are individually viable under full information, and the timing certificate is silent because
the observation arrives without delay. The main text supplies the continuous-time counterpart
(Section 3.7 there): an oracle-relaxed recourse obstruction whose three-branch braking instance
fires exactly in this regime — blind-window safety with insuﬀicient post-observation braking
authority — with exact delay threshold τ = 7/50.

Remark 3 (the certainty-equivalence trap) . Consider ˙S = u − g(S) with u ∈ [0, ¯u], g strictly
increasing on [Smin, S∗ + b], g(0) = 0 , and V = [ Smin, S∗]. Assume the range condition g(S +
b) ≤ ¯u for every S ∈ [Smin, S∗], so that the controller below is admissible. Under perfect
information the zeroing feedback u(t) = g(S(t)) gives ˙S = 0 , so every S0 ∈ V is viable and
Viab(V; U, πperf ) = V ⁄ = ∅. Now take the injective, biased observation ˆS = S + b with b > 0,
and the canonical certainty-equivalence controller: the perfect-information zeroing law applied
directly to the uncorrected observation, u = g( ˆS) = g(S + b). Then
˙S = g(S + b) − g(S) > 0 ∀S,
and since g is strictly increasing on the compact interval [Smin, S∗ + b], ˙S is bounded below by
a positive constant there; hence S strictly increases and exits above S∗ in finite time from
every S0 ∈ V : the kernel of this single controller is empty. The bias-corrected controller
u = g( ˆS − b) = g(S) uses the observation map’s structure, restores ˙S = 0 , and keeps the
kernel nonempty.
The mechanism is a failure of one policy to use the observation map’s structure , not a loss
of information: for a known invertible observation the output-feedback class is as powerful as
the state-feedback class. The phenomenon is the viability-theoretic analogue of Witsenhausen’s
counterexample in stochastic control (Witsenhausen, 1968): the information pattern — not the
dynamics and not the constraint — defeats the uncorrected controller. The remark is policy-
specific; it does not assert that the entire certainty-equivalence class empties the kernel.
