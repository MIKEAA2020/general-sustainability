# Writing for the Sustainability Literature
## A venue-by-venue style, format & jargon guide

**Prepared for:** a multi-paper sustainability research project (readership: ecologists, economists, sustainability scientists, population scientists, policy makers).
**Target venues:** *Ecological Economics* (EE), *Ecological Modelling* (EM), *Sustainability Science* (SS), and — for the math-heavy "atlas" paper — *Set-Valued and Variational Analysis* (SVAA).

**What this guide is:** a synthesis of (a) the current official author guidelines of all four journals (read August 2026), and (b) exemplar papers in each venue, including the viability-theory-for-sustainability cluster that runs from Aubin/Saint-Pierre through Martinet, Doyen, Gajardo, Barrios to Hermosilla's 2024/2026 SVAA papers — the direct lineage of your atlas paper. It tells you **how each venue expects you to write**: structure, formatting specs, reference systems, accepted jargon, voice — and, equally, the *register that is excluded* (Section 6: what expert readers demand, and the one principled exception — methods papers, Section 6.6) and the *novelty discipline* (Section 7: what must earn a place in the main text, and what is relocated — never condensed — to appendix, SI, or a sibling paper).

## 2. Ecological Economics (Elsevier)

**Aims & scope** (verbatim core): *"extending and integrating the understanding of the interfaces and interplay between 'nature's household' (ecosystems) and 'humanity's household' (the economy)"* — the transdisciplinary journal of the ISEE. Scope areas include valuation of natural resources, integrated ecologic–economic modelling, thermodynamics and economics, renewable resource management, alternative principles for valuing natural wealth, and "critical assessments of the basic assumptions underlying current economic and ecological paradigms."

**Article categories that matter for your project:**
- **Analysis** (≤8000 words): research on important questions — originality, quality, accuracy of analysis, importance of question.
- **Methodological and Ideological Options** (≤8000 words): new methodologies or the implications of ideological assumptions — this is the slot for a *new sustainability measure / new formalism with an explicit normative stance*.
- **Survey** (≤8000 words): synthesis of an important subject area.
- **Commentary** (≤5000 words): essays on critical issues.

### Formatting essentials (verified from the guide for authors)
- **Abstract:** ≤250 words, unstructured, no citations, no undefined abbreviations. Must stand alone.
- **Keywords:** 1–7; avoid compounds joined by "and"/"of" (e.g. prefer "degrowth" over "limits of growth"); abbreviations only if firmly established.
- **Highlights:** 3–5 bullets, **≤85 characters each including spaces** (this trips up everyone — write them like headlines).
- **Graphical abstract:** required; one self-contained visual summarizing the main result.
- **CRediT author-contribution statements** and a **data availability statement** are expected at submission.
- **References:** author–date (e.g. *Kallis (2011)*; *Daly and Farley (2004)*; *Costanza et al. (1997)*), alphabetical list, journal titles abbreviated per ISSN LTWA.
- Generative-AI use must be declared in a section before the references; basic grammar/spell-check tools need not be declared.

### Voice and conventions of accepted EE papers
- **Explicitly normative.** Unlike JEP/QJE-style economics, EE papers are permitted — even expected — to argue from values: "sustainability principles," "intergenerational justice," "sufficiency." State your normative premise early and let readers audit it.
- **Paradigm-conscious framing.** Open with the limitation of the incumbent approach (e.g., "the problem with the HDI is that…"), then present your alternative. Hickel (2020) opens exactly this way and the SDI paper is a model for introducing a new composite index in EE.
- **First person is fine** ("we argue," occasionally "I propose" in single-author essays), but keep it sober.
- **Describe math in words.** The SDI paper presents its index as "the quotient of two figures: a development index … and an ecological impact index" — equations exist, but every symbol is translated into prose for the mixed readership.
- **Tables of country rankings / sector comparisons** are idiomatic evidence in this venue.
- **Discussion should close the loop to policy** ("what this implies for measurement of progress / national accounting").

### Jargon lexicon — EE accepted terms
Core: **natural capital, ecosystem services, strong vs weak sustainability** (substitutability between produced and natural capital), **critical natural capital**, **planetary boundaries**, **safe operating space**, **doughnut economics / safe and just space**, **ecological footprint, biocapacity, overshoot, material footprint**, **social metabolism, material flow accounting**, **throughput** (in the Daly thermodynamic sense), **entropy law**, **steady-state economy**, **degrowth / post-growth**, **sufficiency**, **decoupling** (relative vs absolute), **environmental Kuznets curve** (usually to critique), **incommensurability of values, valuation pluralism, deliberative monetary valuation**, **genuine savings / adjusted net savings, inclusive wealth, comprehensive wealth**, **intergenerational equity, discounting (prescriptive vs descriptive)**, **just distribution**, **ecological macroeconomics**, **limits to substitution**, **irreversibility**, **precautionary principle**, **coevolutionary economics**, **human development index / sustainable development index**, **wellbeing beyond GDP**, **regime shifts, tipping points, resilience**.

Avoid (or use knowingly): **utilitarian welfare formalism as an unexamined default**, "total economic value" without acknowledging non-monetizable values, journalese like "holistic" or "paradigm shift" without operational meaning. EE reviewers reward *precision about the value basis*.

### Exemplars to read first
1. **Hickel, J. (2020).** The sustainable development index: measuring the ecological efficiency of human development in the anthropocene. *Ecol. Econ.* 167:106331. — model of a new-index paper in EE (open access).
2. **Daly, H. (1990).** Toward some operational principles of sustainable development. *Ecol. Econ.* 2:1–6. — canonical, short, defines "sustainable throughput."
3. **Kallis, G. (2011).** In defence of degrowth. *Ecol. Econ.* 70:873–880. — exemplary "ideological options" essay.
4. **Martínez-Alier, J., Munda, G., O'Neill, J. (1998).** Weak comparability of values as a foundation for ecological economics. *Ecol. Econ.* 26:277–286. — how EE writes about value pluralism.
5. **Costanza, R., et al. (1997).** The value of the world's ecosystem services and natural capital. *Nature* 387:253–260. — the most-cited reference in EE discussions of valuation; know its critiques.
6. **Rockström, J., et al. (2009).** A safe operating space for humanity. *Nature* 461:472–475; and **Steffen, W., et al. (2015).** Planetary boundaries: guiding human development on a changing planet. *Science* 347:1259855. — boundary language EE authors cite constantly.
7. **Dietz, S., Neumayer, E. (2007).** Weak and strong sustainability in the SEEA: concepts and measurement. *Ecol. Econ.* 61:617–626. — strong/weak sustainability measurement vocabulary.

## 3. Ecological Modelling (Elsevier)

**Aims & scope:** *"new mathematical models and systems analysis for describing ecological processes, and novel applications of models for environmental management"* — with an explicit welcome for **"process-based models embedded in theory with explicit causative agents"** and applications of existing models. Spans basic ecology to socio-ecological systems; official journal ecosystem of ISEM.

**Article types:** regular papers, reviews, short communications (≤6 printed pages), Viewpoints (≤2000 words), Jorgensen Research & Reviews (invited).

### Formatting essentials
- Same Elsevier skeleton as EE: ≤250-word abstract; 1–7 keywords; Highlights (3–5 × ≤85 chars); graphical abstract encouraged; CRediT; data statement.
- **LaTeX explicitly encouraged** (Elsevier template); Word also fine.
- **Nomenclature section / units:** the guide has a dedicated "Units, classifications codes and nomenclature" section — EM expects consistent SI units and a symbol list for models with many parameters.
- ODD/TRACE are not literally mandatory in the guide, but the editorial culture (Grimm lineage) treats them as *de facto* requirements for simulation-based models. Plan for them.

### Voice and conventions
- **Model description as a labelled section** ("2. Model description" or "2. Model formulation"): state variables, processes, equations, parameters (with a parameter table: symbol, meaning, value, unit, source). EM reviewers check whether *every* parameter is sourced or estimated.
- **"Process-based" is a shibboleth:** tie each equation to a biological/ecological mechanism, not just curve-fitting. State explicitly whether your model is process-based or phenomenological.
- **Model evaluation section:** calibration/validation strategy, sensitivity analysis, and honest discussion of **structural uncertainty** — not just parameter uncertainty.
- **Scenario design:** label scenarios descriptively (business-as-usual vs policy scenarios), justify each.
- **Numerics stated:** integration scheme/time-step for ODEs; for set-based computations (viability kernels), the Saint-Pierre algorithm family and grid resolution.
- **Figures idiom:** phase portraits, time-series of state variables, kernel/constraint-set visualizations, sensitivity tornado plots. Captions self-contained ("shows … under scenario …").
- **Conclusion must state model limits** ("our model assumes…; relaxing X is left for future work").

### Jargon lexicon — EM accepted terms
**state variable, parameter, forcing (function), flow/stock, feedback (positive/negative), process-based vs phenomenological model, stylized model, individual-based model (IBM), agent-based model (ABM), coupled human–environment system, socio-ecological system (SES), differential/difference equations, system dynamics, bifurcation, hysteresis, regime shift, alternative stable states, attractor, basin of attraction, resilience (engineering vs ecological), pattern-oriented modelling, ODD (Overview, Design concepts, Details) protocol, ODD+D, TRACE (TRAnsparent and Comprehensive model Evaludation), calibration, validation, verification, sensitivity analysis (local/global; Morris screening, Sobol' indices, LHS), identifiability, structural uncertainty, equilibrium analysis (asymptotic stability), simulation experiment, scenario analysis, model intercomparison, reproducibility, FAIR data, NetLogo/R/GAMS/MATLAB tool disclosure.**

Avoid: presenting a model with no evaluation section; calling regression fits "models of mechanism"; black-box language ("the model predicts…" without stating assumptions).

### Exemplars to read first
1. **Grimm, V., et al. (2006).** A standard protocol for describing individual-based and agent-based models. *Ecol. Model.* 198:115–126. — the ODD protocol itself; read how EM authors structure model documentation.
2. **Grimm, V., et al. (2010).** The ODD protocol: a review and first update. *Ecol. Model.* 221:2760–2768.
3. **Grimm, V., et al. (2014).** Towards better modelling and decision support: documenting model development, testing, and analysis using TRACE. *Ecol. Model.* 280:129–139.
4. **Müller, B., et al. (2013).** Describing human decisions in agent-based models — ODD+D. *Ecol. Model.* 259:37–47.
5. Any recent EM paper in your exact subfield (search "viability" in EM — e.g. papers by **Cury, Mullon, Doyen, Béné** on fisheries viability — to see how kernel-based methods are written for an ecological audience).

## 4. Sustainability Science (Springer)

**Identity:** official journal of IR3S (Univ. of Tokyo) and IGES. Interdisciplinary, solutions-oriented, explicitly engaged with science–society interfaces. Word limits exclude references.

### Article types that matter for your project
- **Original article** ≤8000 words — *"theoretical or methodological advances in research that integrate inquiry in various disciplines."* ← the slot for an integrative framing/methodology paper.
- **Technical report** ≤5000 words — *"results of research into specific areas of concern, including the results of **mathematical modeling**, survey research, and field studies."* ← a natural home for a modelling piece that must speak to non-modellers.
- **Review** ≤8000 words; **Case report** ≤7000; **Notes and comments** ≤2500.

### Formatting essentials (verified from submission guidelines)
- **Abstract:** ~250 words (original/review/overview); ~100 words for technical reports, case reports, notes.
- **Keywords:** up to 6.
- **Structure:** "Text of the paper, divided into the following sections, if appropriate: Introduction; Materials and methods; Results; Discussion; Conclusions; Acknowledgments; References; tables; figure legends." (IMRaD — no exotic headings unless the paper type demands them.)
- **References:** author–year in text *(Hammer 1994; Hammer and Sjöqvist 1995; Hammer et al. 1993)*; alphabetical list, Springer style: *May MO, Tilman K, Wiliams R (2005) Restoration of sound ecosystems in the traditional rural landscapes. Landscape Ecol Eng 1:3–15* — journal names abbreviated.
- **Manuscript format:** 1.5 line spacing, 2.5-cm margins, auto page numbers; figures as separate files; figure legends at end of text; parts labelled (a, b, c).
- **ESM** numbered S1, S2…; **Research Data Policy Type 1** (deposit where possible; data availability statement).
- "Manuscripts should be written so that they are intelligible to the professional reader who is not a specialist in the particular field" — this is the single most important style rule of this journal.

### Voice and conventions
- **Problem-first framing:** open on the sustainability challenge (not the method); method serves the problem.
- **Typologies and design principles:** SS papers love enumerated principles/frameworks (Lang et al. 2012's design principles A/B/C for transdisciplinary research; Abson et al. 2017's leverage points). Present yours as a numbered, testable scheme.
- **Normativity is explicit and welcomed**, but must be flagged as such ("a value-laden choice…").
- **Transdisciplinary literacy:** even if your paper is quantitative, acknowledge the science–policy–society interface, stakeholders, and who uses the results.
- **Figures:** conceptual framework diagrams (boxes-and-arrows) are idiomatic; so are pathway/transition graphics.

### Jargon lexicon — SS accepted terms
**sustainability science (as a field), social–ecological system (SES), coupled human and natural systems (CHANS), telecoupling, Anthropocene, Great Acceleration, planetary boundaries, safe and just operating space, doughnut economics, Earth system governance, planetary stewardship, resilience (and "resilience thinking"), adaptive capacity, transformability, transformation / transformative change, sustainability transitions, transition management, socio-technical regimes, leverage points, pathways (to sustainability), scenario, wicked problems, post-normal science, Mode-2 knowledge production, transdisciplinarity (vs multi-/interdisciplinarity), knowledge co-production, actionable knowledge, solutions-oriented research, SDGs, water–energy–food nexus, provisioning of ecosystem services / nature's contributions to people, limits to growth, degrowth, sufficiency, intergenerational equity, adaptive (co-)management, polycentric governance, institutional fit, normativity.**

Avoid: pure disciplinary jargon unexplained; "we apply method X" without stating the sustainability problem X addresses; unexamined use of "resilience" without defining the system property and to-what-against-what.

### Exemplars to read first
1. **Kates, R., et al. (2001).** Sustainability science. *Science* 292:641–642. — the founding statement; defines the field's vocabulary.
2. **Lang, D.J., et al. (2012).** Transdisciplinary research in sustainability science: practice, principles, and challenges. *Sustain. Sci.* 7:25–43. — model of a principles paper; note its structure (conceptual model → synthesized principles → challenges/coping strategies → future research).
3. **Clark, W.C., Harley, A.G. (2020).** Sustainability science: toward a synthesis. *Annu. Rev. Environ. Resour.* 45:331–386. — current synthesis vocabulary.
4. **Abson, D.J., et al. (2017).** Leverage points for sustainability transformation. *Ambio* 46:30–39. — how to write about systems-intervention points.
5. **Norström, A.V., et al. (2020).** Principles for knowledge co-production in sustainability research. *Nat. Sustain.* 3:182–190.
6. A recent SS **technical report** using mathematical modelling (search the journal for "viability," "thresholds," "population" — several exist, e.g. by Kummu, Dearing, or IHOPE affiliates) to calibrate how much math SS tolerates.

### Formatting essentials (verified from submission guidelines)
- **Length:** ≤40 pages in the Springer template; longer must be *motivated in the cover letter*.
- **Abstract:** 150–250 words, no undefined abbreviations, no references.
- **Keywords:** 4–6, plus **MSC2020 classification codes** (check ams.org/msc). For your paper, the likely set: **34A60** (differential inclusions), **49J53** (set-valued and variational analysis), **49K40** (sensitivity/stability), **49K15** or **49J15** (optimal control/differential inclusions), **93C15** (control systems), **93B03** (attainable sets), **91B76** (environmental economics), **92D25** (population dynamics) — pick 3–6 that actually apply.
- **Editor-in-Chief's standing notes (follow literally):**
  1. *"The proofs should not be put in the appendix but within the natural flow of the arguments in the main text."*
  2. *"Please make sure that your submitted work has line numbers for easier reviewing."*
  3. You may suggest an Associate Editor and reviewers, at **arm's-length distance** (no collaborators, supervisors, or co-authors within 5 years); non-preferred reviewers may also be named.
- **Headings:** decimal system, max 3 levels (e.g., "3.1.2").
- **References:** numbered in text: *"This effect has been widely studied [1–3, 7]."* List numbered consecutively; Springer style: *Hamburger, C.: Quasimonotonicity, regularity and duality for nonlinear systems of partial differential equations. Ann. Mat. Pura Appl. 169, 321–354 (1995)*; journal names per ISSN LTWA; DOIs as links when available.
- **Statements and Declarations** section is mandatory (Competing Interests, Funding, Data Availability, etc.).
- **LaTeX:** use the Springer Nature LaTeX template; no subfolders; the system compiles your source to PDF.
- **Footnotes not endnotes;** footnotes to tables use superscript lower-case letters.
- **Acknowledgments** (incl. funding in full) go in a separate section on the title page.

### Voice and conventions — how SVAA papers actually read
The Hermosilla (2024) abstract is a perfect template. Note its moves:

> "In the context of constrained control-systems, the Set of Sustainable Thresholds plays in a sense the role of a dual object to the so-called Viability Kernel, because it describes all the thresholds that must be satisfied by the state of the system along a time interval, for a prescribed initial condition. This work aims at analyzing the sensitivity of the Set of Sustainable Thresholds, when it is seen as a set-valued map that depends on the initial position. In this regard, we investigate semicontinuity and Lipschitz continuity properties of this mapping, and we also study several contexts when the Set of Sustainable Thresholds is convex-valued."

**Deconstruct the moves:** (1) situate the object relative to the canonical concept (Viability Kernel); (2) restate the object's meaning in one plain clause ("describes all the thresholds that must be satisfied…"); (3) "This work aims at analyzing…" — the contribution sentence, naming the mathematical properties (semicontinuity, Lipschitz continuity, convex-valuedness); (4) "we investigate… we also study…" — first-person plural, economical.

**Standard architecture of an SVAA paper:**
1. **Introduction** — motivation (1–2 paragraphs, possibly sustainability-flavored), the object of study, prior art (Aubin; Saint-Pierre; the sustainable-thresholds cluster), "the contribution of this paper" bulleted or enumerated (Theorem 1: existence; Theorem 2: regularity; etc.), *notation and standing hypotheses* at the end ("Throughout the paper, we assume that …").
2. **Preliminaries** — definitions with precise citations: set-valued maps, Painlevé–Kuratowski limits, contingent cones, normal cones, tangential condition, viability/invariance kernels, capture basins, the Saint-Pierre approximation scheme.
3. **Main results** — Definition–Lemma–Theorem–Proof in sequence; proofs in main text per the EiC note; each theorem followed by a **Remark** bridging to the application ("In ecological terms, Theorem 3 says that …").
4. **Application / worked example** — e.g., a resource-population model (logistic growth, age structure, or a Lotka–Volterra/consumer–resource system) with the constraint set K as the "safe operating space"; computed viability kernels; numerical scheme and grid resolution reported.
5. **Concluding remarks** — no new results; open problems and links to the sustainability literature (Martinet, Doyen, Gajardo).

**Style rules:**
- **Every symbol defined at first use**; heavy notation concentrated in one place. Use "the set of sustainable thresholds" spelled out at first occurrence, then "SST" or a symbol.
- **Theorems stated in full** before proofs; proofs end with "□" (qed symbol) and begin with "Proof.".
- **Proofs in full.** Arguments are never condensed, sketched, or left to "the rest is straightforward". The sole permitted omission is replacement by citation when the proof repeats standard literature or textbooks ("the proof is standard; see Aubin (1991), Theorem 3.2.4"). Combined with the EiC's rule that proofs sit in the main text, this is the discipline that keeps SVAA papers honest within 40 pages: every argument is either given in full or pointed to precisely.
- "**By the viability theorem…**", "**under the tangential condition…**", "**the map x ↦ SST(x) is Lipschitz-like (has the Aubin property)…**" — these are the shibboleths.
- Precision about quantifiers: "for every initial condition", "there exists an admissible control", "for all t ≥ 0".
- The application section is where sustainability jargon is allowed (safe operating space, thresholds, intergenerational equity, maximin); the theorem sections stay purely mathematical.
- Cite the canonical texts (Aubin 1991; Aubin & Cellina 1984; Aubin, Bayen & Saint-Pierre 2011; Mordukhovich for variational analysis; Rockafellar & Wets) — SVAA reviewers expect the lineage.

### Jargon lexicon — SVAA accepted terms
**set-valued map / multifunction, graph, domain, upper/lower semicontinuity, Painlevé–Kuratowski convergence, contingent (Bouligand) cone, adjacent cone, Clarke tangent/normal cone, limiting (Mordukhovich) normal cone, Aubin property (Lipschitz-like / pseudo-Lipschitz), calmness, metric regularity, graphical derivative, coderivative, differential inclusion ẋ ∈ F(x), Marchaud map, set-valued solution, tangential condition, viability kernel Viab_F(K), invariance kernel Inv(K), capture basin Capt_F(K,C), absorbing set, regulation map R_K, heavy viable solutions, minimal time function, value function, Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman framework, mutational/morphological equations, Pompeiu–Hausdorff distance, Saint-Pierre algorithm, set-valued numerical analysis, robust/tychastic viability, state constraints, sustainable thresholds, maximin, intergenerational equity, Pareto front, sensitivity analysis, Lipschitz stability, convex-valuedness, positively invariant set.**

Avoid: "prove" claims without proof in main text; appendix-only proofs (explicitly discouraged); undefined notation; vague "there exist thresholds" without regularity statements; sustainability rhetoric without a precise constraint-set translation.

### Why this venue fits the atlas paper — and what the gatekeepers screen for
The scope explicitly lists **viability theory** and **applications to economics**; the editorial board has handled the sustainable-thresholds papers above, so your paper is on-topic by precedent. Desk-screening focuses on: (1) a clean mathematical object (not a simulation report); (2) non-trivial regularity results (semicontinuity, Lipschitz/Aubin properties, convexity, sensitivity); (3) the 40-page limit; (4) proofs in main text; (5) line numbers. The cover letter should name the lineage above (especially the two SVAA threshold papers) and justify placement.

## 6. The register boundary: what never appears in these journals

Articles in EE, EM, SS, and SVAA are written in **scientific register**, not **project register**. This boundary is where internal drafts most often diverge from publishable prose — and where desk-rejection or reviewer irritation originates. Six classes of material are systematically absent from the published literature, and must be stripped or translated before submission.

**The reader contract.** These journals are read by working domain experts — not by project colleagues and not by a general audience. An expert reader scans for five things, in roughly this order: (1) **claims** — what is asserted, and how strongly; (2) **methods** — how the claims were produced and whether they are defensible; (3) **evidence** — data, simulations, or proofs that bear the claims out; (4) **policies** — what should be done differently (EE/EM/SS); (5) **implications** — what follows for theory and future work. Every paragraph should be traceable to one of these five functions. Paragraphs that serve none of them — activity narration, effort reports, process commentary, team memory — are dead weight to the expert reader and should be cut regardless of how much work they represent. The excluded registers below are excluded precisely because they serve the project's needs (memory, management, team alignment), not the reader's.

Two qualifications. First, the five functions are weighted differently by venue: in **SVAA** the evidence *is* the proof, and policy content is carried by the Application section and the Remarks; in **EM** the method is often the claim itself; in **SS** the implication for transformation is the point of the paper; in **EE** policy relevance frequently motivates the entire analysis. Second, the one principled exception to the anti-report rule — **when the method itself is the contribution** — is treated in Section 6.6, and it matters directly for this project.

### 6.1 Change-log and diary narration
The manuscript is not a chronology of the research. "First we did X, then we did Y, then we discovered Z" belongs to a lab notebook.

| Project-diary phrasing | Journal phrasing |
|---|---|
| "We first tried a logistic benchmark, but it behaved poorly, so we switched to an age-structured formulation." | "We adopt an age-structured formulation. A logistic benchmark (Supplementary Material S1) reproduces the qualitative dynamics but cannot resolve cohort effects, which are central to the constraints considered here." |
| "After running the model we realized the constraint was too loose." | "The constraint set is tightened to the safe operating space defined by…" |
| "Initially we used h = 0.1, then 0.05, then 0.01 until the kernel stabilized." | "A grid of step h = 0.01 was used; convergence of the Saint-Pierre approximation was assessed by successive grid refinement (Section 4.2)." |

Rule: failed attempts are either (a) silently dropped, or (b) converted into *model-selection rationale*, *robustness checks*, or *SI content*. Nothing is narrated in the order it happened; everything is ordered by its logical role.

**Manuscript history never surfaces.** The published article contains no trace of its own drafting: no "in an earlier version", no "we previously assumed", no narration of corrected mistakes from older manuscript versions. Where a rejected alternative has genuine pedagogical or methodological value, it appears as an anonymous comparison — "an alternative formulation aggregates the two constraints and therefore fails to capture their interaction" — never as autobiography ("we had initially used…"). Genuine caveats about the approach belong in a limitations/discussion passage, stated as present-tense properties of the method, not as a confession of past error.

### 6.2 Meta-commentary on the writing process
References to the act of writing, reading, or planning are absent.

| Meta-commentary | Journal phrasing |
|---|---|
| "As discussed in Section 2, we then…" | "With the specification of Section 2, we simulate…" |
| "As we will see later, the answer is surprising." | Either say it ("The result is counterintuitive: …") or say nothing. |
| "Let's now take a look at the results." | "Figure 3 reports the computed viability kernel." |
| "This will be discussed in a future paper." | "This extension is beyond the scope of the present work." |

The only sanctioned self-reference is *structural signposting* in the introduction ("Section 3 presents the model; Section 4 reports results"), which is standard in all four venues. It is roadmapping, not narration.

### 6.3 Editorializing, self-praise, and phantom opponents

**No self-praise.** Evaluative adjectives attached to the authors' own work are absent from the published literature: *novel, innovative, pioneering, groundbreaking, cutting-edge, state-of-the-art, first-ever, seminal* — never applied to one's own results (and applied to others' work only with justification). The journal-appropriate form is the factual contribution claim: "we establish properties of the sustainable-threshold map not previously analyzed" is a testable claim; "our groundbreaking framework" is advertising. These venues let the result, the proof, and the data carry the weight.

**No editorial commentary.** Voice-over nudges to the reader are omitted: *it is important to note, it is worth emphasizing, notably, interestingly, as one might expect, readers should bear in mind, surprisingly* (unless the surprise is quantified as a finding). Emphasis is achieved structurally — first sentence of a section, a theorem statement, a Remark — never by authorial interjection.

**No phantom or strawman positions.** Every position the paper criticizes or contrasts with is anchored to a named, cited, accurately characterized source. "Critics often claim…", "the prevailing view assumes…", "it is widely believed that…" without citation never appear. Where paradigm-level critique is the paper's business (idiomatic in EE and SS), the strongest available formulation of the opposing position is engaged, not a caricature. This is both an honesty rule and a practical one: referees frequently hold the positions under critique, and they judge fairness before they judge the argument.

**No chat-history residue.** Phrasings, framings, or terms that originated in project discussion do not migrate into the manuscript by default. The manuscript is written in the register of its venue (Sections 2–5 lexicons); internal vocabulary enters only if it passes that venue's lexicon.

### 6.4 Informal language and chat artifacts
Conversational register is invisible in these journals.

| Informal | Journal |
|---|---|
| "Basically, the kernel shrinks when constraints tighten." | "The viability kernel is monotone with respect to inclusion of the constraint set: if K₁ ⊆ K₂, then Viab(K₁) ⊆ Viab(K₂) (Aubin, 1991)." |
| "This makes sense because…" | "This follows from…" / "This is consistent with…" |
| "A lot / a bunch / tons of parameters" | "The model has 23 parameters, of which…" |
| "Interestingly / surprisingly enough" | State the fact; let the reader find it interesting. |
| "In a nutshell" | "In brief" (still rare in print). |

Search-and-destroy list for informal artifacts: *basically, actually, of course, obviously, clearly (unless provable), a lot, get, big, huge, stuff, things, nice, pretty (as intensifier), kind of, sort of, really, quite (as filler), in a nutshell, dive into, dig into, wrap up, take a look, on the flip side, the bottom line, here's the thing, as it turns out, let's, we'll, don't, isn't* — contractions never appear; exclamation marks never appear; rhetorical questions almost never appear (and never in EE/EM/SS).

### 6.5 Project reports, implementation summaries, and project-management language
These journals do not publish "what we did in the project" — they publish *claims with evidence*. A "Model description" section (EM), "Materials and methods" (SS), or "Application" section (SVAA) is not an implementation diary; it is a *protocol*: stated once, complete, replicable, in past tense.

| PM / implementation language | Journal phrasing |
|---|---|
| "Milestone 2: sensitivity analysis for all scenarios; deliverables updated in the appendix." | "Sensitivity of the results to parameter uncertainty is assessed with Sobol' indices (Section 3.4); full scenario outputs are provided in Supplementary Material S2." |
| "Next steps: we plan to extend the model to…" | "Extensions to … are left for future work." |
| "The team decided to focus on fisheries." | "We consider a fishery example." |
| "Per our project plan, Phase 3 covers…" | Delete entirely. |
| "Status: the atlas is 80% complete." | Delete entirely. |

Search-and-destroy list for PM register: *milestone, deliverable, workstream, work package, phase (in the PM sense), roadmap, action item, next steps, status, task, ticket, sprint, backlog, stakeholder meeting, alignment, kickoff, onboarding, owners, assignees, due date, plan of record*. Caveat: **"stakeholders" itself is legitimate vocabulary in Sustainability Science** — but in the transdisciplinary sense ("involving stakeholders in co-design"), never in the PM sense ("stakeholder alignment meeting").

### 6.6 When the method itself is the contribution
The ban on implementation summaries has one principled exception: **when the method is the contribution, the method is the object of study and must be written as one.** This changes the register, not the substance — and it is the exception that governs this project's atlas paper.

A methods paper presents the method like a specimen under examination, with four elements the reviewers will demand:

1. **Formal definition of what the method is** — inputs, outputs, invariants, hypotheses under which it operates.
2. **Properties** — convergence, order of approximation, computational complexity, regularity of the output (e.g., semicontinuity or Lipschitz-like behavior of the map being computed).
3. **Validation against a reference** — closed-form case, analytic benchmark, or cross-check with an independent method.
4. **Comparison with alternatives** — when it outperforms or complements existing methods, stated precisely, with the trade-offs named.

The ancestor of your atlas paper is the model here: Saint-Pierre (1994) is entirely about an algorithm — the kernel-approximation scheme — yet it reads as a definitions–theorems–numerics paper, not as a build report. The distinction in practice:

| Project register (banned) | Methods-paper register (required) |
|---|---|
| "We wrote code that computes the kernel over a grid." | "Algorithm 1 computes an outer approximation of the viability kernel; Proposition 3 establishes convergence as the grid is refined." |
| "The tool took about X hours to run." | "The scheme requires O(n^d) evaluations per time step (Section 4.1)." |
| "We debugged the edge cases." | "On ∂K the approximation preserves the tangential condition (Remark 2)." |
| "Here are the output plots." | "Fig. 2 reports the computed kernel for the reference fishery (Section 5); it coincides with the closed-form kernel of the linear case (Appendix A) to grid precision." |
| "It handles more scenarios than tool Y." | "Unlike the shooting methods of [ref], the approximation applies to non-smooth dynamics and provides inner and outer bounds (Theorem 4)." |

Which papers in this project are methods papers: the **atlas paper** (SVAA), whose contribution is the set-valued machinery and its properties; and possibly an **EM paper**, if its contribution is a new model formalism or computational approach. In these, the method sections are the *contribution* and must carry the four elements above.

The contrast, stated plainly: an **application paper** treats the method instrumentally — cite it, summarize its assumptions in one paragraph, and spend the paper on claims, evidence, policies, and implications. A **methods paper** spends the paper on the method itself, and the "policy" content is a carefully bounded final section (an application example, or remarks on what the method makes possible). Most of this project's papers will be application papers; the atlas paper will not. Confusing the two registers within one manuscript — half instrument, half specimen — is a characteristic review failure.

### 6.7 Foreign-field jargon (especially computer science)
Each venue has its own lexicon (Sections 2–5). Terms from unrelated disciplines read as author artifacts. The most common offender in quantitative sustainability work is computer-science register.

| CS-culture phrasing | Journal phrasing |
|---|---|
| "Our pipeline ingests the time series, then the backend runs the kernel algorithm and outputs the plot." | "The Saint-Pierre algorithm (Saint-Pierre, 1994) is applied to the discretized dynamics; results are summarized in Fig. 4." |
| "We refactored the codebase to make the simulation scale." | "The numerical scheme was implemented in Python 3.11; runtimes are reported in Section 4.3." |
| "The atlas is generated from a modular stack." | Delete. |
| "Feature extraction / end-to-end / plug-and-play / out-of-the-box" | Describe the method in its own terms. |

Words to purge unless they denote the actual technical object: *pipeline, backend, frontend, endpoint, stack, codebase, refactor, deploy, runtime (computing sense), environment (computing sense), API (unless a real API is used), bug, feature, patch, modular (CS sense), scalable (unless performance is measured), hard-coded, boilerplate, scaffolding*.

**The legitimate exceptions (do not overcorrect):**
- **Tool and software disclosure is expected in EM** — "the model is implemented in NetLogo 6.2 / GAMS / R 4.3 (package 'deSolve')" is correct and required. Naming the tool is fine; borrowing its culture's phrasing is not.
- **Method terms are fine when they denote the actual method**: "machine learning," "artificial intelligence," "digital twins," "remote sensing" appear in EM/SS when they are the subject matter or the instrument.
- **SVAA's "Application" section** describes an applied example (a fishery, an epidemic, a population system) — in full mathematical formality, not as a case write-up.
- **Implementation detail belongs in Supplementary Material** (EM/SS) or a final "Numerical aspects" section (SVAA), written as protocol, not as changelog.

### 6.8 The self-audit: 60-second scan before submission
1. Search for dates/sequencers tied to your workflow ("first, then, after that, finally we realized") — reorder by logical role.
2. Search for "we will / we plan / next steps / future work (as promise)" — replace with "left for future work" or delete.
3. Search for words in the 6.3–6.7 kill-lists.
4. Check every "As discussed/mentioned above" — convert to structural signposting or delete.
5. Read the abstract and conclusion: neither should contain any narrative about the research process — only findings.
6. Confirm every section exists because a *reader* needs it, not because the project had that task.
7. Trace every paragraph to one of the five reader functions — claims, methods, evidence, policies, implications. Cut paragraphs that serve none of them, however much effort they represent.
8. Decide which register the manuscript belongs to and hold it: **methods paper** (method sections carry definition → properties → validation → comparison) or **application paper** (method cited instrumentally in one paragraph; the paper spent on claims, evidence, policies, implications). Half-and-half manuscripts read as neither.
9. Search for manuscript-history words — "earlier version, previous draft, we previously assumed, we corrected, we initially" — all must be gone; rejected alternatives appear only as anonymous methodological comparisons (6.1).
10. Search the self-praise and editorial word-lists (6.3); confirm every critiqued or contrasted position carries a citation to a named source (no phantom opponents).
11. Verify completeness: every valid result, derivation, and dataset must exist in full somewhere — main text, appendix, SI, or a named sibling paper (7.4). Flag every "after some algebra", "the rest is straightforward", or elided case as condensation to repair.

## 7. Novelty discipline: contributions in the main text, everything else relegated

**The rule:** the main text of an article carries only the novel contribution and the minimal apparatus needed to verify it. Everything that does not clear the novelty bar — and is not required for verification — is **relocated, not condensed**: delegated in full to an appendix or supplementary file, or demoted to a sibling paper (evaluate which destination is most appropriate). Nothing valid is silently omitted; the only material that is removed outright is content that is irreparably invalid. The landmark papers of these four venues obey this rule visibly: they are short relative to their influence, and each carries exactly one idea.

### 7.1 What "novel" means at each venue

| Venue | Counts as a genuine contribution | Does not clear the bar |
|---|---|---|
| **SVAA** | New theorems, characterizations, or algorithms: a result that could not have been stated or proven before this paper, with new consequences (a property, a bound, a regularity result, a convergent scheme). | Re-deriving known results under cosmetic changes; "viability theory restated for our application"; a computation-only paper with no new mathematics; a survey of known results. |
| **EM** | A new model structure or process representation; a novel coupling of processes; behavior not obtainable from existing formulations; a methodological advance (estimation, sensitivity, evaluation) with demonstrated payoff. | Re-running an existing model with new parameters; transferring a published model to a new site without new structural insight; a simulation study whose conclusions the literature already implies. |
| **EE** | A new measure, method, or conceptual tool that changes conclusions; a paradigm-level critique with an operational alternative; a synthesis that reorganizes a literature into a testable framework. | Applying an established index to a new country or year; "we quantify X with standard methods"; commentary without an operational proposal (that is News & Views, not an Analysis paper). |
| **SS** | Methodological or theoretical advances in integrating disciplines; framings that reorganize how a problem is studied; original findings on transitions and governance with policy traction. | Describing an existing framework again; single-discipline results relabeled as sustainability science; a literature review without a new organizing principle. |

The shared test, in one sentence: **the paper must contain at least one claim that a well-informed reader could not already have written.** If every sentence of the manuscript could have been written by someone who had only read the cited literature, the manuscript is repackaging, not research.

### 7.2 The contribution sentence (write it before drafting)

Every paper in this project should have a one-sentence contribution statement, drafted first and defended through every revision:

> "We establish X — which was previously unknown, open, or unaddressed — by means of Y; this matters because Z."

The target literature fills it in like this:

- **Hermosilla (2024, SVAA):** "We establish semicontinuity and Lipschitz properties of the sustainable-threshold map — previously unanalyzed as a set-valued map of the initial state — using tools of variational analysis; this matters because it characterizes how thresholds move with initial conditions."
- **Hickel (2020, EE):** "We construct a development index that corrects the HDI for ecological overshoot — previous modifications corrected only part of the problem — by dividing a development index by an ecological-impact index."
- **Grimm et al. (2006, EM):** "We define a standard documentation protocol for individual-based models — previously undocumented and hence unverifiable — thereby making ABMs reproducible and comparable."

Discipline: if the X clause cannot be filled with something genuinely new, the paper does not exist yet — revise the research, not the sentence. The final manuscript's introduction should contain this sentence nearly verbatim. Landmark papers state the contribution in one uncompromising paragraph early in the introduction.

### 7.3 Repackaging failure modes (audit against these)

1. **Method transplant without advance.** "We apply the Saint-Pierre algorithm to system S." That is an application paper only if the application changes understanding of S; a methods paper only if the algorithm or its analysis is advanced. Without either, it belongs in grey literature, not these journals.
2. **Re-derivation.** Proving known results under marginally different hypotheses with no new consequences is repackaging. The correct form: one sentence citing the known result ("under these hypotheses the tangential condition yields… (Aubin, 1991)"), with the cited proof standing in for the repeated one — retention by reference — and the saved space invested in what is actually new. The inverse failure is equally real: when a derivation is *not* standard, it is presented in full (main text, appendix, or SI); "the proof is omitted for brevity" with no citation is a gap, not a stylistic choice.
3. **Juxtaposition as combination.** "We combine method A with method B" is a contribution only when the coupling produces results unavailable to A and B separately — and the paper demonstrates that difference. Otherwise it is two papers stapled together.
4. **Compilation dressed as theory.** The atlas risk: a compilation of cases, maps, and parameter sets presented as a mathematical paper. If the set-valued core carries new mathematics, the compilation belongs to the application papers or the SI; if it does not, the atlas paper has no claim on SVAA.
5. **Framework inflation.** "We present a framework for…" earns its place in SS/EE only if the framework reorganizes existing work so that new predictions or policies follow (as planetary boundaries or leverage points did). A diagram plus a literature walkthrough is a review — for a review venue or an invited overview.
6. **Validation-only or benchmark-only material.** Demonstrating that a method reproduces a reference case is one element of a methods paper (Section 6.6), never a paper by itself.

### 7.4 Triage: main text vs appendix vs supplementary vs sibling paper

For every element of a draft, apply the triage. Two principles govern it. First, **relocation, not condensation**: valid content is never compressed out of existence — the triage decides *where* content lives, not whether it survives. Second, **nothing is omitted unless irreparably invalid**: content that is wrong beyond repair is removed; everything else is retained in full, in the most appropriate place.

| Element | Test | Destination |
|---|---|---|
| The contribution and its proof/evidence | The paper's central claim cannot stand without it. | **Main text** |
| Material needed to verify a main claim but routine or derivative (standard lemmas, extended derivations, full tables) | A reviewer needs it to check the claim, but it interrupts the argument. | **Appendix** (Elsevier venues, SS) — in full, never condensed — subject to the SVAA exception below |
| Material a reviewer might want but does not need: parameter tables, full scenario outputs, robustness checks, data-cleaning details, code, extra figures, non-novel but necessary validation | Verification of the main claim does not depend on it, but the content is valid and part of the work. | **Supplementary material / ESM** — retained in full |
| Valid content that does not serve this paper's claim but is substantive on its own terms (e.g., a secondary application, a background synthesis, an empirical aside) | It would dilute this paper's argument, yet deserves publication. | **Sibling paper** — demoted to the paper in the project whose claim it serves; evaluate which. |
| Activity narration, effort reports, irreparably wrong material | Nothing depends on it and it cannot be salvaged. | **Cut** (the only case of removal) |

Background that is valid but redundant with the literature is not removed: it is **retained by citation** — the content already lives in the literature, and the paper points to it instead of restating it (7.3).

**The SVAA exception (mandatory):** the Editor-in-Chief's standing instruction — *"The proofs should not be put in the appendix but within the natural flow of the arguments in the main text"* — means that in the atlas paper, proofs are main text by rule, however long. What SVAA relegates instead: computational details of the numerical scheme (grid, tolerances, runtimes) to a final "Numerical aspects" section or SI; background lemmas to the Preliminaries, kept minimal by citation rather than restatement.

**The length corollary.** Landmark papers are rarely the longest papers in their venues. The main text is reduced to the minimum that keeps the claim verifiable — and the apparatus removed from it is *relocated in full*, never condensed out of existence — while the freed space is not refilled with background. If a manuscript is long, the problem is almost never too much novelty.

### 7.5 Anatomy of a landmark paper (the pattern to imitate)

Across all four venues, the flagship papers share a formula:

1. **One idea, named.** The contribution is a *named object*: viability kernel; planetary boundaries; ODD protocol; leverage points; sustainable thresholds; doughnut. A named concept is quotable and citable — it becomes vocabulary.
2. **The gap is stated against named predecessors.** "The problem with the HDI is…" (Hickel); "Until now, sustainable thresholds were treated only for fixed initial states" (the SVAA cluster). Novelty is defined by what it is not.
3. **Minimal apparatus.** Only the machinery that makes the idea operational and checkable. What readers can reproduce themselves is cited or relegated.
4. **One decisive piece of evidence.** A theorem, a computed kernel, a cross-country table, a worked protocol — singular, central, and used to validate the whole claim.
5. **Implications that others can build on.** The paper closes by handing the named concept forward: open problems (SVAA), "future research needed" (SS), policy consequences (EE), model extensions (EM).

Audit: read your introduction beside any two exemplars from Sections 2–5 and check that your first page does the same five jobs in the same order. If the exemplar's first page reads cleaner, the difference is almost always apparatus and background that should have been triaged out (7.4).

### 7.6 The cross-paper novelty audit (mandatory for a multi-paper project)

The venues' readerships overlap and Elsevier screens for duplicate publication, so each paper must carry a distinct, defensible core:

1. Write the contribution sentence (7.2) for every planned paper.
2. Check pairwise: no two sentences may be satisfied by the same result, the same figure, or the same data table. One figure appearing in two papers is a duplicate-publication risk unless it is explicitly repurposed with new analysis — and declared.
3. Check the chain: the papers should form a ladder — atlas paper (set-valued machinery, methods register, SVAA) → application papers (each with its own new modeling insight or empirical finding, EM/EE) → synthesis paper (the integration insight, SS) → policy commentary (EE Commentary / News & Views). Each rung cites the one above and is novel relative to it.
4. If two planned papers fail the audit (one would be implied by the other), merge them, or demote one to the other's SI. Publishing both invites the review outcome hardest to recover from: the accusation that the second paper adds nothing.

## 8. Cross-venue style rules that hold everywhere

1. **Abstract arithmetic:** purpose + method + principal results + major conclusion, in that order. Every one of the four venues mandates "concise and factual … able to stand alone." Never open an abstract with "In this paper, we…" in EE/EM/SS — those venues prefer the problem first. SVAA tolerates "This work aims at…" as sentence two.
2. **Keyword hygiene:** noun phrases, no "and"/"of" compounds, no invented abbreviations, 4–7 per venue's cap. Reuse the same keyword set across your paper family so they co-index.
3. **Tense discipline:** present tense for established facts and your results ("the viability kernel is the largest closed viability domain"); past for what you did ("we computed…"); past for cited work ("Saint-Pierre proved…").
4. **"We" is universal in all four venues**; avoid "the author" in EE/EM/SS.
5. **Equation grammar:** equations are sentences — punctuate them (commas/full stops), define every symbol at first use, and *restate each important equation in words* once (this is mandatory in EE/SS, good practice in EM, and in SVAA it goes in the Remarks).
6. **Hedging norms:** EE/SS reward epistemic modals ("may," "suggests," "consistent with"); EM expects calibrated uncertainty language ("under the assumptions of the model"); SVAA is declarative in theorems and hedged only in interpretation ("in ecological terms, this suggests…").
7. **Figures:** self-contained captions (what, under what scenario, units); cited in order; minimal panels; letter labels (a)(b)(c); no color-only encodings (EE/EM print constraints). Vector graphics preferred everywhere; SVAA: TikZ/PGF plots are the house style.
8. **Data & code statements:** all four venues now expect a data availability statement; EM additionally expects code/software disclosure (name the language, version, and repository).
9. **Tables:** numbered in citation order, title explains abbreviations, footnotes with superscript lowercase letters; don't duplicate figure content in tables.
10. **Titles:** declarative and specific — "Sensitivity analysis of the set of sustainable thresholds" (SVAA style), "The sustainable development index: measuring the ecological efficiency of human development in the Anthropocene" (EE style). No abbreviations or formulae in titles (Elsevier rule).
11. **Completeness and relocation.** Valid content is never condensed out of existence. Material that does not belong in the main text is relocated — appendix, supplementary file, or a sibling paper, whichever is most appropriate — and retained in full. Nothing is omitted unless irreparably invalid. Proofs and derivations appear in full; the single exception is that a proof repeating standard literature or textbooks is replaced by a citation (retention by reference).
12. **No manuscript archaeology.** No reference to earlier manuscript versions, prior mistakes, or the drafting process — not even when a rejected alternative has pedagogical value, which is then presented as an anonymous methodological comparison (6.1). No phantom or strawman positions: every position critiqued or contrasted carries a citation to a named source (6.3). No self-praise or editorial commentary (6.3).
