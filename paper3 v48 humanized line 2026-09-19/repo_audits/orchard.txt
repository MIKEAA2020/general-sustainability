## 2. Where the analogy loses, and why it is not the final word

**(a) It is a special case.** In the orchard, fruit is separable from trees: sustainable harvest never touches the stock, so under f ≤ 1 the environment sits at Aₘₐₓ and does nothing. But a large share of real biocapacity is not fruit—it is *stock increment*: forest biocapacity is measured as annual timber growth, fish as recruitment. Harvesting increment keeps the stock below its unmanaged maximum even when fully sustainable; that is the Schaefer/forestry picture, and it is exactly the manuscript's Eq. (1): dA/dt = ρA(1 − A/Aₘₐₓ) − γE with an interior sustainable equilibrium A* < Aₘₐₓ. The manuscript's *depletion term* was right for that component. Its error was elsewhere: defining B = bM while depleting with E, so that biocapacity and depletion used different books. Under the increment picture, B must be the regeneration flow b_G·G(A), and then "deficit = stock decline" holds as an identity, no ramp needed.

**(b) It discards the only hidden channel.** Killing trees is visible in area accounts (deforestation is measured). What area accounts *cannot* see is yield erosion on the surviving stock—soil fertility loss under continuous cropping, overgrazed pasture, climate-reduced yields. That is what the manuscript's D → b channel was reaching for, and it is the channel behind the reviewer's point that accounted biocapacity is overstated. The orchard has no counterpart (over-picking does not reduce next year's yield). The analogy alone therefore weakens the paper's strongest empirical message.

**(c) It trivialises the sustainable-baseline analysis.** At the kink (f = 1) the environment is inert, K is constant, and the population equation is pure Hutchinson: Hopf at rτₚ = π/2 = 78.5 yr, τ_g irrelevant. The manuscript's centrepiece—two lags interacting in a dynamically active environment—is deleted, not corrected.

## 3. The version that contains both

Let the stock's service have two parts: a flow yield φ(A) = bA that is separable from the stock (fruit, crops, eggs) and a stock increment G(A) = ρA(1 − A/Aₘₐₓ) that can be harvested only by removing stock (timber, fish). With b_G the value of one hectare of standing stock in gha·yr (your V), biocapacity is

B = bA + b_G G(A),

and demand is met first from flow, then from increment, then from capital:

dA(t)/dt = G(A(t − τ_g)) − [E − bA]₊ / b_G.   (1‴)

Whenever E > bA this reads dA/dt = (B − E)/b_G: the GFN statement "overshoot is drawn down from the stock" becomes an identity, with the kink only at the point where demand falls entirely within flow yield.

The two limits:
- b → 0 (all biocapacity is increment): manuscript's Eq. (1) exactly, γ = 1/b_G, B = b_G G(A). Interior sustainable equilibrium, a₁₁ = ρ(1 − 2A*/Aₘₐₓ), the χ-classification and two-delay analysis as in the joint assessment.
- b_G G ≪ bA (all biocapacity is flow): your orchard, with A_c(E), the vicious cycle and the liquidation mask.

In between—which is where the global system sits—the sustainable equilibrium is interior (because some biocapacity is increment) *and* the liquidation feedback is present:

a₁₁ = ρ(1 − 2A*/Aₘₐₓ) + b/b_G.

The composition of biocapacity, ψ = bA*/B* (flow share), becomes a new control parameter with direct empirical meaning (cropland and grazing yields versus forest increment and fish recruitment in the accounts). Small ψ: overshoot manifests as smooth stock decline that can be stabilised by demographic feedback; large ψ: overshoot is invisible until liabilities exceed flow yield, then liquidation with a threshold. That is a testable prediction the manuscript could not make and the analogy alone cannot state.

The degradation channel is kept as a third, separately evidenced ingredient: b declines under sustained overuse of the *remaining* stock, and it is the only channel that area accounts miss. It should be presented as such, not as "ecological debt" doing the work that A already does.

**What the analogy actually changes (and why it is deeper than a local B2 patch)**

The vicious cycle is then in the vector field: fewer trees \(\to\) smaller \(B\) \(\to\) larger gap if demand does not fall as fast \(\to\) more liquidation, plus the separate debt channel that lowers \(b\) on the remaining trees.
