# Delay-Induced Regime Change in Harvested Stocks: The Mobilising and Protective Channels of Institutional Feedback, and the Review Interval as Control

## Abstract

Delayed feedback is a classic source of instability in renewable-resource systems. Almost all of the delays studied so far, however, have been ecological: maturation lags, recruitment lags, delayed density responses. The delay studied in this paper sits in a different place — in the governance loop, the chain that leads from an observed stock decline to an institutional response. We analyse a three-state model: the harvested stock, a memory of the deficit, and the management effort currently deployed. A multiplicative gate holds effort between fixed bounds by construction. Two response rules are compared throughout. Under the mobilising rule, effort grows as more effort is deployed. Under the protective rule, a quota-tracking law restores harvest toward a cap.

For the mobilising rule, the main finding is that a delay of intermediate length stabilises the equilibrium. Two Hopf crossings — parameter values at which a pair of eigenvalues crosses the imaginary axis and oscillations are born — bound a window of delays inside which the lag itself holds the loop stable. Both crossings are subcritical, and both are certified by interval arithmetic (near 3.7 and 150 yr). For the protective rule the result is simpler and stronger: the loop gain stays below one at the calibrated point, so the equilibrium is exponentially stable at every delay — a no-Hopf theorem. When governance acts instead through periodic review, in which the stock is assessed once per review interval and the decision is held fixed in between (sample-and-hold), the two rules respond in opposite directions. Annual protective review remains stable. Annual mobilising review is unstable, and the loop restabilises only when the review interval exceeds about 6.5 yr, through a Neimark–Sacker-type crossing — the sampled-review analogue of a Hopf transition. An apparent threshold near 2.3 yr, reported by a first-order numerical update, is an artefact of the discretisation, not a property of the governance system. Continuing the solution branches in the delay reveals a five-regime attractor topology, with two folds certified at the discrete collocation level (the continuum off-grid residual stage and the continuous-delay lift remain open) and a basin-boundary transition toward a large-amplitude attractor of unverified identity.

The analysed class is deliberately stock-agnostic: a harvested renewable stock under institutional control, with fisheries as the motivating instance. Its three design coordinates — the sign structure of the response rule, the deployment delay, and the review cadence — are therefore available to any periodically reviewed renewable-resource regime. Documented governance timelines occupy the same scales: the northern cod record runs from annual assessment with incremental response to the 1992 moratorium and, thirty-two years later, the 2024 reopening. That record grounds the two timing coordinates without calibrating any coefficient. The message of the paper is stated plainly. The form and timing of institutional response, not ecological lag alone, determine whether governance stabilises or destabilises a harvested stock; and the review interval acts as a local spectral design parameter — a setting that shapes the eigenvalues which decide local stability — with opposite effects under the two rules.
