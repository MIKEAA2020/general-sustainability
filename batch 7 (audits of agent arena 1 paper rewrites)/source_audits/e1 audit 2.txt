# 1. The most consequential remaining issues

## 1.1 The bootstrap explanation is mathematically misleading

You write:

> “The two columns of Table 9 are separate procedures applied to different estimands…”

and:

> “Because the square root compresses the heavy right tail … the resampled RMSE gap is far more stable than the raw loss differential…”

The two effect measures differ in scale, but their zero nulls and signs are equivalent. For any paired sample or bootstrap resample,

\[
\sqrt{\overline{L_A}}-\sqrt{\overline{L_B}}
=
\frac{\overline{L_A}-\overline{L_B}}
{\sqrt{\overline{L_A}}+\sqrt{\overline{L_B}}},
\]

provided the denominator is positive.

Consequently:

- the RMSE difference and mean squared-loss difference always have the same sign;
- their zero-null hypotheses are equivalent;
- if your interval-derived \(p\) is calculated from bootstrap tail proportions around zero, applying the square root does not change those sign proportions.

Different inferential procedures can certainly disagree. But **“different estimands” and square-root tail compression do not adequately explain these particular disagreements**.

**Action:** delete that explanation. Provide the exact bootstrap algorithm, including the formula for the reported \(p\). Preferably remove the \(p\) column unless you implement and justify a calibrated hypothesis test.

Use:

> Diebold–Mariano statistics and paired moving-block-bootstrap intervals summarize uncertainty using different approximations. Their conclusions need not coincide in these small, dependent samples. We therefore report the statistics and intervals separately and do not interpret the bootstrap intervals as an additional calibrated hypothesis test.

Also remove “decisive” later in the section.

## 1.3 The new profile analysis does not yet justify a confidence set

You now report a profile objective, which is a valuable improvement. But:

> “A profile-likelihood diagnostic settles the identification question directly.”

is too strong.

You fit by least squares and have not yet supplied the likelihood assumptions needed to turn that objective into a likelihood-ratio statistic. Under independent Gaussian residuals with unknown variance, one conventional profile statistic is

\[
\Lambda(\mathfrak s)
=
n_{\mathrm{tr}}
\log
\left\{
\frac{\mathrm{SSE}(\mathfrak s)}
{\mathrm{SSE}(\widehat{\mathfrak s})}
\right\}.
\]

Your \(0.78\) is consistent with approximately twelve transitions and the printed MSE ratio. But the conventional \(\chi^2_1\) reference needs justification because:

- the estimated threshold is at a boundary;
- \(r\) is also at its upper bound;
- the sample is very small;
- the observations are reconstructed states;
- residual independence and homoskedasticity are not established.

Furthermore, the “admissible range”
\[
[0,\min S_t]
\]
has not previously been declared as the threshold’s estimation bound. Negative production above the observed minimum does **not** make larger thresholds mathematically inadmissible.

**Action:** distinguish:

1. the actual estimation domain;
2. the subset explored in the profile;
3. whether the profile is descriptive or inferential.

> A profile of the least-squares objective indicates weak practical constraint on the Allee parameter over the examined interval.

```latex
\textbf{Proposition 4.1 (No recovery within a declining invariant region).}
Let \(F:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)\) satisfy \(F(0)=0\), and suppose
there exists \(s_*>0\) such that \(F(s)<s\) for every \(0<s<s_*\).
```

```latex
\subsection{1. Introduction}\label{introduction}

Fisheries assessments use biological and statistical structure to
estimate stock status and evaluate management options. Whether that
structure also improves forecasts of a specified assessment output is
a separate empirical question. For point forecasting, additional
structure should therefore be evaluated against simple benchmarks
rather than judged from in-sample fit alone.

Northern cod (\emph{Gadus morhua}) in NAFO divisions 2J3KL provides a
demanding case. The stock underwent a severe collapse in the late
1980s and early 1990s, under an assessment and management regime whose
failures have been extensively examined (Hutchings and Myers, 1994;
Walters and Maguire, 1996). The prolonged period of low biomass
following the July 1992 moratorium motivated debate about depensation
and constraints on recovery (Shelton and Healey, 1999). Partial
rebuilding was documented in the 2010s (Rose and Rowe, 2015), while
subsequent reconstructions describe a post-2015 slowdown and years of
negative surplus production (Rose, 2026).

This study evaluates forecasts of assessment-derived spawning-stock
biomass (SSB) using five surplus-production implementations and two
simple baselines. The implementations differ in their production
function, catch treatment, residual dynamics, and initialization.
Additional variants use survey-based initialization or prey-informed
production. Last-value persistence provides the principal benchmark:
each forecast holds SSB at its value at the prediction origin.

The evaluation uses two assessment reconstructions, analysed
separately: the 1983--2015 NCAM M-shift series and the 1954--2024
xteNCAM series. Fixed-origin windows examine the collapse and
subsequent rebuilding, while rolling-origin predictions assess
one- and five-year performance. Retention is determined by RMSE
relative to persistence and designated structural comparators;
secondary diagnostics do not determine selection.

The experiment is retrospective rather than operational. Historical
SSB values are taken from later assessment reconstructions rather
than assessment vintages available at each origin. Prescribed catch
paths, and future index observations in the acoustic-index variant,
make the corresponding predictions conditional hindcasts.
Accordingly, the question is whether the tested implementations
predict these reconstructed SSB series better than persistence, not
whether they would have provided successful real-time advice.

The study does not identify the cause of the collapse, interpret a
fitted depensation parameter as an established biological Allee
threshold, or evaluate the adequacy of the moratorium. Its
contribution is a benchmark comparison and an examination of how
parameter constraint and forecast initialization affect the tested
implementations. Section 2 describes the data and methods, Section 3
reports the results, and Sections 4 and 5 discuss their interpretation
and limitations.
```

## 1.5 “Training-window maximum” for the `K` bound is defined inconsistently

**Locations:** Section 2.2, Section 3.6/Table 10 bounds paragraph.

## 1.8 Proposition 4.1 says “fixed catch sequence,” but the proof requires an autonomous constant-catch map

**Fix:** either:

1. replace “fixed catch sequence” with “constant catch” in Proposition 4.1, or  
2. state explicitly that the proposition applies to M1 and M1b under constant catch, and only motivates, but does not formally cover, M2’s time-varying catch case.

# 3. Logical and methodological gaps

---

## 3.1 The profile-likelihood diagnostic for \(\mathfrak{s}\) needs a short methods description

**Location:** Observation 3.2.

The paper says:

> “Referred to a \(\chi^2_1\) distribution, the implied likelihood-ratio statistic reaches \(0.78\)…”

But it does not state:

- the likelihood assumption, e.g. Gaussian one-step errors;
- the formula used to convert sum-of-squares to a likelihood-ratio statistic;
- the grid resolution for \(\mathfrak{s}\);
- whether the residual variance is re-estimated at each profile point.

**Problem:** the diagnostic is introduced as if standard, but the implementation details are missing.

**Fix:** add one sentence such as:

> “Assuming Gaussian one-step errors with variance estimated from the minimum-objective fit, the profile likelihood-ratio statistic is \(n\log(\mathrm{SSE}(\mathfrak{s})/\mathrm{SSE}_{\min})\), evaluated on a grid of \(\mathfrak{s}\) values over \([0,\min_{\text{train}}S_t]\).”

---

## 3.2 The role of M3’s residual state in forecasts should be specified more explicitly

**Location:** Section 2.2.

The text says:

> “all forecasts reported here set \(\varepsilon_t = 0\), so every forecast trajectory is the deterministic conditional-mean path. Except in M3, which carries the fitted residual forward as a state.”

This is better than before, but still underspecified.

Questions remaining:

- Is the residual state initialized at the last training residual?
- For horizon \(h\), is the residual propagated as \(\phi^h e_T\)?
- Does M4 inherit the same residual propagation with a delayed \(S_{t-1}\) start?

**Fix:** add a short equation, e.g.

> “At origin \(t\), M3 initializes the residual at the last training residual \(e_t\) and propagates it as \(e_{t+h}=\phi^h e_t\); M4 uses the same residual propagation but starts the surplus trajectory from \(S_{t-1}\).”

---

## 3.3 The index module’s missing-year rule needs clarification for multi-step forecasts

**Location:** Section 3.4.

Text:

> “if no observation exists in \([1991, t]\) the module is not evaluated at that origin rather than falling back on a pre-1991 value.”

Then:

> “Along a multi-step trajectory \(t\) is the forecast year being stepped to…”

**Problem:** For a five-year forecast, there are several future \(t\) values. If one of those years lacks an index observation, is the whole origin dropped? Is the missing step skipped? Is the index scaling set to one for that step? The reduced origin counts suggest origins are dropped, but the rule is not explicit.

**Fix:** state explicitly:

> “If any forecast year in the horizon lacks an admissible post-1991 index observation, that origin is excluded from the index-module origin set.”

if that is the rule.

## 3.4 “Constant-productivity surplus law cannot reproduce” the stall is a strong claim

**Location:** Section 4, reconstruction-level corroboration.

Text:

> “…is precisely the configuration a constant-productivity surplus law cannot reproduce…”

**Problem:** A constant-productivity model can produce low or negative surplus if biomass is above carrying capacity or if catch exceeds surplus. The more precise claim is that a constant-productivity model cannot easily reproduce a prolonged low-biomass stall with negative surplus without invoking catch or observation errors. Given the paper’s caution elsewhere, this sentence is a little too absolute.

**Fix:** soften to:

> “is difficult to reconcile with a constant-productivity surplus law in the absence of time-varying productivity, unmodelled mortality, or observation error.”

## 3.5 The phrase “the value of a timely assessment exceeds the value of any structure tested” risks overinterpretation

**Fix:** make the descriptor explicit:
