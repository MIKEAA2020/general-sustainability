## 1. Separate a harvest-induced collapse threshold from biological depensation

### The ecological issue

For the Schaefer model,

\[
S_{t+1}=S_t+rS_t(1-S_t/K)-C,
\]

positive equilibria satisfy

\[
rS(1-S/K)=C.
\]

When \(0<C<rK/4\),

\[
S_{\pm}(C)
=
\frac K2
\left(
1\pm\sqrt{1-\frac{4C}{rK}}
\right).
\]

The lower threshold therefore depends on catch. It is a **harvest-induced threshold**, even though the underlying Schaefer production function has no biological Allee effect.

Using your printed \(r=1.935\) and \(K=1032.7\) kt:

- \(C=240\) kt gives \(S_-\approx144\) kt;
- \(C=5\) kt gives \(S_-\approx2.6\) kt;
- as \(C\rightarrow0\), the lower threshold approaches zero.

The 1995 SSB of approximately \(9.7\) kt is below the first threshold but above the second.

**Consequently, failure to recover under continued catch of 240 kt is not an ecological explanation of failure to recover after catches were drastically reduced.**

## 2. Test whether persistence wins because the model imposes historical catches on a depleted stock

This is closely related to the first insight, but it directly addresses your forecast results.

### The ecological hypothesis

Your expanding-window M1 uses the historical training-mean catch throughout each forecast. After collapse, that mean can remain dominated by catches from the former high-biomass fishery.

At low biomass,

\[
g(S)\approx rS,
\]

whereas constant \(C\) does not decline with stock size. Thus the effective removal burden,

\[
\frac{\overline C_{\mathrm{train}}}{S_t},
\]

can become extremely large.

Your numerous floor-hitting forecasts may therefore reflect an interaction between:

1. a depleted stock;
2. historical averaging of catch;
3. constant absolute removals;
4. a nonnegative state constraint.

That is more specific than “constant productivity fails.”

### Targeted analysis

For every M1 forecast origin, calculate:

\[
R_t=\frac{\overline C_{\mathrm{train}}}{S_t},
\qquad
Q_t=\widehat g(S_t)-\overline C_{\mathrm{train}},
\]

and record:

- whether the forecast reaches the floor;
- years since the catch regime changed;
- forecast error;
- whether the initial state lies below the fitted harvest-induced threshold.

Then compare a small set of **explicit catch assumptions**:

- expanding historical mean;
- recent-window mean;
- last observed catch;
- realized future catch, already represented by M2.

First hold production parameters fixed to isolate the forecast-input effect. Then, separately, refit to examine the combined estimation-and-input effect.

These are predictive sensitivity analyses, not management counterfactuals.

### Informative outcomes

- **If floor hits largely disappear under recent catch assumptions:** part of the forecast failure is inherited exploitation history, not an intrinsic low-stock production barrier.
- **If floor hits persist:** production estimation, numerical behavior, or the biomass-state representation deserves greater attention.
- **If floor hits disappear but RMSE remains poor:** removing an implausible extinction mechanism is insufficient to recover useful dynamics.

### Ecological payoff

You could establish whether the model carries the memory of the **old fishery** longer than the stock carries the productive capacity that supported it.

That is a meaningful regime-change result, rather than another catch sensitivity table.

## 3. Ask whether SSB is an adequate ecological state variable

**This is probably the highest-value enhancement requiring additional biological data.**

### The ecological hypothesis

Two stocks with identical SSB can have different near-term trajectories because they contain different:

- age compositions;
- immature cohorts approaching maturity;
- mature-fish survival prospects;
- weight-at-age;
- maturity schedules.

A scalar SSB map assumes these differences can be adequately represented by the current SSB and the specified inputs. That assumption is biologically consequential.

The ecological question is therefore:

> Does comparable SSB imply comparable subsequent production across collapse, rebuilding, and stall phases?

### A direct demographic diagnostic

Write SSB as

\[
S_t=\sum_a N_{a,t}m_{a,t}w_{a,t},
\]

where \(N\) is abundance, \(m\) maturity, and \(w\) weight.

With suitable age-resolved assessment outputs, annual SSB change can be decomposed into contributions from:

- cohort survival and abundance change;
- growth of surviving cohorts;
- changing maturity;
- entry of younger cohorts into the spawning stock.

The decomposition must align cohorts across years and handle the plus group correctly. If several factors change simultaneously, use a stated decomposition convention, such as a symmetric allocation, rather than implicitly assigning interactions to whichever factor is changed first.

This is a decomposition of the assessment reconstruction—not independent identification of causes.

### A minimal predictive test

Rather than immediately building another full assessment, add **one biologically selected state descriptor**:

- biomass or abundance of cohorts approaching maturity; or
- an age-composition measure among mature fish.

For example, a parsimonious model could predict the SSB increment from current SSB and a pre-spawner measure \(J_t\):

\[
\widehat{\Delta S_t}
=
\alpha+\beta S_t+\gamma J_t.
\]

The precise age range for \(J_t\) should follow cod maturity information, not whichever range produces the best score.

Compare it with a model using \(S_t\) alone, using the same origins and estimation safeguards.

### What different results would mean

- **Demographic information improves forecasts:** the failure is partly a failure of scalar state representation, not simply a lack of enough parameters.
- **It explains reconstruction changes but does not improve forecasts:** biologically meaningful accounting does not necessarily provide usable predictive information.
- **It adds little:** the tested demographic descriptor is insufficient; this does not establish that age structure is irrelevant.

### Why this is genuinely ecological

It moves the paper from “complexity did not help” to:

> The missing structure may concern the composition of the stock, rather than additional nonlinear functions of its aggregate biomass.

That is a much more consequential distinction.

## 4. Test whether the capelin module represents the right biological mechanism

Your current prey module has a structural limitation that deserves much more attention.

### What follows from the equation

You multiply production by

\[
q(I_t)=
\left(\frac{I_t}{I_{\mathrm{ref}}}\right)^b.
\]

For positive index values, \(q(I_t)>0\), regardless of the sign of \(b\). Therefore,

\[
\operatorname{sign}\{q(I_t)g(S_t)\}
=
\operatorname{sign}\{g(S_t)\}.
\]

For a Schaefer fit with \(0<S_t<K\) and \(r>0\), production remains positive. The index can reduce its magnitude, but it cannot make the modeled production term negative.

Low prey availability can, however, potentially affect more than positive growth:

- adult condition and survival;
- weight-at-age;
- maturation;
- reproductive output;
- recruitment appearing in SSB after a lag.

**Your module tests a positive multiplicative modulation of production, not food limitation in general.**

### A targeted mechanistic contrast

Compare two constrained formulations:

#### Growth-modulation formulation

\[
\Delta S_t
=
r f(I_t)S_t(1-S_t/K)-C_t.
\]

#### Food-deficit loss formulation

\[
\Delta S_t
=
rS_t(1-S_t/K)-C_t
-
\mu S_t d(I_{t-\ell}),
\qquad \mu\ge0,
\]

where \(d\) is a bounded prey-deficit index and \(\ell\) is a biologically specified lag.

The second formulation can represent an additional loss associated with food deficit. It is still phenomenological when applied to SSB and should not be labelled estimated starvation mortality without corroborating data.

Do not freely optimize several lags, nonlinear shapes, and thresholds on these short records. Select one or two hypotheses from external biological evidence.

### A stronger process-based enhancement

If condition or weight-at-age data are available, test the sequence:

\[
\text{capelin availability}
\rightarrow
\text{cod condition or growth}
\rightarrow
\text{SSB change}.
\]

This is more discriminating than a direct correlation between capelin and SSB.

Also address spatial support: a 3L spring acoustic index is not automatically equivalent to prey availability throughout 2J3KL. Where available, cod–capelin overlap or diet information would be more mechanistically relevant than simply adding more basin-wide covariates.

### Ecological payoff

You would distinguish:

> The available prey index did not improve forecasts through the tested positive production multiplier.

from the much broader—and unsupported—claim:

> Prey information does not help explain the stock’s dynamics.

The first is already warranted; the proposed comparison could establish **which pathway is missing**.

## 5. Distinguish low-density depensation from phase-dependent productivity

### The ecological hypothesis

A pooled relationship between biomass and subsequent growth can mix two different phenomena:

1. growth changes because biomass is low;
2. growth changes because ecological conditions differ between historical periods.

Low biomass and poor environmental conditions may coincide. A fitted Allee term can then absorb regime effects without identifying density dependence.

### First analysis: repeated biomass states

Identify observations from different phases with comparable SSB. Within the region of overlapping biomass support, compare:

- subsequent SSB increments;
- catch;
- available prey or demographic descriptors.

A phase plot can be informative:

- horizontal axis: \(S_t\);
- vertical axis: \(\Delta S_t\), or apparent production clearly labelled as such;
- color: time or externally defined phase;
- arrows: temporal ordering.

If similar SSB corresponds to systematically different increments across phases, a time-invariant biomass-only relationship is questionable.

This would be evidence of omitted state or changing conditions—not proof of alternative stable states or ecological hysteresis.

### Second analysis: separate weak depensation from a positive threshold

Your \(\mathfrak s\rightarrow0\) model is not ecologically neutral:

\[
g(S)=rS(1-S/K)\frac{S}{K}.
\]

Its per-unit-biomass production is

\[
\frac{g(S)}{S}
=
r\frac{S}{K}(1-S/K),
\]

which approaches zero at low biomass. It retains **weak depensation**, even though there is no positive zero-production threshold.

Add one simple control:

- Schaefer;
- cubic model with \(\mathfrak s=0\) fixed;
- freely estimated \(\mathfrak s\).

All three should use the same catch convention and comparable estimation effort.

### Informative outcomes

- **Fixed-zero cubic matches the free-threshold model:** any improvement comes from production-curve shape, not information identifying a positive Allee threshold.
- **A positive threshold improves held-out forecasts robustly:** this supports that implementation, but biological interpretation still requires separating density effects from phase effects.
- **Phase-specific patterns dominate:** density dependence estimated across regimes is likely confounded.

### Ecological payoff

This would make the depensation analysis a real contribution rather than a boundary-estimate caveat:

> The data may distinguish production curvature from compensation while failing to identify a positive recovery threshold.

That is a sharper and more biologically informative conclusion.

## 6. Determine whether models fail to predict change or merely predict too much change

This can be done largely with your existing forecast files.

### The diagnostic

For origin \(t\) and horizon \(h\), define

\[
\Delta_{t,h}=S_{t+h}-S_t,
\qquad
\delta_{t,h}=\widehat S_{t+h}-S_t.
\]

Persistence predicts \(\delta=0\). The model’s squared-error improvement over persistence is exactly

\[
\Delta_{t,h}^{\,2}
-
(\Delta_{t,h}-\delta_{t,h})^2
=
2\Delta_{t,h}\delta_{t,h}
-
\delta_{t,h}^{\,2}.
\]

This separates:

- **alignment with observed change:** \(2\Delta\delta\);
- **the penalty for the magnitude of the predicted change:** \(\delta^2\).

A model can predict the correct direction and still lose because it extrapolates too strongly.

### Ecologically informative analyses

For each phase and horizon, report:

- observed versus predicted increments;
- the alignment term;
- the magnitude penalty;
- signed bias;
- contributions to total squared-error differences.

Use a small number of externally motivated phases. Do not choose boundaries to maximize apparent differences.

### Possible interpretations

- **Negative alignment during collapse:** the model projects rebuilding when the reconstructed stock is declining.
- **Positive alignment but excessive magnitude during recovery:** the model detects growth direction but overstates productive capacity.
- **Near-zero observed increments during stall with large predicted changes:** persistence wins partly because the model invents directional dynamics not expressed in the target.
- **Skill outside transition periods but losses concentrated at regime changes:** the central limitation is ecological transfer across phases rather than uniformly poor dynamics.

### An optional forecasting control

Shrink structural departures toward persistence:

\[
\widehat S^{(\lambda)}_{t+h}
=
S_t+\lambda\bigl(\widehat S^{(M)}_{t+h}-S_t\bigr),
\qquad 0\le\lambda\le1.
\]

Estimate \(\lambda\) using training-only validation.

If moderate shrinkage restores skill, the model contains useful directional information but expresses it too strongly. If only \(\lambda\approx0\) works, the tested departures contribute little.

**That distinguishes an ecological signal with poor calibration from a forecast with no useful signal.**

## 3. M1b cannot test the Allee hypothesis that matters for this stock

The biologically serious depensation hypotheses for Northern cod are not a small threshold below the 1995–2007 minimum (\(9.7\) kt). They are:

- a predator pit (seals) that can trap the stock below a *high* threshold;
- recruitment depensation with a multi-year lag;
- spatial contraction and lost spawning aggregation.

Your estimator forbids the first of these. ## 5. Persistence winning is itself ecological

SSB is a stock of many age classes. Northern cod generation time is on the order of a generation per five-to-ten years, not per year. NCAM/xteNCAM then smooth that stock. High autocorrelation is therefore the expected baseline, for biological as well as statistical reasons.

The five-year horizon is the interesting one ecologically, because it is a substantial fraction of a generation. One-year RMSE mostly measures whether a smoother was followed. Do not treat \(h=1\) and \(h=5\) as two copies of the same test.

\hat r_t=\frac{P_t}{S_t(1-S_t/K)}.

This is a reconstruction of productivity, not a forecast model. If \(\hat r_t\) collapses in 1991–94, rebounds slowly, and sags after 2015, you have shown the regime structure that a constant-\(r\) ladder cannot forecast. If it does not, that is also informative.

## 1. The core ecological insight that should be made explicit

The strongest ecological message of the paper is not simply that “structure fails.” It is more specific and more interesting:

> For Northern cod, the reconstructed spawning-stock biomass contains more usable one- to five-year forecast information in its recent demographic memory than in a stationary surplus-production relation. The dominant forecastable signal is therefore persistence, demographic memory, and assessment smoothing, while the dominant forecast failures arise from nonstationary vital rates, unmodelled mortality/productivity shocks, and the inability of a constant-parameter biomass map to represent collapse and stall dynamics.

That is a much richer ecological statement than “the ladder loses to persistence.” It connects the result to cod biology: long-lived iteroparous adults, cohort memory, variable recruitment, shifting natural mortality, prey limitation, predation, and changing reference points.

You can say this without overclaiming:

> “Persistence should not be interpreted as biological stasis or as evidence that density dependence is absent. It is better interpreted as a demographic and observation benchmark: adult biomass integrates surviving cohorts, assessment reconstructions impose temporal smoothing, and the scored windows contain strong regime shifts that a constant-productivity model cannot track.”

This framing turns a negative forecasting result into an ecological diagnosis.

## 2. Ecological insights already latent in the results

These are insights that the current evidence supports if worded carefully.

---

### 2.1 Persistence’s dominance is ecological information, not merely a statistical embarrassment

Last-value persistence is often treated as a trivial baseline. But for a long-lived fish stock, persistence is not ecologically empty. Spawning-stock biomass is a stock of mature individuals with nonzero survival. Unless there is a large mortality shock, a strong recruitment pulse, or a major revision in maturity/weight-at-age, next year’s SSB is strongly constrained by this year’s mature biomass.

Thus persistence is an implicit demographic model:

- adult survival creates inertia;
- mature biomass changes slowly relative to annual fluctuations;
- assessment smoothing adds additional autocorrelation;
- cohort structure makes SSB path-dependent.

The fact that surplus-production models lose to persistence therefore says something ecologically meaningful: the added density-dependent production term is either not identifiable, not stationary, or specified in a way that introduces bias.

**Enhancement:** add a short diagnostic paragraph or table quantifying ecological memory. For example:

- lag-1 autocorrelation of SSB and of SSB increments;
- partial autocorrelation structure;
- estimated effective half-life or persistence of log SSB;
- comparison of autocorrelation in the raw assessment series versus the increments.

This would show that persistence is not winning by accident; it is winning because the reconstructed biological state has strong memory.

---

### 2.2 The collapse is best interpreted as an unobserved vital-rate or observation-regime shock, not as a catch-driven surplus-production response

The paper already has the right ingredients:

- apparent net production is strongly negative in 1991–1993;
- observed decline exceeds recorded catch;
- supplying the catch path does not improve the collapse forecast;
- annual landings make the stock-flow module worse;
- Regular et al. attribute much of the disappearance to elevated natural mortality or unreported removals.

The ecological insight is that the collapse cannot be represented as a simple catch-driven movement along a stationary surplus-production curve. It behaves more like:

- a mortality pulse;
- a productivity regime shift;
- a change in availability/observation;
- or some combination of those processes.

This is important because it separates “catch was present” from “catch explains the forecast failure.” Catch is ecologically relevant but not forecast-sufficient.

**Suggested wording:**

> “The collapse is not interpretable, within this accounting, as a catch-driven transition along a stationary surplus-production surface. The catch path is an endogenous response to depletion and therefore carries information about management and stock status, but it does not supply the missing vital-rate process needed to reproduce the observed decline.”

That is a strong ecological conclusion and is well supported by the paper’s own diagnostics.

---

### 2.3 Constant-productivity surplus models fail because they assume ecological stationarity

The paper tests models with fixed \(r\), fixed \(K\), or fixed catch regimes, but the biological history includes:

- collapse;
- moratorium;
- delayed recovery;
- partial rebuilding;
- post-2015 stall;
- downward revision of the LRP;
- changing capelin state;
- possible increased natural mortality/predation.

That is a nonstationary ecological history. A constant-productivity model is not merely statistically weak; it is ecologically brittle because it assumes that the stock’s productive capacity and compensatory response remain stable across periods that clearly did not remain stable.

**Enhancement:** explicitly connect the forecasting failure to ecological nonstationarity.

> “The ladder’s failure is consistent with a broader ecological interpretation: when productivity, natural mortality, prey availability, or observation relations shift faster than density-dependent compensation can restore biomass, a stationary surplus-production map becomes a hindrance rather than an aid.”

This does not claim density dependence is absent. It says that density dependence is not the dominant forecastable signal in these windows.

---

### 2.4 The prey-module result is about ecological predictability, not ecological irrelevance

The capelin and acoustic-index modules fail. A superficial reading would be: “capelin does not matter.” That would be wrong.

The deeper ecological insight is:

> A variable can be an important ecological driver and still fail as a forecast module if its transmission mechanism, lag structure, and parameter values are not identifiable from the training data.

Capelin may affect cod condition, reproduction, growth, or survival. But the tested module imposed a particular transmission mechanism: contemporaneous or near-contemporaneous multiplicative scaling of surplus production. That may not be the biological pathway.

Possible ecological pathways are different:

- capelin shortage affects cod condition, not immediately SSB;
- capelin affects recruitment with a lag;
- capelin affects adult mortality indirectly;
- capelin interacts with temperature or seal predation;
- the effect may be thresholded rather than smooth;
- the relevant capelin state may be prey availability in a specific area or season.

**Enhancement:** add a paragraph distinguishing mechanism from forecast skill.

> “The non-retention of the prey-informed modules should not be read as evidence that capelin limitation is ecologically unimportant. It shows only that the tested forecast transmission — a short-window, weakly identified scaling of surplus production by an acoustic prey index — did not provide predictive skill beyond persistence.”

That distinction is scientifically important and protects the paper from an ecological overclaim.

---

### 2.5 The Allee parameter result is an identification boundary, not evidence against depensation

The paper already handles this cautiously, but the ecological implication can be sharpened.

Depensation in fish populations is often a low-density phenomenon expressed through:

- reduced spawning success;
- altered mate finding;
- reduced reproductive timing or spatial aggregation;
- changed vulnerability to predators;
- cohort failure at very low adult abundance;
- altered survey availability.

Those processes are not necessarily well represented by a multiplicative Allee term in an aggregate surplus-production equation. Moreover, the recovery-window data may not sample the relevant low-state dynamics well enough to identify a threshold.

So the ecological insight is:

> The boundary estimate of \(\mathfrak{s}\) tells us that the scored data and model form cannot locate a depensation threshold. It does not tell us that depensation is absent.

**Suggested wording:**

> “The Allee parameter is better interpreted as a sampling and identification boundary than as biological evidence. Low-density depensation, if present, is a property of vital rates near the low state — especially recruitment and survival — and cannot be diagnosed from a short aggregate biomass-increment fit dominated by regime shifts.”

This is a genuinely ecological interpretation and avoids the common mistake of treating a boundary estimate as absence of effect.

---

### 2.6 The change in reference points is itself an ecological signal

The paper mentions that the old LRP is 884.6 kt and the new one is 276 kt. That is not just an assessment-technical detail. It is an ecological signal: the historical productivity regime that supported the older safe set may no longer describe the stock’s current capacity or the assessment’s understanding of it.

This is important because fixed historical reference points can become ecologically obsolete after regime shifts.

**Enhancement:** add a short discussion of reference-point nonstationarity.

> “The divergence between the historical and revised reference points is not merely a normative change. It indicates that the productivity regime used to define the historical safe set may no longer represent the stock’s realized ecological capacity. Fixed reference points can therefore make threshold diagnostics degenerate and can misrepresent rebuilding potential if the underlying productivity has shifted.”

This is a valuable ecological insight that follows directly from the two-specification design.

### 3.1 Add an ecological memory diagnostic

**Purpose:** show that persistence is winning because of biological/assessment memory, not because the models were arbitrarily weak.

**Implementation:**

Report, for each specification:

- lag-1 autocorrelation of SSB;
- lag-1 autocorrelation of \(\Delta SSB\);
- lag-1 autocorrelation of log SSB;
- perhaps partial autocorrelations up to lag 5;
- an estimated AR(1) coefficient for log SSB, with confidence interval if desired;
- the implied half-life of shocks, if interpretable.

**Ecological interpretation:**

A high lag-1 autocorrelation in SSB is expected for a long-lived stock and for an assessment smoother. It explains why last-value persistence is a strong benchmark. It also shows that the surplus-production ladder is being tested against a biologically informed memory effect, not against a meaningless straw man.

**Where to place it:**

Section 3.1 or Section 4, preferably as a short diagnostic table.

### 3.2 Add a cohort- or age-structured ecological benchmark

This is one of the most substantive enhancements possible.

The current baselines are statistical: persistence and training mean. But the ecologically natural null model for a fish stock is not merely “next year equals this year.” It is:

> current cohorts survive, age, and contribute to future spawning biomass, while recruitment is drawn from some historical expectation.

If age composition is available from the assessment or publications, you could construct a simple age-structured projection benchmark.

A minimal version:

\[
\hat S_{t+1}
=
\sum_a N_{a,t} e^{-Z_{a,t}} w_{a+1} m_{a+1}
+
\hat R_t w_0 m_0,
\]

where:

- \(N_{a,t}\) is numbers at age;
- \(Z_{a,t}\) is total mortality, or a declared approximation such as natural mortality in low-catch periods;
- \(w_a\) is weight at age;
- \(m_a\) is maturity at age;
- \(\hat R_t\) is expected recruitment, perhaps the training-window mean or a simple stock-recruitment mean.

For \(h=5\), iterate the cohort projection with training-window mean recruitment or a declared recruitment rule.

**Why this matters ecologically:**

If a simple cohort benchmark beats the surplus-production ladder, the interpretation becomes:

> age structure, not biomass surplus, is the ecologically relevant structure for forecasting this stock.

If the cohort benchmark also loses to persistence, the interpretation becomes stronger:

> even biologically natural demographic structure does not overcome nonstationarity and assessment smoothing in these windows.

Either outcome is scientifically valuable.

**Caveat:**

This may require assessment numbers-at-age, weight-at-age, and maturity-at-age. If those are public, the enhancement is feasible. If not, it can be proposed as a future ecological extension.

### 3.3 Decompose forecast error into ecological components

RMSE tells you the ladder failed, but not which ecological process failed. A post hoc diagnostic decomposition would add substantial value.

For each origin or test window, decompose realized SSB change into components such as:

- recruitment contribution;
- survival and growth of existing mature fish;
- changes in maturity-at-age or weight-at-age;
- catch removals;
- residual natural mortality/productivity/observation revision.

A simplified accounting identity is:

\[
SSB_{t+1} - SSB_t
=
\text{recruitment contribution}
+
\text{survival/growth contribution}
+
\text{maturity/weight contribution}
-
\text{catch contribution}
-
\text{natural mortality contribution}
+
\text{observation/assessment revision}.
\]

Not all terms may be cleanly separable from public assessment outputs, but even a partial decomposition would be useful.

**Ecological payoff:**

You could say things like:

- “The collapse-window error is dominated by unexplained mortality/productivity loss, not by misforecast recruitment.”
- “The recovery-window error is dominated by unexpected recruitment or survival gains that the constant-productivity models could not anticipate.”
- “The post-2015 stall is associated with negative residual production rather than catch error.”

This turns a model comparison into an ecological diagnosis.

**Where to place it:**

Section 3.2 already has apparent net production. Expand that into a small diagnostic table or figure.

### 3.5 Test time-varying productivity as an explicit ecological hypothesis

The paper’s current models mostly assume constant productivity. The ecological history suggests productivity shifts. A time-varying productivity module would directly test that hypothesis.

Possible implementations, ordered by increasing complexity:

1. **Predeclared regime breaks**  
   Use ecologically motivated breaks only, such as:
   - 1991 capelin collapse;
   - 1995 post-moratorium low state;
   - 2015 rebuilding stall.

2. **Training-window-only changepoint detection**  
   Detect a break in apparent productivity using only training data, then freeze the regime for forecasting.

3. **Random-walk productivity**  
   Let \(r_t\) or log productivity follow a random walk, with strict estimation constraints and declared bounds.

4. **Regime-specific carrying capacity**  
   Allow \(K\) to differ before and after an ecological break, again with training-only estimation.

**Important:** the module must be forecast-valid. It cannot use future information to choose the regime.

**Ecological payoff:**

If time-varying productivity improves forecasts, the paper can say:

> nonstationarity is not only a limitation; it is the missing ecological structure.

If it does not improve forecasts, the paper can say:

> even allowing regime shifts does not recover skill, which strengthens the conclusion that short-window biomass forecasts for this stock are dominated by persistence and assessment memory.

Either result is valuable.

### 3.6 Re-specify prey modules with biologically motivated lags and transmission pathways

The current prey module scales surplus production by an index. Ecologically, that is only one possible transmission mechanism, and perhaps not the most plausible.

Capelin could affect cod through:

- condition and growth;
- recruitment survival;
- spawning success;
- natural mortality;
- spatial overlap;
- prey availability to juvenile cod;
- indirect effects through predator-prey dynamics with seals.

These effects are likely lagged.

**Enhancement:** test alternative ecologically motivated modules such as:

1. **Lagged capelin effect on surplus production**

\[
g_t = r S_t \left(1 - \frac{S_t}{K}\right) f(I^{cap}_{t-\ell}),
\]

with \(\ell\) predeclared, for example 1, 2, or 3 years.

2. **Lagged capelin effect on recruitment**

If recruitment can be obtained from the assessment:

\[
R_t = f(S_t, I^{cap}_{t-\ell}),
\]

then forecast SSB using cohort survival.

3. **Threshold prey effect**

Capelin limitation may operate only below some prey threshold. A binary regime or saturating function may be more plausible than a smooth power law.

4. **Condition-mediated effect**

Use capelin to forecast condition or weight-at-age, then translate that into SSB. This requires more data but is ecologically more realistic.

**Key principle:**

The paper should not ask, “Does capelin scale surplus production?” It should ask, “Does the available capelin information provide a stable, identifiable, lead-lag ecological signal that improves future SSB forecasts?”

That is a much better ecological question.

### 3.7 Add predation/natural-mortality modules as disturbance hypotheses

The paper mentions elevated natural mortality and possible seal predation. This could be treated as an ecological disturbance module rather than a productivity scaling module.

For example:

- use harp seal abundance or an index of predation pressure;
- allow a lagged effect on apparent mortality;
- treat the effect as additive or multiplicative mortality;
- test whether it improves forecasts beyond persistence.

A simple form would be:

\[
S_{t+1}
=
S_t + g(S_t) - C_t - M^{extra}_t,
\]

where \(M^{extra}_t\) is a function of a lagged predator index.

**Caveats:**

- identification will be weak;
- predator abundance may be endogenous to ecosystem state;
- collinearity with capelin/temperature may be severe;
- the module must use only information available at the forecast origin.

But even a declared negative result would be ecologically informative: it would show that plausible top-down forcing is not forecastable in this implementation.

### 3.8 Move depensation analysis from surplus production to recruitment or survival

The current Allee term acts on surplus production. But depensation in fish stocks is often expressed through recruitment failure or reduced survival at low stock size.

If assessment recruitment estimates are available, a more biologically appropriate enhancement would be to test depensation in the stock-recruitment relation:

\[
R_t = f(S_t),
\]

where \(f\) allows a threshold or low-stock depensation term.

Examples:

- Beverton-Holt with depensation;
- Ricker with depensation;
- threshold recruitment function;
- piecewise-linear recruitment failure below a biomass level.

Then combine the recruitment model with a cohort projection to forecast SSB.

**Why this is better ecologically:**

It tests the biological process where depensation is most likely to occur: production of new recruits, not an aggregate biomass increment.

**Interpretation:**

If recruitment depensation is also unidentified, the paper can say:

> the data do not resolve low-density biological processes.

If recruitment depensation is identifiable and improves forecasts, the paper can say:

> the correct structure was not surplus-production depensation but recruitment depensation.

Both are meaningful ecological conclusions.

### 3.9 Separate ecological memory from assessment smoothing

A key interpretive issue is whether persistence wins because of biological memory or because the assessment reconstruction is smoothed.

The current paper acknowledges this, but an enhancement could make it sharper.

Possible approaches:

1. **Compare forecast skill on raw survey indices versus assessment SSB**  
   If an independent or semi-independent survey index is available, test whether persistence still dominates.

2. **Use assessment uncertainty draws**  
   If assessment bootstrap or posterior samples are available, score forecasts across multiple reconstructed SSB realizations.

3. **Fit an observation-error state-space version**  
   Separate process error from observation error, at least as a sensitivity analysis.

4. **Simulate retrospective revision error**  
   If real-time vintages are unavailable, impose plausible revision structures and test whether persistence still wins.

**Ecological payoff:**

If persistence dominance disappears when the smoother is weakened, the conclusion becomes:

> the result is partly an artifact of forecasting a reconstructed state.

If persistence dominance remains, the conclusion becomes stronger:

> even after accounting for observation smoothing, the ecological signal available for short-horizon forecasts is weak relative to demographic memory.

### 3.10 Add ecologically meaningful threshold metrics

The Brier score on Specification A is degenerate because the historical LRP lies above all origin states. That is not an ecological failure of the paper; it is a signal that the threshold is poorly matched to the tested forecast problem.

More ecologically meaningful threshold diagnostics could include:

1. **Further decline risk**

\[
\Pr(S_{t+h} < S_t)
\]

or

\[
\Pr(S_{t+h} < 0.8 S_t).
\]

2. **Risk of falling below recent low state**

\[
\Pr(S_{t+h} < \min_{u \in \text{recent training}} S_u).
\]

3. **Risk relative to a time-varying reference point**

For example, a rolling lower quantile of recent biomass rather than a fixed 1980s mean.

4. **Rebuilding risk**

Probability that biomass remains below a rebuilding threshold for the next \(h\) years.

5. **Expected ecological loss**

Use asymmetric loss that penalizes overestimating biomass near a dangerous zone more heavily than underestimating it.

If forecasts remain deterministic, you can still create predictive intervals from residual bootstraps and score:

- interval coverage;
- continuous ranked probability score;
- probability mass below ecologically relevant thresholds.

**Why this is nondecorative:**

It changes the management/ecological relevance of the paper. The question becomes not only “Which point forecast has lower RMSE?” but “Which forecast better represents ecological risk?”

### 3.11 Add spatial, condition, and maturity diagnostics

Northern cod dynamics are not only total biomass dynamics. Important ecological state variables include:

- spatial distribution;
- survey occupancy;
- center of gravity;
- condition factor;
- mean weight-at-age;
- maturity-at-age;
- age-at-maturity shifts;
- proportion of older spawners;
- age diversity among spawners.

These variables can affect both true productivity and survey observability.

**Enhancement:**

Add a diagnostic subsection or supplementary analysis showing whether forecast errors correlate with changes in:

- mean weight-at-age;
- maturity schedule;
- spatial center of gravity;
- survey catchability proxies;
- proportion of older mature fish.

Even if not used as forecast modules, these diagnostics would help interpret why surplus-production models failed.

For example:

- if maturity-at-age changed, SSB revisions may not reflect abundance alone;
- if distribution shifted, survey availability may have changed;
- if older spawners disappeared, reproductive resilience may have declined;
- if condition deteriorated, surplus production may have fallen independent of biomass.

This would add ecological depth without requiring a full ecosystem model.

### 3.12 Add horizon-specific ecological interpretation

The paper scores \(h=1\) and \(h=5\), but the ecological processes dominating those horizons differ.

For \(h=1\):

- adult survival dominates;
- existing mature biomass dominates;
- assessment smoothing is strong;
- persistence is hard to beat.

For \(h=5\):

- recruitment dominates;
- cohort turnover matters;
- productivity regime matters;
- natural mortality shocks matter;
- maturity schedules matter.

The ladder uses the same basic surplus-production structure for both horizons. An ecological enhancement would be to interpret horizon-dependent failure.

**Suggested wording:**

> “At the one-year horizon, forecast skill is dominated by the survival and assessment memory of existing mature biomass. At the five-year horizon, forecast error is increasingly governed by recruitment, productivity regime, and mortality processes that the constant-parameter surplus models cannot anticipate.”

This is simple but ecologically important.

### 3.13 Distinguish forecast skill from management usefulness

The paper is careful to say it is not an operational forecast test. That is good. But an ecological enhancement would be to explain why management relevance requires a different design.

Catch is endogenous. Management changes catch in response to assessment state. Therefore, a hindcast conditional on observed catch cannot evaluate the management value of a model.

A more ecologically and managerially relevant extension would be a management strategy evaluation:

- define a harvest control rule;
- simulate assessment error;
- simulate catch advice;
- project SSB under each model;
- score outcomes by probability of being above reference point, catch stability, rebuilding time, or expected loss.

This would test whether a model that loses RMSE might still improve management decisions.

**Suggested wording:**

> “Because catch is managed in response to perceived stock status, forecast RMSE conditional on realized catch is not equivalent to management value. A closed-loop simulation would be required to determine whether any of the structural modules improves management outcomes despite failing the point-forecast retention rule.”

This is a serious ecological/management enhancement and could be stated as future work.

### 3.14 Add a cross-stock ecological generalization, carefully

The paper is deliberately scoped to Northern cod. But the ecological question is general:

> After collapse and regime shift, do simple surplus-production models forecast biomass better than persistence?

You could add a short paragraph proposing that this design be applied to other collapsed and recovering stocks.

Candidate features for cross-stock comparison:

- collapse magnitude;
- time since collapse;
- generation time;
- age truncation;
- trophic level;
- prey dependence;
- predation pressure;
- environmental variability;
- assessment smoothing;
- reference-point revisions.

This would frame the Northern cod result as a case study in a broader ecological problem: forecastability after regime shifts.

**Important:** do not claim generality from one stock. But you can propose a hypothesis:

> “If persistence dominance is common after large productivity shocks, that would suggest that forecastable biomass memory, not stationary density dependence, is the primary short-horizon signal in collapsed stocks.”

That is a meaningful ecological research direction.

### 4.1 Paragraph on persistence as ecological memory

Insert in Section 4, near the discussion of assessment smoothing.

> The superiority of persistence should not be read as evidence that Northern cod dynamics are static or that density dependence is absent. Spawning-stock biomass is a stock of mature individuals with nonzero survival, and its year-to-year path is strongly constrained by cohort memory. The observed decline exceeded recorded removals, apparent net production became strongly negative, and supplying the catch path did not improve the forecast. Within this accounting, the collapse therefore behaves like an unobserved vital-rate or observation-regime shock: a mortality pulse, a productivity shift, a change in availability, or a combination of those processes. That interpretation does not identify the mechanism, but it constrains the class of explanations that this forecast design can support.

---

### 4.3 Paragraph on prey drivers versus forecastability

Insert in Section 3.4 or Section 4.

> The prey-informed results separate ecological mechanism from forecast skill. A capelin collapse is consistent with reduced cod productivity, and independent reconstruction-level analyses support that reading. But a usable forecast module requires more than a plausible driver: it requires a stable transmission mechanism, a defensible lag structure, and enough training information to identify the relation without overfitting. The tested modules imposed short-window multiplicative scaling of surplus production and therefore conflated a plausible ecological driver with an unvalidated forecast transmission. The non-retention of those modules should therefore be read as a forecast-identification result, not as evidence that prey limitation is ecologically unimportant.

---

### 4.4 Paragraph on depensation and identification

Insert near the Allee discussion.

> The boundary estimate of the Allee parameter is an identification result, not a biological verdict. Depensation at low stock size would be expressed through low-state recruitment, survival, spawning success, or availability, not necessarily through a multiplicative term in aggregate biomass surplus. Because the scored windows provide little information about the shape of vital rates near the low state, the data cannot locate a threshold. The appropriate ecological conclusion is therefore not that depensation is absent, but that this implementation cannot diagnose it.

---

### 4.5 Paragraph on reference-point drift and ecological nonstationarity

Insert near the two-specification comparison.

> The divergence between the historical and revised limit reference points is not merely a technical assessment change. It signals that the productivity regime underlying the historical safe set may no longer represent the stock’s realized ecological capacity. Fixed reference points can therefore make threshold diagnostics degenerate and can misrepresent rebuilding potential if the ecological baseline has shifted. The two-specification design is useful precisely because it exposes this drift without pooling the two biological narratives.

---

## 5. Analyses that would be especially valuable but require care

These are not mandatory, but they would substantially increase the ecological depth.

---

### 5.1 State-space surplus production with observation error

The current approach treats reconstructed SSB as the state. But assessment SSB is an estimated latent variable. A state-space version would distinguish:

- process variability in true biomass;
- observation error in the assessment;
- smoothing induced by the assessment model.

This would help answer:

> Are we forecasting ecology, or are we forecasting the assessment filter?

If feasible, this would be one of the strongest ecological upgrades.

---

### 5.2 Recruitment-based forecast diagnostics

Since five-year forecasts depend heavily on recruitment, the paper could report how well each model anticipates recruitment-driven changes.

For example:

- compare forecast error in years with unusually high or low recruitment;
- test whether forecast failure correlates with recruitment surprises;
- report a recruitment-conditioned version of the h=5 score.

This would connect the biomass forecast failure to a core ecological process.

---

### 5.3 Mortality-pulse diagnostics

The paper already has apparent net production. It could be extended to identify candidate mortality-pulse years:

- years where apparent production is strongly negative;
- years where apparent production is strongly positive;
- periods where catch explains little of the change.

Then compare model performance conditional on those periods.

This would show that the ladder fails specifically during ecological disturbances, not uniformly everywhere.

---

### 5.4 Ecological forecast intervals and risk communication

Point RMSE is symmetric, but ecological risk is not. Overestimating biomass near a dangerous zone is more consequential than underestimating it.

A risk-oriented enhancement would be to produce predictive intervals and evaluate:

- probability of being below a threshold;
- expected shortfall below a threshold;
- asymmetric zone-based loss;
- probability of advising the wrong management zone.

This would make the ecological and management relevance much clearer.

---

## 6. What not to claim

To keep the ecological insights genuine, the paper should avoid the following overclaims.

---

### 6.1 Do not claim density dependence is absent

The result shows that constant-parameter surplus-production models do not forecast better than persistence in these windows. It does not show that density dependence is ecologically absent.

Better:

> “Stationary surplus-production structure did not provide forecast skill beyond persistence in these windows.”

---

### 6.2 Do not claim capelin is unimportant

The prey modules failed as forecast modules. That is not the same as saying capelin is not an ecological driver.

Better:

> “The tested capelin-informed modules did not provide forecast skill beyond persistence.”

---

### 6.3 Do not claim Allee effects are absent

The Allee parameter is unidentified or weakly identified. That is not evidence against depensation.

Better:

> “The data and model form do not identify an Allee threshold.”

---

### 6.4 Do not interpret persistence as biological equilibrium

Persistence is a forecast rule, not a biological claim that the stock should remain unchanged.

Better:

> “Persistence is retained as the scoring benchmark because it represents the demographic memory available in the reconstructed state.”

---

### 6.5 Do not claim operational forecast value

The design is a conditional hindcast with supplied catch and retrospective assessment states.

Better:

> “The comparison measures retrospective forecast skill for a reconstructed assessment series, not real-time operational forecast skill.”

---

## 7. Priority list

If you want the highest ecological return for the least disruption to the paper, I would prioritize these five enhancements:

1. **Add ecological memory diagnostics** to explain why persistence is strong.
2. **Expand the apparent-production diagnostic into an ecological periodization** of collapse, recovery, and stall.
3. **Add explicit language distinguishing ecological mechanism from forecast skill**, especially for capelin and depensation.
4. **Add reference-point nonstationarity as an ecological insight**, not just a specification difference.
5. **Propose age/cohort-structured benchmarks and time-varying productivity as future ecological extensions**, even if not fully implemented.

If you want a more ambitious but still feasible upgrade, add:

6. **Age/cohort-structured naive benchmark**, if age data are public.
7. **Forecast error decomposition into recruitment, survival, mortality, and observation components**.
8. **Ecological risk metrics** instead of relying mainly on degenerate LRP Brier scores.
9. **Lagged prey/predator modules** with biologically motivated transmission pathways.
10. **Observation-error or vintage-based sensitivity analysis** to separate assessment smoothing from ecological memory.

---

## 8. The single most important ecological reframing

If only one enhancement is adopted, it should be this:

> Reframe the paper as a test of whether stationary biomass-production structure can extract forecastable ecological signal from a stock whose recent history is dominated by demographic memory, assessment smoothing, and nonstationary vital rates.

That reframing makes the negative result scientifically valuable. It says:

- persistence wins because of cohort memory and assessment smoothing;
- surplus-production models fail because they impose stationarity on a nonstationary ecological history;
- prey and depensation processes may still be real, but they are not forecastable under the tested implementations;
- reference-point drift is itself evidence of ecological regime change;
- the result is not anti-structure, but a precise statement about which ecological structure is identifiable and forecastable in this stock.
