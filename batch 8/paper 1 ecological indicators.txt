## 3. Make Non-Compensatory Aggregation a First-Class Methodological Contribution

The journal has published explicit methodological arguments for non-compensatory or “one-out-all-out” aggregation in ecological assessment. The paper’s typed operator \(E_{\mathrm{typ}}\) is precisely a formalization of non-compensatory assessment, but the manuscript positions it primarily as a mathematical object (the intersection over the weight cone) rather than as a **methodological proposal for indicator construction.**

- **Formalize the non-compensatory operator as a design principle for ecological indicators.** The typed operator is not just a doctrinal stance; it is an *assessment operator* with precise mathematical properties (Proposition 3: localization, hierarchy). Argue that where separately-binding floors exist—biomass limits, critical habitat thresholds, safe operating spaces—the non-compensatory operator is the *indication-theoretically correct* choice, because it alone detects path-wise breaches. ## Overall assessment

The manuscript contains a potentially publishable theoretical contribution, but it is not yet optimally configured for *Ecological Indicators*. Its strongest result is not simply that weighted aggregation can mask component failures. The more precise contribution is:

> A full-cone scalarization is pointwise lossless, yet a certification protocol based on \(\forall w\,\exists a_w\) can be dynamically unsound for separately binding floors because the action selected may change with the weighting. The failure is therefore generated by the interaction between aggregation semantics, policy-menu geometry, and path-wise assessment.

That is a stronger and more original claim than the conventional statement that “composite indices can hide poor component performance.” The manuscript should make this distinction the organizing principle.

At present, however, the paper sometimes moves too quickly between four different objects:

1. a composite indicator evaluated at a fixed trajectory;
2. an assessment operator that evaluates whether an action is admissible;
3. a certification protocol that permits the action to depend on \(w\);
4. a fishery model said to realize the abstract datum.

These should be separated rigorously. The central mathematical result is soundly motivated, but the indicator-theoretic and ecological claims need tighter qualifications.

# 1. Highest-priority revisions

## 1.1 Recast the contribution around indicator validity, but do not overstate what the theorem proves

The manuscript currently frames the result primarily as a separation between scalarized and coordinate-wise feasibility. For *Ecological Indicators*, the central question should instead be:

> Under what assessment semantics does a composite indicator provide a valid signal of path-wise transition safety?

The distinction should be made explicit:

- **Pointwise indicator validity:** for a fixed trajectory, \(w\cdot s(t)\ge 0\) for all \(w\in W_+\) is equivalent to \(s_i(t)\ge 0\) for every \(i\).
- **Policy-certification validity:** the existence of a safe action for every \(w\), \(\forall w\,\exists a_w\), does not imply the existence of one action safe for every floor, \(\exists a\,\forall w\).
- **Operational indicator failure:** the failure occurs when the assessment protocol treats the former as sufficient for approving a transition.

This distinction is already present in Remark 2 and Proposition 3, but it needs to become the conceptual center of Sections 1, 5, and 7.

### Suggested replacement paragraph for the Introduction

> Composite indicators are often criticized because compensation among components can conceal deterioration in one component. The present paper identifies a narrower and more consequential failure mode. With the full nonnegative weight cone, a weighted aggregate is lossless for any fixed trajectory: nonnegativity of every weighted sum is equivalent to nonnegativity of each component. The problem arises one level later, during policy certification. If the assessment protocol asks whether each weighting admits some action, \(\forall w\,\exists a_w\), the action may vary with the weighting. This condition is weaker than requiring one transition to satisfy all separately binding floors, \(\exists a\,\forall w\). Thus, the relevant failure is not static information loss caused by an unfortunate weight vector; it is a mismatch between the quantifier structure of indicator-based certification and the commitment structure of transition management.

This is more precise than saying that “the aggregate alone cannot tell” in all circumstances.

## 1.2 Clarify that the practical failure concerns a certification protocol, not every use of a composite index

Several claims are currently too broad:

- “the aggregate alone cannot tell the rescuable from the impossible”;
- “aggregate indices alone cannot certify noncompensatory transition safety”;
- “the dashboard remains certified while the biomass floor is breached.”

These are valid only under the manuscript’s specific protocol:

1. a family of weights is considered;
2. each weight may select a different plan;
3. the plan is accepted if it satisfies the aggregate along its own path;
4. the reviewer does not require a common plan or separately report the component floors.

A practitioner using a fixed, predeclared weight and reporting all component values may not be making the same error. The manuscript should distinguish:

- **single-weight evaluation**;
- **weight-robust evaluation**;
- **weight-dependent plan selection**;
- **common-plan evaluation**.

Add a four-row table early in the paper:

| Assessment protocol | Mathematical form | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed-weight plan assessment | \(E_w(z)\) | One declared index and one selected plan |
| Weight-robust acceptance | \(\forall w\,\exists a_w\) | Every weighting can be paired with some plan |
| Common-plan robustness | \(\exists a\,\forall w\) | One plan works for all weightings |
| Coordinate-wise assessment | \(\exists a\) satisfying \(s_i(t)\ge0\ \forall i,t\) | Separately binding floors |

This table would prevent reviewers from interpreting the paper as an indiscriminate attack on composite indicators.

## 1.3 Make the non-compensatory operator a conditional design principle, not the universally “correct” indicator

The proposed suggestion that \(E_{\mathrm{typ}}\) is “the indication-theoretically correct choice” is too strong. The mathematics establishes that it is the correct operator **if the floors are genuinely separately binding**. It does not establish that biomass, income, liability coverage, or every ecological indicator must be treated non-compensatorily.

Use the following conditional formulation:

> The coordinate-wise operator is appropriate when failure of any component is unacceptable, irreversible, legally binding, or not substitutable through an identified physical or institutional mechanism. A compensatory operator is defensible when substitution is empirically supported, institutionally implementable, and consistent with the management objective.

This should be added to the doctrinal subsection and the conclusions. The distinction matters because *Ecological Indicators* will expect a methodological contribution, not a normative declaration that strong sustainability is always preferable.

## 1.4 Revise the finite-menu theorem to distinguish static margin geometry from dynamic tube geometry

Theorem 6 is important, but its domain needs to be stated more sharply. It applies when each plan has a state-independent required-margin vector \(d_a\) and when admissibility is exactly

\[
d_a\le s.
\]

That is not automatically true for a dynamic robust-control problem. In general, required margins may depend on:

- the initial state;
- the disturbance set;
- the horizon;
- the destination condition;
- the order in which actions are applied;
- nonlinear state dynamics;
- correlations among disturbances.

The current wording—“a general geometric result characterizes the gap for any finite menu of plans”—is too broad. It is general only for the **required-margin class**.

### Suggested revised statement

> For finite plan menus whose exact-tube admissibility can be reduced to componentwise comparison with a required-margin vector, the scalarized accepted set is the upper image of the convex hull of those vectors, whereas the coordinate-wise accepted set is the union of their upper orthants. The result is therefore a geometric characterization of a specified margin-reducible class, not of arbitrary nonlinear transition systems.

Also revise the title of Theorem 6 to:

> **Theorem 6. Finite-menu characterization for margin-reducible plans**

This would eliminate a likely reviewer objection.

## 1.5 Qualify Theorem 9: convexification closes the gap only under a genuine convexity/affinity assumption

The manuscript correctly distinguishes continuous blending from discrete time-sharing. However, the statement that the blend tube is “the pointwise convex combination” of the primitive tubes requires strong assumptions.

It requires, at minimum:

- an affine control-to-trajectory map, or a validated relaxed-control construction;
- compatible disturbance semantics;
- convexity of the safe set;
- a successor map that preserves the required convexity;
- no nonlinear state interaction that changes the blended trajectory.

The manuscript itself later embeds the witness in a nonlinear Schaefer model. That creates a risk that readers will mistakenly transfer Theorem 9 to the fishery benchmark.

Add a prominent limitation immediately before Theorem 9:

> The blend-collapse result is a theorem about the abstract witness datum and its explicitly defined convexified control map. It does not follow for an arbitrary nonlinear ecological model merely because two primitive plans are available. In a nonlinear model, the trajectory generated by a convex combination of controls need not equal the convex combination of the primitive trajectories. Convexification must therefore be demonstrated from the model equations or treated as an additional management assumption.

Theorem 9 should then be presented as a result about **menu convexification**, not as a general result about “mixing plans.”

2. **Common biomass shock:** the same heatwave affects all plans’ biomass trajectories;

- \(\mathcal V_{\mathrm{weak}}\setminus\mathcal V_{\mathrm{typ}}\) is nonempty;
- the gap has positive measure or relative interior;

| Disturbance structure | FAST | SLOW | STAGED | Gap |

# 3. Specific problems in the current proposed enhancement suggestions

The suggestions supplied with the manuscript are directionally correct but should be improved as follows.

## 3.1 “The typed operator is the indication-theoretically correct choice”

Revise to:

> The typed operator is the appropriate design when failure of any floor is unacceptable or non-substitutable. The paper should provide a decision rule for identifying such floors rather than asserting that non-compensatory aggregation is generally superior.

Add criteria:

- legal or regulatory binding;
- ecological irreversibility;
- threshold or tipping-point behavior;
- lack of verified substitutability;
- asymmetric loss from failure;
- inability to compensate after a breach;
- stakeholder agreement that the floor is a constraint rather than a preference.

This turns a normative claim into an indicator-design contribution.

## 3.2 “Convexification makes the dashboard trustworthy”

Revise to:

> Convexification removes the particular finite-menu gap only when the blended control is physically feasible, administratively implementable, and dynamically validated. It does not make the aggregate intrinsically trustworthy.

The relevant management question is not merely whether fractional allocation is mathematically possible, but whether:

- effort can actually be divided continuously;
- quota or seasonal controls can be implemented at the required resolution;
- the blend remains feasible under nonlinear dynamics;
- the disturbance is common or action-specific;
- the blended policy has a well-defined monitoring and enforcement mechanism.

## 3.3 “Per-floor reporting is a detection requirement”

This is one of the strongest practical messages, but qualify it:

> Per-floor reporting is a detection requirement when separately binding floors are part of the management objective and the certification protocol permits plan adaptation across weights. It is not necessarily required for every composite-index application.

The paper should distinguish **reporting** from **decision logic**. Merely displaying the components does not solve the problem if the approval rule still uses \(\forall w\,\exists a_w\).

## 3.4 “The aggregate remains green while the indicated system breaches”

Use “the aggregate remains nonnegative” rather than “green” unless the manuscript defines a traffic-light classification. “Green” introduces an unmodeled classification threshold and may sound rhetorical.

Composite ecological indicators are frequently used to certify sustainability transitions by aggregating multiple performance margins into a weighted sum. \end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}
Composite indicators are routinely criticized because compensation among components can conceal deterioration in a critical subsystem. The present paper identifies a narrower, more consequential, and purely dynamic failure mode. With the full nonnegative weight cone, a weighted aggregate is lossless for any fixed trajectory: nonnegativity of every weighted sum is mathematically equivalent to nonnegativity of each component. The problem arises one level later, during policy certification. 

If the assessment protocol asks whether each weighting admits \emph{some} action ($\forall w\,\exists a_w$), the selected action may vary with the weighting. This condition is strictly weaker than requiring \emph{one} transition to satisfy all separately binding floors ($\exists a\,\forall w$). Thus, the relevant failure is not static information loss caused by an unfortunate weight vector; it is a mismatch between the quantifier structure of indicator-based certification and the commitment structure of transition management.

To isolate this mechanism, we must distinguish between the indicator itself and the certification protocol applied to it. Table~\ref{tab:protocols} formalizes four distinct assessment protocols. The separation analyzed in this paper occurs specifically between Protocol 2 (weight-adaptive certification) and Protocol 4 (coordinate-wise, non-compensatory certification).

\textbf{Theorem 6 (Finite-menu characterization for margin-reducible plans).} \emph{Let a finite menu $\mathcal{A}$ of plans be given. Suppose the exact-tube admissibility of each plan can be reduced to componentwise comparison with a state-independent required-margin vector $d_a \in \mathbb{R}^n_+$ (i.e., plan $a$ is admissible at floor margins $s \ge 0$ exactly when $d_a \le s$). Write $\mathcal{D} = \{d_a : a \in \mathcal{A}\}$. Then:}

\emph{(i) Coordinate-wise acceptance (Protocol 4) is the union of upper orthants:}
\[
\{ s : \exists a \in \mathcal{A},\; d_a \le s \} = \bigcup_{a \in \mathcal{A}} (d_a + \mathbb{R}^n_+);
\]
\emph{(ii) Scalarized per-weight acceptance (Protocol 2) is the upper convex image:}
\[
\{ s : \forall w \in \mathbb{R}^n_+ \setminus \{0\} \;\; \exists a \in \mathcal{A},\; w \cdot d_a \le w \cdot s \} = \operatorname{conv}(\mathcal{D}) + \mathbb{R}^n_+;
\]
\emph{hence the acceptance gap is the convexified indicator performance that is not achievable by any available discrete plan.}

\emph{Remark.} This geometric characterization applies strictly to the margin-reducible class. For general nonlinear transition systems where required margins depend on initial states, disturbance correlations, or horizon lengths, the gap geometry may deviate from the convex hull formulation.

\noindent\textbf{Theorem 9 (Blend collapse under menu convexification).} \emph{Assume the control-to-trajectory map is affine (or a validated relaxed-control construction applies) and the safe set is convex. \[
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{typ}}^{\mathrm{blend}} = \{ x \ge 1 \} \cup \{ s_1 + s_2 \ge 2 \} = \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{weak}}.
\]
\emph{Convexification removes the finite-menu gap only when the blended control is physically feasible and dynamically validated. In nonlinear ecological models, blending controls does not automatically blend trajectories; convexification must therefore be demonstrated from the model equations.}

\section{Indicator Validity Under Transition Assessment}\label{indicator-validity}

The separation theorem implies that composite indicators can be pointwise lossless yet dynamically unsound as transition-certification devices. We formalize this into four design tests for indicator validity, followed by a practitioner audit checklist.

\subsection{Four Tests for Indicator Validity}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Static Losslessness:} Does the aggregate indicator $w \cdot s$ preserve component violations for the declared weight family $W$? If $W$ is restricted, characterize the specific component violations the index is structurally blind to.
    \item \textbf{Protocol Quantifiers:} Does the certification rule require a common plan ($\exists a \forall w$) or permit weight-adaptive plan selection ($\forall w \exists a_w$)? If the latter, the indicator is vulnerable to the quantifier-order gap.
    \item \textbf{Path-wise Observability:} Is the indicator evaluated continuously along the tube, or only at audited endpoints? Endpoint evaluation ($E_{\mathrm{end}}$) cannot detect transient breaches (e.g., extinction vortices) that violate path-wise safety.
    \item \textbf{Menu Completeness and Convexity:} Is the plan menu exhaustive? Are blended (convexified) policies physically and institutionally implementable? If the menu is discrete and non-convex, the indicator's aggregate certification may overstate true system safety.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Practitioner Audit Checklist}
For management agencies using composite dashboards to certify sustainability transitions, the following 10-point audit checklist operationalizes the mathematical results:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Object of Certification:} Are we certifying a static state, an endpoint, or the existence of a path-wise safe management plan?
    \item \textbf{Binding Floors:} Identify which ecological thresholds are legally binding, irreversible, or lack verified substitutability. These require non-compensatory (Protocol 4) treatment.
    \item \textbf{Policy Quantifier:} Explicitly state whether the approval rule assumes a single robust plan or permits the plan to adapt to the chosen weighting.
    \item \textbf{Weight-Dependent Plans:} If weight-adaptive, report the specific management plan associated with each relevant weight range.
    \item \textbf{Path-wise Evaluation:} Ensure indicators are evaluated along the full transition trajectory, not just at annual assessment snapshots.
    \item \textbf{Disturbance Structure:} Specify whether environmental shocks are common to all plans, action-dependent, or correlated across floors.
    \item \textbf{Menu Completeness:} Acknowledge that negative feasibility results are menu-relative unless the action space is demonstrably exhaustive.
    \item \textbf{Blended Policies:} Do not infer convexification from mathematical convenience; verify that fractional effort/quota allocation is administratively implementable.
    \item \textbf{Rescue Margins:} Report the minimum resource increment ($\kappa^*$) required to transition from the menu-relative impossibility region to the rescue set.
    \item \textbf{Mandatory Reporting:} Require that component trajectories and minimum margins be reported alongside the aggregate index to allow independent verification of Protocol 4.
\end{enumerate}

### C. Coupled floors and cascades — *high topical value*
- **Math move:** Let disturbances couple coordinates via a coupling matrix `C`; floors no longer fail independently.
