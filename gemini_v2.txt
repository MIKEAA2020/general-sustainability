\subsection{1. Introduction}\label{introduction}

\subsubsection{1.1 Two failure modes}\label{two-failure-modes}

Sustainability assessment sits on a conceptual fault line that has structured the field since its inception. The weak-sustainability tradition, formalized in the genuine-savings and inclusive-wealth frameworks (World Bank, 2011; Boos, 2015; Neumayer, 2013), treats distinct capital forms as mutually substitutable through shadow prices. Under this view, the depletion of a natural asset is consistent with sustainability as long as the aggregate value of the total capital stock does not decline. The strong-sustainability tradition holds, by contrast, that critical natural assets and ecosystem services are separately binding constraints whose degradation cannot be compensated by accumulation elsewhere, at any price (Daly, 1990; Ekins et al., 2003). In ecological economics, this position is anchored in the thesis of weak comparability: the values at stake in environmental decisions are often incommensurable in a single monetary or scalar metric (Martinez-Alier, Munda, and O'Neill, 1998). This debate is well established in environmental policy and multi-criteria decision analysis (Cinelli, Coles, and Kirwan, 2014; Sch\"ar, Pohl, and Geldermann, 2025; Hanley et al., 1999; Usubiaga-Lia\~no, 2025), and the mathematical theory of sustainability indicators is correspondingly mature (Martinet, 2011; Cairns and Martinet, 2014), notably in maximin formulations where strong sustainability emerges from constraint viability rather than intertemporal optimization (Solow, 1974; Doyen and Gajardo, 2020).

In the dynamical terms of this paper, the two traditions diverge on whether compensatory substitution can physically keep pace with depletion over time. The material-cycle dimension of that distinction—treating waste as an open loop, requiring cycles to close at the rate of throughput, and ruling out substitution against deep-time renewal—is analyzed in a companion study (Abaee, 2026a) and is not re-argued here; no theorem in this paper concerns material cycles directly. Instead, we examine the formal assessment operators defined in Section 3.1 and interpreted doctrinally in Section 5.1.

Here, we focus on how this distinction governs the dynamic certification of sustainability transitions. Our blend-collapse result (Theorem 8) shows that the gap between compensatory and noncompensatory acceptance closes precisely on the region where convexified substitution—the continuous, fractional allocation of control effort—keeps pace with depletion. Conversely, the impossibility region of Theorem 5(4) delineates where substitution fails, even under discrete time-sharing. In our framework, substitution is admitted only through explicit, physically realized control pathways; compensation is never presumed merely because a weighted aggregate index remains non-negative. The broader question of aggregate maintenance—whether total capital can be preserved—turns out to be ambiguous between two distinct quantifier orderings, which we formalize in Remark 1 and Section 4.5.

This paper addresses a question that the indicator literature has left comparatively open: the \emph{dynamic} question. During an active sustainability transition, when can a compensatory aggregate certify a trajectory that a noncompensatory assessment rejects? Two practical failure modes motivate this inquiry.

The first is \emph{commensurability drift}. Transition assessments routinely bundle heterogeneous stocks, service flows, liabilities, and safety thresholds into single composite indices whose underlying substitution rules are rarely stated as explicit mathematics. Composite indices are frequently criticized on this ground: the weights they assign embed subjective value judgments disguised as objective measurement (Hickel, 2020; Martinez-Alier, Munda, and O'Neill, 1998). Yet standard critiques typically focus on the specific \emph{choice} of weights. Here, we establish a more fundamental result: no choice of weights can resolve the underlying tension. The failure is structural—it is embedded in the logic of aggregation itself.

The second failure mode is the tendency to treat conditional findings as unconditional safety certificates. To prevent this, every result below explicitly states its operational assumptions, disturbance bounds, and verification status.

The dynamic masking formalized here operates as follows: a weighted aggregate of two typed service floors can remain strictly non-negative along an entire worst-case trajectory tube, even while each individual floor undergoes a temporary deficit that no single control policy can prevent. This is distinct from the \emph{productivity illusion}—where adequate service delivery conceals a degrading resource base, as analyzed in Abaee (2026a). On the witness system constructed below, the aggregate is defined directly over two service floors, \(s_1\) and \(s_2\). What the aggregate misses is that each floor drops below its threshold mid-interval. This dynamic blind spot constitutes the acceptance gap characterized in Theorem 5(4).

\subsubsection{1.2 The central result}\label{the-central-result}

The paper's organizing result is a failure of quantifiers to commute. Consider a typed transition system: a state space constrained by typed floors \(s_i \ge 0\) (normalized to zero), an action set, a disturbance set, and exact-tube semantics under which a transition is safe only if every state visited along its continuous trajectory—not merely its endpoint—satisfies the constraints. For each non-negative scalarization weight vector \(w\) in the punctured positive orthant \(W_+ = \mathbb{R}^n_+ \setminus \{0\}\), let \(E_w(z)\) denote the set of actions admissible at state \(z\) under the aggregate floor \(w \cdot s \ge 0\), and let \(E_{\mathrm{typ}}(z)\) denote the set of actions admissible when each typed floor \(s_i \ge 0\) is enforced separately. Two distinct acceptance criteria emerge, differing solely in the order of their quantifiers:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Common-plan acceptance (noncompensatory).} A single, unified action is safe under every scalarization weight:
  \[\exists a\; \forall w:\; a \in E_w(z).\]
\item
  \textbf{Per-weight acceptance (compensatory).} For each weight, there exists some safe action, which may vary with the weight:
  \[\forall w\; \exists a_w:\; a_w \in E_w(z).\]
\end{itemize}

Common-plan acceptance implies per-weight acceptance, but the converse fails. This failure is not a flaw of any particular weight choice; it reflects the noncommutation of the existential quantifier over actions with the universal quantifier over weights. 

This noncommutation is the assessment-theoretic counterpart of the strict minimax inequality in game theory. Treating the predicate ``action \(a\) is admissible at state \(z\) under weight \(w\)'' as a binary payoff, the two quantifier orders correspond to the two sides of the minimax interchange:
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item Under common-plan acceptance (\(\exists a\, \forall w\)), the planner must commit to an action before the evaluation weight is known—mirroring a pure-strategy maximin commitment.
\item Under per-weight acceptance (\(\forall w\, \exists a_w\)), the action may be selected after the weight is revealed—mirroring a wait-and-see response.
\end{itemize}

The witness system of Section 4.5 exhibits an explicit transition problem where this minimax interchange strictly fails. Remark 1 formalizes the general inclusion; Proposition 3(ii) establishes that the noncompensatory operator coincides with the common-plan set over the full cone; and Theorem 5 constructs an explicit rational witness where the acceptance gap \(\mathrm{FP}_{\mathrm{agg}} = \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{weak}} \setminus \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{typ}}\) forms a region with non-empty interior—the \emph{impossibility region} (Theorem 5(4)). 

The witness further partitions the discrepancy region \(\mathcal{Q}\) (where the aggregate and individual floors diverge) by a resource threshold into this impossibility region and a \emph{rescue set}, whose states can be safely transitioned using a dedicated, resource-backed action (Theorem 5(7)). The rescue operation analyzed in Section 5.5—resource augmentation—operates specifically on the impossibility region, not on the rescue set.

\subsubsection{1.3 Contribution and scope}\label{contribution-and-scope}

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
\tightlist
\item The action-set identity \(E_{\mathrm{typ}}(z) = \bigcap_{w \in W_+} E_w(z)\), showing that evaluating over the full non-negative cone isolates the separation as purely dynamic rather than static (Proposition 3(ii)).
\item A general quantifier-separation principle (Remark 1).
\item A proof that the accepted-state hierarchy contracts monotonically as the family of permitted weights expands (Proposition 4).
\item An explicit rational witness exhibiting an open region of strict separation, the discrepancy partition \(\mathcal{Q} = R \cup I\), the identification \(\mathrm{FP}_{\mathrm{agg}} = I\), closed-form per-weight licensing thresholds (Theorem 5), and the exact rescue threshold \(\kappa^* = 1 - x\) (Section 5.5).
\item A demonstration that this separation persists when the planning horizon is extended by prepending hold intervals (Remark 6).
\item An explicit two-stage erasure model showing that downstream recourse can eliminate the gap, confirming that multi-stage persistence is architecture-conditional (Theorem 7).
\item The blend-collapse theorem: convexifying the action menu eliminates the acceptance gap precisely across the compensatory region (Theorem 8), whereas discrete time-sharing closes it only where both floors survive their deficits independently—leaving the gap intact across the impossibility region (Proposition 9).
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Scope.} This paper does not assert a universal ranking between weak and strong sustainability doctrines; it does not claim that any specific empirical boundary must be managed noncompensatorily; nor does it evaluate real-world resource systems. Extensions addressing governance structures, intergenerational equity, and composite system architectures are reported with partial status in the Supplementary Material.

To our knowledge, this work offers the first explicit, exact-tube witness demonstrating dynamic quantifier separation between compensatory and noncompensatory sustainability assessments. The elementary logical property behind the separation is standard; its novelty and boundaries are delineated in Section 5.2.

\subsection{2. A Typed Framework for Transition Assessment}\label{a-typed-framework-for-transition-assessment}

This section defines the mathematical objects and semantics required for the analysis.

\subsubsection{2.1 Types and physical state}\label{types-and-physical-state}

Physical state variables are strictly typed. Each state variable denotes a \emph{moiety}—a distinct, conserved physical substance—measured in explicit units, and typed fluxes connect typed stocks. Conservation laws hold strictly per moiety: the framework does not permit adding biomass, currency, and biodiversity into a single conserved scalar. Service flows, critical thresholds, informational states, and institutional variables carry distinct types. Quantities of different types are never combined without an explicit physical bridge.

This typing discipline serves two purposes: it clarifies the domain of every physical conservation law, and it records the provenance of every floor—whether derived from physical dynamics, contractual commitments, or normative targets—as an intrinsic part of its definition.

\subsubsection{2.2 The assessment datum}\label{the-assessment-datum}

Our results are defined over a typed exact-tube transition datum: a state space \(Z\) carrying typed floors \(s_i \ge 0\) (normalized to zero), a discrete action menu, a disturbance set \(D\), a typed safe set \(S\), a destination set \(G\), and deterministic tube and successor mappings \(\mathrm{Tube}(a,d)\) and \(\mathrm{Succ}(a,d)\) as formalized in Section 3.1. 

Under exact-tube semantics, a transition is certified as safe if and only if every state visited along the entire continuous-time trajectory—not merely the endpoint—satisfies the constraints. The witness constructed in Section 4.5 is a concrete instance of this datum.

\subsubsection{2.3 Quantifier discipline}\label{quantifier-discipline}

Every assessment operator places the disturbance quantifier innermost: an action is admissible only if it guarantees safety against \emph{every} disturbance \(d \in D\). The separation analyzed in Section 3 introduces an outer quantifier over the scalarization weights, examining the noncommutation between \(\exists a\, \forall w\) and \(\forall w\, \exists a_w\).

\subsubsection{2.4 Notation}\label{notation}

The following notation is used throughout the paper:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \(z \in Z\): system state; \(a\): an action; \(d \in D\): an environmental disturbance; \(s = (s_1, \ldots, s_n)\): typed service floors (normalized such that \(s_i \ge 0\) represents the threshold); \(x\): reserve stock (bridge capital) in the witness system.
\item
  \(W_+ = \mathbb{R}^n_+ \setminus \{0\}\): the positive orthant of non-negative scalarization weights excluding the origin (Section 3.1); \(w \in W_+\): a scalarization weight vector; \(W \subseteq W_+\): a restricted weight family (Proposition 4); \(r = w_2 / w_1\): the weight ratio used in the two-floor witness; \(\kappa\): the resource augmentation increment (Section 5.5).
\item
  \(S = S^{\mathrm{phys}} \cap \{ s_i \ge 0,\ i = 1,\dots,n \}\) and \(G = G^{\mathrm{phys}} \cap \{ s \ge 0 \}\): typed safe and destination sets; \(S^w, G^w\): their scalarized counterparts under weight \(w\); \(S^{\mathrm{phys}}, G^{\mathrm{phys}}\): their underlying physical bounds; on the witness system, \(S = S_0\).
\item
  \(\mathrm{Tube}(a,d)\): the exact set of states visited throughout the review interval under action \(a\) and disturbance \(d\); \(\mathrm{End}(a,d)\): the set of terminal states reached at the end of the interval; \(\mathrm{Succ}(a,d)\): the successor state set following destination reset.
\item
  \(E_{\mathrm{typ}}(z)\), \(E_w(z)\), \(E_{\mathrm{tube,phys}}(z)\), \(E_{\mathrm{end}}(z)\), \(E_{\mathrm{end,typ}}(z)\): the five assessment operators defined in Section 3.1.
\item
  \(\mathcal{V}[E] = \{ z : E(z) \neq \varnothing \}\): the set of states accepted by operator \(E\). Specifically, \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{typ}} = \mathcal{V}[E_{\mathrm{typ}}]\), \(\mathcal{V}_w = \mathcal{V}[E_w]\), \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{weak}} = \bigcap_{w \in W_+} \mathcal{V}_w\), \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{phys}} = \mathcal{V}[E_{\mathrm{tube,phys}}]\), and \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{end}} = \mathcal{V}[E_{\mathrm{end}}]\).
\item
  Witness parameters (Section 4.5): state vector \((q, x, s_1, s_2)\); terminal gain vector \(e = (1/4, 1/4)\); transition cost \(c = 1\); worst-case dip depth \(2\); action menu \{NO-SWITCH, FAST, SLOW, STAGED\}.
\item
  Discrepancy sets (Section 4.6): \(\mathcal{Q}\): aggregate-versus-direct discrepancy region; \(R\): rescue set; \(I\): impossibility region; \(\mathrm{FP}_{\mathrm{agg}} = \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{weak}} \setminus \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{typ}}\): acceptance gap; \(\rho_1, \rho_2\): weight-ratio licensing thresholds.
\item
  \(\mathcal{A}\): the action menu; \(\mathsf{Aug}_\kappa\): the resource augmentation mapping, with minimal rescue threshold \(\kappa^*\).
\end{itemize}

Note that the gain vector \(e\) of the witness system is distinct from the standard basis vector \(e_k\) used in Remark 2.

\subsection{3. Assessment Operators}\label{assessment-operators}

\subsubsection{3.1 Five operators}\label{five-operators}

Consider a typed exact-tube transition datum with transition-safe sets and a destination target. For any state \(z\) and candidate action \(a\), let \(\mathrm{Tube}(a,d)\) be the continuous set of states traversed over the interval under disturbance \(d\), let \(\mathrm{End}(a,d)\) be the terminal state reached at the interval's boundary, and let \(\mathrm{Succ}(a,d)\) be the successor set following any post-transition reset. 

We distinguish five assessment operators. All five share the same robust disturbance quantifier (\(\forall d \in D\)), differing only in their constraint geometry and temporal scope. The first four operate either on full trajectory tubes or on isolated endpoints; the fifth is a typed-endpoint operator used in Section 5.4.

The \textbf{noncompensatory typed} operator enforces every physical and service constraint separately along the entire trajectory:
\[E_{\mathrm{typ}}(z) = \{ a : \forall d,\; \mathrm{Tube}(a,d) \subseteq S \ \text{ and } \ \mathrm{Succ}(a,d) \subseteq G \},\]
where \(S = S^{\mathrm{phys}} \cap \{ s_i \ge 0,\ i = 1,\dots,n \}\) and \(G = G^{\mathrm{phys}} \cap \{ s \ge 0 \}\).

The \textbf{scalarized aggregate} operator evaluates constraints against a single composite index defined by weight \(w \in W_+\):
\[E_w(z) = \{ a : \forall d,\; \mathrm{Tube}(a,d) \subseteq S^w \ \text{ and } \ \mathrm{Succ}(a,d) \subseteq G^w \},\]
where \(S^w = S^{\mathrm{phys}} \cap \{ w \cdot s \ge 0 \}\) and \(G^w = G^{\mathrm{phys}} \cap \{ w \cdot s \ge 0 \}\).

The \textbf{exact-tube physical} operator enforces only physical constraints, ignoring service floors, across the full trajectory:
\[E_{\mathrm{tube,phys}}(z) = \{ a : \forall d,\; \mathrm{Tube}(a,d) \subseteq S^{\mathrm{phys}} \ \text{ and } \ \mathrm{Succ}(a,d) \subseteq G^{\mathrm{phys}} \}.\]

The \textbf{endpoint-only physical} operator evaluates physical constraints strictly at the terminal boundary:
\[E_{\mathrm{end}}(z) = \{ a : \forall d,\; \mathrm{End}(a,d) \subseteq S^{\mathrm{phys}} \ \text{ and } \ \mathrm{Succ}(a,d) \subseteq G^{\mathrm{phys}} \}.\]

The endpoint operator reflects periodic snapshot audits (such as annual reporting), whereas typed floors continuously govern the full trajectory: it is the difference between inspecting a still photograph and reviewing the entire film. Terminal snapshots cannot, in general, verify whether the system maintained its structural integrity along the way (see the analysis of the productivity illusion in Abaee, 2026a). 

Because \(\mathrm{End}(a,d) \subseteq \mathrm{Tube}(a,d)\), the endpoint operator imposes the least restrictive requirement. Consequently, the inclusion chain
\[E_{\mathrm{typ}}(z) \;\subseteq\; E_w(z) \;\subseteq\; E_{\mathrm{tube,phys}}(z) \;\subseteq\; E_{\mathrm{end}}(z)\]
holds for every weight \(w \in W_+\) (Proposition 3(i)). While the final inclusion is strict in general dynamical systems, it collapses to an equality on our specific witness system because its only physical constraint governs a monotonically decreasing reserve stock.

\textbf{Definition (typed-endpoint operator).} The \textbf{typed-endpoint} operator tests typed floors exclusively at the terminal boundary:
\[E_{\mathrm{end,typ}}(z) = \{ a : \forall d,\; \mathrm{End}(a,d) \subseteq S \ \text{ and } \ \mathrm{Succ}(a,d) \subseteq G \}.\]
It satisfies \(E_{\mathrm{typ}}(z) \subseteq E_{\mathrm{end,typ}}(z) \subseteq E_{\mathrm{end}}(z)\), because \(\mathrm{End}(a,d) \subseteq \mathrm{Tube}(a,d)\), \(S \subseteq S^{\mathrm{phys}}\), and \(G \subseteq G^{\mathrm{phys}}\). 

On the witness datum of Section 4.5, the action FAST restores \(s_1\) to its initial value by the end of the interval, and its successor lands in \(G\) whenever \(x \ge 0\) (see the action table in Section 4.5 and the proof of Theorem 5(1)). Thus, \(E_{\mathrm{end,typ}}(z) \neq \varnothing\) across all states in \(X_0\), whereas true typed safety, \(E_{\mathrm{typ}}(z) \neq \varnothing\), strictly requires \(s_1 \ge 2\). This observation follows directly by inspecting the dynamics and is treated separately from the machine-checked rational proofs described in Section 4.9.

\subsubsection{3.2 Accepted-state operators}\label{accepted-state-operators}

For any operator \(E\), we define its accepted-state set as:
\[\mathcal{V}[E] \;=\; \{ z : E(z) \neq \varnothing \}.\]
This yields \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{typ}} = \mathcal{V}[E_{\mathrm{typ}}]\), \(\mathcal{V}_w = \mathcal{V}[E_w]\), \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{phys}} = \mathcal{V}[E_{\mathrm{tube,phys}}]\), and \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{end}} = \mathcal{V}[E_{\mathrm{end}}]\). 

The compensatory accepted set across all non-negative weights is:
\[\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{weak}} \;=\; \bigcap_{w \in W_+} \mathcal{V}_w .\]
The central noncommutation between common-plan and per-weight acceptance is then expressed as:
\[\underbrace{\{ z : \textstyle\bigcap_w E_w(z) \neq \varnothing \}}_{\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{typ}} \ \text{(common plan)}} \;\subseteq\; \underbrace{\bigcap_w \{ z : E_w(z) \neq \varnothing \}}_{\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{weak}} \ \text{(per weight)}}.\]

\subsection{4. Results: The Separation Theorem}\label{results-the-separation-theorem}

\subsubsection{4.1 A general quantifier-separation remark}\label{a-general-quantifier-separation-remark}

\textbf{Remark 1.} \emph{Let \(\{E_\lambda(z)\}_{\lambda \in \Lambda}\) be an arbitrary family of action sets. Then the following inclusion always holds:}
\[\Big\{ z : \textstyle\bigcap_{\lambda} E_\lambda(z) \neq \varnothing \Big\} \;\subseteq\; \bigcap_{\lambda} \big\{ z : E_\lambda(z) \neq \varnothing \big\}.\]
\emph{Equality holds if and only if, for every \(z\) in the right-hand set, the family \(\{E_\lambda(z)\}_\lambda\) admits a common selector. No topological structure on the action space is assumed. When the action menu is finite, this condition can be verified combinatorially: only finitely many distinct sets \(E_\lambda(z)\) can arise, reducing the search for a common selector to finite enumeration (on our witness system, \(|\mathcal{A}| = 4\)). For infinite action spaces, standard sufficient conditions—such as the finite intersection property paired with compactness—apply directly, though they are not needed here.}

\emph{Proof.} If an action \(a\) belongs to \(\bigcap_\lambda E_\lambda(z)\), then every \(E_\lambda(z)\) contains \(a\) and is therefore non-empty, placing \(z\) in the right-hand intersection. The inclusion fails to reverse at \(z\) precisely when every \(E_\lambda(z)\) is individually non-empty but their intersection is empty—that is, when no single action satisfies all constraints simultaneously. \ensuremath{\square}

The separation demonstrated in Section 4.5 is a strict instance of this gap, occurring where the actions certifying safety vary with the chosen weight.

\subsubsection{4.2 The full-cone identity}\label{the-full-cone-identity}

\textbf{Remark 2 (full-cone pointwise equivalence).} \emph{For any vector \(v \in \mathbb{R}^n\),}
\[v \ge 0 \ \text{componentwise} \iff w \cdot v \ge 0 \ \text{for every } w \in W_+ = \mathbb{R}^n_+ \setminus \{0\}.\]

\emph{Proof.} 
\((\Rightarrow)\) If \(w \ge 0\), \(w \neq 0\), and \(v \ge 0\), the inner product satisfies \(w \cdot v = \sum_i w_i v_i \ge 0\). 

\((\Leftarrow)\) Suppose \(v_k < 0\) for some coordinate \(k\). Selecting the standard basis vector \(w = e_k \in W_+\) yields \(w \cdot v = v_k < 0\), establishing the contrapositive. \ensuremath{\square}

Remark 2 shows that evaluated along any \emph{fixed} trajectory, scalarization over the full non-negative cone is completely lossless: enforcing every non-negative linear combination is strictly equivalent to enforcing each constraint separately. The divergence established below is therefore entirely dynamic—a product of quantifier ordering—rather than a static limitation of linear indexing.

\subsubsection{4.3 The assessment identity and hierarchy}\label{the-assessment-identity-and-hierarchy}

\textbf{Proposition 3 (assessment identity and hierarchy).}

\emph{(i) Hierarchy.} \emph{For every weight \(w \in W_+\) and every state \(z\),}
\[E_{\mathrm{typ}}(z) \;\subseteq\; E_w(z) \;\subseteq\; E_{\mathrm{tube,phys}}(z) \;\subseteq\; E_{\mathrm{end}}(z).\]

\emph{(ii) Localization.} \emph{For every state \(z\),}
\[E_{\mathrm{typ}}(z) \;=\; \bigcap_{w \in W_+} E_w(z).\] 
\emph{Consequently, \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{typ}} = \{ z : \bigcap_w E_w(z) \neq \varnothing \}\).}

\emph{Proof.} 
(i) Let \(a \in E_{\mathrm{typ}}(z)\). For every disturbance \(d\), the trajectory satisfies \(\mathrm{Tube}(a,d) \subseteq S = S^{\mathrm{phys}} \cap \{s \ge 0\}\). By Remark 2, every point along the tube satisfies \(w \cdot s \ge 0\) for all \(w \in W_+\), and every successor state lies in \(G^w\); hence \(a \in E_w(z)\). The remaining inclusions follow immediately from \(S^w \subseteq S^{\mathrm{phys}}\), \(G^w \subseteq G^{\mathrm{phys}}\), and \(\mathrm{End}(a,d) \subseteq \mathrm{Tube}(a,d)\). 

(ii) Taking the intersection of the first inclusion in (i) over all \(w \in W_+\) yields \(E_{\mathrm{typ}}(z) \subseteq \bigcap_{w \in W_+} E_w(z)\). For the reverse inclusion, suppose \(a \in \bigcap_{w \in W_+} E_w(z)\). For every disturbance \(d\) and every trajectory point \(p \in \mathrm{Tube}(a,d)\), the condition \(w \cdot s(p) \ge 0\) holds for all \(w \in W_+\). Remark 2 implies that \(s(p) \ge 0\) componentwise, so \(\mathrm{Tube}(a,d) \subseteq S\). Applying the same reasoning to \(\mathrm{Succ}(a,d)\) places all successor states in \(G\). Therefore, \(a \in E_{\mathrm{typ}}(z)\). \ensuremath{\square}

Part (i) reflects standard constraint monotonicity for viability kernels under nested constraint sets (Aubin, 1991; Frankowska, 1989). Part (ii) shows that noncompensatory safety can be localized as the common-plan core across the full weight cone; Theorem 5 demonstrates that this core can be strictly smaller than the compensatory accepted set.

\subsubsection{4.4 Weight-family monotonicity}\label{weight-family-monotonicity}

\textbf{Proposition 4 (weight-family monotonicity).} \emph{For any subfamily of weights \(W \subseteq W_+\), define the accepted set \(\mathcal{V}_W = \bigcap_{w \in W} \mathcal{V}_w\). Then}
\[\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{typ}} \;\subseteq\; \mathcal{V}_W \;\subseteq\; \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{phys}},\]
\emph{and if \(W_1 \subseteq W_2\), then \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{W_2}} \subseteq \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{W_1}}\).}

\emph{Proof.} 
For the upper bound: by Proposition 3(i), \(E_w(z) \subseteq E_{\mathrm{tube,phys}}(z)\) for every \(w\), so \(\mathcal{V}_w \subseteq \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{phys}}\). Intersecting over all \(w \in W\) preserves this inclusion. 

For the lower bound: if \(z \in \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{typ}}\), Proposition 3(ii) guarantees an action \(a \in \bigcap_{w \in W_+} E_w(z)\). This action necessarily belongs to \(\bigcap_{w \in W} E_w(z)\), meaning \(E_w(z)\) is non-empty for every \(w \in W\), and thus \(z \in \mathcal{V}_W\). 

Monotonicity follows directly: intersecting conditions over a larger weight family \(W_2\) imposes more restrictions, shrinking the resulting set of accepted states. \ensuremath{\square}

Proposition 4 highlights a vital structural property: \emph{restricting} the set of permissible weights \emph{expands} the compensatory accepted set. The full cone \(W_+\) therefore represents the most demanding compensatory standard possible. Any separation between compensatory and noncompensatory acceptance established on the full cone persists a fortiori for any restricted weight family.

\subsubsection{4.5 The witness datum}\label{the-witness-datum}

To ground these theoretical relations, we construct an explicit, rational transition system that exhibits the separation proven in Theorem 5. 

The system transitions between two regimes—an extractive phase (\(q = 0\)) and a regenerative phase (\(q = 1\))—over a single normalized review interval \([0,1]\). The state vector is \((q, x, s_1, s_2)\), consisting of a physical reserve stock \(x\) and two typed service floors: \(s_1\) (protected-group service surplus) and \(s_2\) (remediation liability coverage), both normalized such that non-negativity is the binding constraint. The transition-safe set is \(S_0 = \{ x \ge 0, s_1 \ge 0, s_2 \ge 0 \}\). The target destination set is \(G = \{ (1, x, s) : x \ge 0, s \ge 0 \}\), within which future viability is secured by an invariant hold policy. 

Successful transitions apply a strictly positive terminal gain \(e = (1/4, 1/4)\) to both service floors; this ensures successor states lie strictly in the interior of \(G\), so none of the inequalities in Theorem 5 bind on the magnitude of \(e\). Deploying the reserve-backed transition (STAGED) incurs a physical capital cost of \(c = 1\) (spending one unit of \(x\)).

\textbf{Disturbance convention (action-indexed).} The disturbance set is \(D = \{\beta, \alpha\}\). Disturbance \(\alpha\) induces a worst-case deficit of fixed depth 2 in the coordinate directly modified by the active policy (\(s_1\) for FAST, \(s_2\) for SLOW). Disturbance \(\beta\) acts analogously on the alternate coordinate. Disturbances do not affect coordinates that an action leaves untouched. 

Consequently, each action's worst-case trajectory tube reflects its own characteristic deficit. Even if a disturbance label is shared across actions, it operates only on the coordinate dynamically engaged by the policy: FAST leaves the \(s_2\)-tube undisturbed, and SLOW leaves the \(s_1\)-tube undisturbed.

The system provides four actions from any initial state \((0, x, s)\) with \(x \ge 0\) and \(s \ge 0\). Trajectories are continuous and piecewise-linear across breakpoints \(\{0, \tfrac12, 1\}\), and monotonic on each segment, ensuring that each calculated tube matches the exact set of visited states without approximation:

FAST & \(s_1(t) = s_1 - 4t\) on \([0,\tfrac12]\), \newline \(s_1 - 4(1-t)\) on \([\tfrac12,1]\); \newline \(s_2, x\) constant & \(\{(1, x, s + e)\}\) \\
SLOW & \(s_2(t) = s_2 - 4t\) on \([0,\tfrac12]\), \newline \(s_2 - 4(1-t)\) on \([\tfrac12,1]\); \newline \(s_1, x\) constant & \(\{(1, x, s + e)\}\) \\
STAGED & \(x(t) = x - t\) on \([0,1]\); \newline floors grow monotonically to \(s + e\) & \(\{(1, x - 1, s + e)\}\) \\
\end{longtable}

Under worst-case disturbances, FAST traces the interval \([s_1 - 2, s_1]\) in coordinate \(s_1\) while holding \(s_2\) and \(x\) fixed; SLOW traces \([s_2 - 2, s_2]\) in coordinate \(s_2\) while holding \(s_1\) and \(x\) fixed; and STAGED draws the reserve stock down through \([x - 1, x]\) while maintaining or improving both service floors.

\subsubsection{4.6 Witnessed separation}\label{witnessed-separation}

\textbf{Theorem 5 (witnessed separation).} \emph{On the witness datum of Section 4.5, over initial states \(X_0 = \{(0, x, s) : x \ge 0, s \ge 0\}\), the following characterizations hold:}

\textbf{(1)}
\(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{typ}} = \{ x \ge 1 \} \cup \{ s_1 \ge 2 \} \cup \{ s_2 \ge 2 \}.\)

\textbf{(2)}
\(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{weak}} = \bigcap_{w \in W_+} \mathcal{V}_w = \{ x \ge 1 \} \cup \{ s_1 + s_2 \ge 2 \}.\)

\textbf{(3)}
\(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{phys}} = \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{end}} = X_0.\)

\emph{Define:}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item \(\mathcal{Q} = \{ s_1 < 2,\ s_2 < 2,\ s_1 + s_2 \ge 2 \}\): the discrepancy region where aggregate and individual floor requirements diverge (with \(x\) unconstrained). This set is not entirely composed of false positives, because whenever \(x \ge 1\), STAGED provides a viable noncompensatory plan.
\item \(R = \mathcal{Q} \cap \{ x \ge 1 \}\): the \textbf{rescue set}, containing states within the discrepancy region that can already achieve safe transition via STAGED at physical cost \(c = 1\).
\item \(I = \mathcal{Q} \cap \{ x < 1 \}\): the \textbf{impossibility region}.
\item \(\mathrm{FP}_{\mathrm{agg}} = \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{weak}} \setminus \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{typ}}\): the genuine compensatory-versus-noncompensatory acceptance gap.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{(4)} \(\mathrm{FP}_{\mathrm{agg}} = I.\) \emph{The genuine acceptance gap coincides exactly with the impossibility region. It is non-empty, and its relative interior within the \(q = 0\) slice is given by \(\{ 0 < x < 1,\ 0 < s_1 < 2,\ 0 < s_2 < 2,\ s_1 + s_2 > 2 \}\). The rescue set \(R\) belongs entirely to \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{typ}}\) and does not contribute to the acceptance gap: it is the portion of \(\mathcal{Q}\) that is not a false positive.}

\textbf{(5)} \emph{Both inclusions in the assessment hierarchy are strict: every state in \(I\) belongs to \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{weak}} \setminus \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{typ}}\), and the state \((x, s_1, s_2) = (\tfrac12, \tfrac1{10}, \tfrac1{10})\) lies in \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{phys}} \setminus \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{weak}}\).}

\textbf{(6) Per-weight plan disagreement.} \emph{On the relative interior of \(\mathcal{Q}\) (where \(s_2 > 0\) and \(s_2 < 2\)), let \(r = w_2 / w_1\) denote the relative weight ratio. Then FAST is licensed precisely when \(r \ge \rho_1\) and SLOW is licensed precisely when \(r \le \rho_2\), where}
\[\rho_1 = \frac{2 - s_1}{s_2}, \qquad \rho_2 = \frac{s_1}{2 - s_2}, \qquad \text{and} \qquad \rho_2 \ge \rho_1 \iff s_1 + s_2 \ge 2.\]
\emph{Assessors who place high relative weight on the second floor (\(r > \rho_2\)) license only FAST; assessors placing low relative weight on it (\(r < \rho_1\)) license only SLOW; intermediate assessors license both. On the impossibility region \(I\), no single action is valid for all weights simultaneously—meaning \(\bigcap_w E_w(z) = E_{\mathrm{typ}}(z) = \varnothing\) by Proposition 3(ii). On the rescue set \(R\), STAGED is typed-admissible and satisfies all weights simultaneously.}

\textbf{(7) The rescue split.} \emph{States in \(R\) are transformable under the noncompensatory doctrine via STAGED: investing one unit of physical reserve stock (\(c = 1\)) preserves both floors throughout the transition and lands safely in \(G\). States in \(I\), while certified as feasible by the aggregate doctrine under every possible weight vector, admit no valid noncompensatory action. The failure on \(I\) is exhaustive: FAST violates the \(s_1\) floor under adverse disturbance; SLOW violates the \(s_2\) floor; STAGED depletes the reserve stock below zero (\(x < 1\)); and NO-SWITCH fails to reach the destination \(G\).}

\emph{Proof.} 
\textbf{(1)} Typed admissibility requires that the worst-case tube remain within \(S_0\) and that the successor reach \(G\). NO-SWITCH is never admissible because its successor remains at \(q = 0\), failing to reach \(G\). 

For FAST, the worst-case \(s_1\)-tube is \([s_1 - 2, s_1]\), which remains non-negative if and only if \(s_1 \ge 2\). Its successor \((1, x, s + e)\) satisfies \(G\) whenever \(x \ge 0\). Thus, FAST is typed-admissible if and only if \(s_1 \ge 2\). By symmetry, SLOW is typed-admissible if and only if \(s_2 \ge 2\). 

For STAGED, both service floors increase monotonically and the successor lands in \(G\); the sole binding constraint is the physical reserve tube \([x - 1, x]\), which remains non-negative if and only if \(x \ge 1\). 

Combining these conditions, \(E_{\mathrm{typ}}(z) \neq \varnothing\) if and only if \(x \ge 1\), \(s_1 \ge 2\), or \(s_2 \ge 2\).

\textbf{(2)} Fix an arbitrary weight vector \(w = (w_1, w_2) \in W_+\). NO-SWITCH remains inadmissible as it never reaches \(G^w\). 

FAST is admissible under weight \(w\) if and only if its aggregate floor remains non-negative under the worst-case dip: \(w_1(s_1 - 2) + w_2 s_2 \ge 0\), which simplifies to \(w_1 s_1 + w_2 s_2 \ge 2 w_1\) (trivially satisfied when \(w_1 = 0\)). Symmetrically, SLOW is admissible under \(w\) if and only if \(w_1 s_1 + w_2 s_2 \ge 2 w_2\). STAGED is admissible under \(w\) if and only if \(x \ge 1\) (the physical constraint within \(S^w\)), as its service floors never decrease.

Consequently, if \(x \ge 1\), then \(z \in \mathcal{V}_w\) for every \(w\), placing \(z \in \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{weak}}\). 

If \(x < 1\), the state is weakly accepted if and only if every weight vector permits either FAST or SLOW. When \(s_1 \ge 2\) (or \(s_2 \ge 2\)), FAST (or SLOW) is typed-admissible by part (1), and therefore valid for all weights. 

It remains to analyze the case where \(x < 1\), \(s_1 < 2\), and \(s_2 < 2\). For weights with \(w_1 > 0\), define the ratio \(r = w_2 / w_1\). FAST is admissible if and only if \(s_1 + r s_2 \ge 2\), which rearranges to \(r \ge (2 - s_1)/s_2 = \rho_1\) (since \(s_2 > 0\); if \(s_2 = 0\), this collapses to \(s_1 \ge 2\), which is excluded). Similarly, SLOW is admissible if and only if \(s_1 + r s_2 \ge 2r\), which rearranges to \(r \le s_1/(2 - s_2) = \rho_2\) (since \(s_2 < 2\)). 

At the boundary weights: when \(w_1 = 0\) (\(r \to \infty\)), FAST is licensed whenever \(s_2 \ge 0\), while SLOW is rejected because \(s_2 < 2\). Conversely, when \(w_2 = 0\) (\(r = 0\)), SLOW is licensed whenever \(s_1 \ge 0\), while FAST is rejected because \(s_1 < 2\). 

Therefore, every weight \(w \in W_+\) admits an action if and only if the intervals \([0, \rho_2]\) and \([\rho_1, \infty]\) overlap to cover all of \([0, \infty]\). This occurs if and only if \(\rho_2 \ge \rho_1\). Evaluating this inequality:
\[\frac{s_1}{2 - s_2} \ge \frac{2 - s_1}{s_2} \iff s_1 s_2 \ge (2 - s_1)(2 - s_2) \iff s_1 + s_2 \ge 2.\]
Both denominators are strictly positive on this domain. Thus, for \(x < 1\), \(z \in \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{weak}}\) if and only if \(s_1 + s_2 \ge 2\). Combining this with the \(x \ge 1\) case yields \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{weak}} = \{ x \ge 1 \} \cup \{ s_1 + s_2 \ge 2 \}\).

\textbf{(3)} The physical operators evaluate only the reserve stock constraint \(x \ge 0\) and regime transition \(q = 1\). FAST and SLOW modify only \(s_1\) and \(s_2\), leaving \(x\) constant; their tubes remain in \(S^{\mathrm{phys}}\) and successors reach \(G^{\mathrm{phys}}\) for all initial states in \(X_0\). Hence, \(E_{\mathrm{tube,phys}}(z)\) is non-empty across \(X_0\), giving \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{phys}} = X_0\). Because endpoint evaluation relaxes trajectory evaluation, \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{end}} = X_0\) holds immediately.

\textbf{(4)} Subtracting the characterization in (1) from that in (2):
\[\mathrm{FP}_{\mathrm{agg}} = \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{weak}} \setminus \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{typ}} = \big[ \{x \ge 1\} \cup \{s_1 + s_2 \ge 2\} \big] \setminus \big[ \{x \ge 1\} \cup \{s_1 \ge 2\} \cup \{s_2 \ge 2\} \big] = \{ x < 1,\ s_1 < 2,\ s_2 < 2,\ s_1 + s_2 \ge 2 \} = I.\]
This set is non-empty (witnessed by \((x, s_1, s_2) = (\tfrac12, \tfrac65, \tfrac65)\)), and its relative interior corresponds to the stated open inequalities.

\textbf{(5)} By part (4), \(I = \mathrm{FP}_{\mathrm{agg}} \neq \varnothing\), proving the first inclusion is strict. For the second, consider \((x, s_1, s_2) = (\tfrac12, \tfrac1{10}, \tfrac1{10})\). Here, \(x < 1\) and \(s_1 + s_2 = \tfrac15 < 2\), placing the state outside \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{weak}}\), even though it belongs to \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{phys}} = X_0\).

\textbf{(6)} The threshold characterization follows from the proof of part (2). On \(\mathcal{Q}\), the condition \(s_1 + s_2 \ge 2\) ensures that \(\rho_2 \ge \rho_1\). On its relative interior, both thresholds are strictly positive and finite, leaving a region of disagreement: weights with \(r > \rho_2\) accept only FAST, while weights with \(r < \rho_1\) accept only SLOW. On \(I\), where \(x < 1\), STAGED is unavailable, meaning no single action satisfies all weights simultaneously: \(\bigcap_w E_w(z) = \varnothing\). On \(R\), where \(x \ge 1\), STAGED is viable for every weight.

\textbf{(7)} For any state in \(R\), STAGED's reserve trajectory remains non-negative (\([x - 1, x] \ge 0\)), its service floors improve, and its successor lands in \(G\). On \(I\), every action violates at least one non-negotiable constraint: FAST breaches \(s_1 < 0\) under disturbance \(\alpha\); SLOW breaches \(s_2 < 0\) under disturbance \(\alpha\); STAGED depletes the reserve stock below zero (\(x - 1 < 0\)); and NO-SWITCH fails to transition to \(q = 1\). \ensuremath{\square}

\emph{Boundary behavior.} The ratio expressions for \(\rho_1\) and \(\rho_2\) assume \(s_2 \in (0, 2)\). On the boundary segments (\(s_1 = 0, 2\), \(s_2 = 0, 2\), or \(s_1 + s_2 = 2\)), state classifications follow directly from the closed sets established in parts (1) and (2); no interior formula is applied there.

\textbf{Takeaway.} The compensatory assessment's binding condition is the aggregate budget \(s_1 + s_2 \ge 2\). The noncompensatory assessment requires either that one service floor can survive its worst-case deficit independently (\(s_i \ge 2\)) or that the system holds sufficient reserve capital to finance a controlled transition (\(x \ge 1\)). The region between these two conditions is where compensatory assessment certifies—per weight, with weight-dependent plans—transitions that noncompensatory assessment rejects.

\subsubsection{4.8 Propagation under hold-prefix extension}\label{propagation-under-hold-prefix-extension}

This subsection examines whether this one-interval separation persists when the horizon is extended by prepending hold intervals. Two results delimit the answer. Remark 6 establishes persistence under a precisely specified hold-prefix extension; Theorem 7 constructs a two-stage counterexample where downstream recourse erases the gap, demonstrating that multi-stage persistence is architecture-conditional.

\textbf{Remark 6 (propagation under hold-prefix extension).} \emph{Extend the witness datum to \(m \ge 2\) review intervals by prepending hold intervals governed solely by a HOLD action (constant tube \(\{z\}\), successor \(\{z\}\), safe set \(S_0\)), with the final interval carrying the active transition. Assume:}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item \emph{(H1) HOLD is available at every prefix state;}
\item \emph{(H2) the hold tube is exactly the identity \(\{z\}\);}
\item \emph{(H3) prefix safe sets contain the witness states;}
\item \emph{(H4) terminal targets and successor architectures are preserved; and}
\item \emph{(H5) no additional reset or disturbance branches are introduced.}
\end{itemize}

\emph{Then:}

\emph{(i) Structural hierarchy.} \emph{At every stage \(j\), the accepted sets satisfy}
\[\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{typ}}_j \;\subseteq\; \bigcap_{w \in W_+} \mathcal{V}^{w}_j \;\subseteq\; \mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{phys}}_j.\]
\emph{This chain holds for any multi-interval typed exact-tube datum by backward induction on constraint sets.}

\emph{(ii) Preservation of strictness.} \emph{Initial accepted states at stage 0 correspond to the single-stage witness regions pulled back across identity hold steps. Thus, both strictness properties from Theorem 5 persist under this hold-prefix extension.}

\emph{(iii) Delimitation.} \emph{The separation is not an artifact of evaluating a single isolated interval. However, extending strict separation to arbitrary multi-stage systems requires explicit conditions: later stages can erase the gap if every aggregate-feasible state gains access to a common typed-safe continuation, if weak and typed terminal sets coincide on the reachable domain, or if actions are coupled across stages.}

\emph{Proof.} 
(i) By backward induction across stages. At the final stage, terminal sets satisfy \(G \subseteq G^w \subseteq G^{\mathrm{phys}}\) by Remark 2. At each preceding stage, the set of viable states consists of those possessing an action that keeps the tube in the safe set and the successor in the next stage's accepted set. Applying Proposition 3(i) to the stage-level action sets and invoking the induction hypothesis preserves the three-way inclusion. 

(ii) With HOLD the sole prefix action, an initial state is accepted if and only if it belongs to the prefix safe set and to the next stage's accepted set. Because HOLD acts as an identity operator on states, the stage-0 viable regions are the exact pullbacks of the sets from Theorem 5. Both witnesses (\(I \neq \varnothing\) and the point \((\tfrac12, \tfrac1{10}, \tfrac1{10})\)) remain valid. 

(iii) The three mechanisms listed in (iii) are each sufficient to collapse the gap, showing why multi-stage extensions must be evaluated conditionally. \ensuremath{\square}

\textbf{Theorem 7 (erasure witness).} \emph{The propagation of the acceptance gap across multiple stages depends on system architecture: there exists a two-stage transition system whose single-stage truncation exhibits an acceptance gap, but whose full two-stage execution erases it entirely via terminal coincidence (Remark 6(iii)).}

\emph{Construction.} Consider two capital forms with floors at zero and no physical reserve stock (\(x \equiv 0\)), so states are pairs \(s = (s_1, s_2)\). 

Stage 2 (the final stage) imposes no trajectory constraint (\(S_0^{(2)} = \mathbb{R}^2\)) and features identical terminal sets for all weights: \(G^{(2)} = G^{w,(2)} = \{ s \ge 0 \}\). It contains a single action, REPAIR, which has tube \(\{s\}\) (safe trivially) and successor \((1, 1) \in G\). 

Stage 1 enforces the safe set \(S_0 = \{ s \ge 0 \}\) and provides two actions with constant tubes, safe at every state of \(S_0\): A1 transitions to \((s_1 - 1, s_2 + 1)\), and A2 transitions to \((s_1 + 1, s_2 - 1)\). Stage 1 targets the accepted set of Stage 2. Consider the initial state \(z^* = (\tfrac25, \tfrac25)\).

\emph{Gap erasure in the two-stage model.} Because REPAIR is typed-admissible—and thus \(w\)-admissible for every weight—from every state in Stage 2, the stage-2 accepted sets coincide completely: \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{typ}}^{(2)} = \mathcal{V}_w^{(2)} = \mathbb{R}^2\) for all \(w\). Backward induction to Stage 1 reveals that both A1 and A2 maintain safe tubes and land within \(\mathbb{R}^2\). Consequently, \(z^*\) is typed-accepted at Stage 1, and weak acceptance follows immediately. The acceptance gap vanishes.

\emph{Gap emergence in single-stage truncation.} Now consider Stage 1 in isolation, truncating the model by forcing its terminal targets to match standard terminal sets: \(G = \{s \ge 0\}\) and \(G^w = \{w \cdot s \ge 0\}\). 

Under typed evaluation, \(z^*\) is rejected: neither A1 nor A2 reaches \(G\), because \(s_1 - 1 = -\tfrac35 < 0\) and \(s_2 - 1 = -\tfrac35 < 0\). 

Under weak evaluation, let \(r = w_2 / w_1\) on the interior of the weight cone. Action A1 is admissible if \((s_1 - 1) + r(s_2 + 1) \ge 0\), which requires \(r \ge \tfrac{1 - s_1}{s_2 + 1} = \tfrac37\). Action A2 is admissible if \((s_1 + 1) + r(s_2 - 1) \ge 0\), which requires \(r \le \tfrac{s_1 + 1}{1 - s_2} = \tfrac73\). 

Because \(\tfrac37 < \tfrac73\), these two intervals overlap and cover all of \([0, \infty]\); boundary weights are covered directly (\(r = 0\) is satisfied by A2, and \(r \to \infty\) is satisfied by A1). Hence, \(z^* \in \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{weak}}\) in the truncation, and the acceptance gap \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{weak}} \setminus \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{typ}}\) contains \(z^*\). \ensuremath{\square}

The witness datum of Section 4.5 is immune to this type of erasure. Its gap is driven by trajectory tube violations: across the impossibility region \(I\), every action in the four-action menu violates the intermediate safe set \(S_0\) itself (due to the two service floor deficits and the reserve deficit of Theorem 5(7)). Because intermediate tube breaches cannot be undone by later recourse—the stage-1 constraint is local to stage 1—the gap on \(I\) is structurally uncorrectable downstream. Remark 6's safe direction is secured by exactly the assumptions the erasure datum violates (unchanged terminal architecture, no new branches). Together, the two results delimit the persistence claim: hold-prefix extensions preserve the separation, arbitrary multi-stage extensions do not, and the erasure mechanisms of Remark 6(iii) are realizable.

\subsubsection{4.10 Menu convexification: the blend family}\label{menu-convexification-the-blend-family}

The baseline action menu \(\mathcal{A} = \{\text{FAST}, \text{SLOW}, \text{STAGED}, \text{NO-SWITCH}\}\) consists of discrete, deterministic actions; fractional or alternating policies are not members of it. To examine whether the acceptance gap stems from this discreteness, we introduce control convexification. 

For each mixing parameter \(\delta \in (0,1)\), let \(\text{BLEND}_\delta\) represent the fractional allocation of control inputs:
\[u_\delta = \delta\, u_{\mathrm{FAST}} + (1-\delta)\, u_{\mathrm{SLOW}}.\]
By linearity of the flow map, its worst-case trajectory tube is the pointwise convex combination of the two primitive worst-case tubes, and its successor is the convex combination of the two successors. This models continuous control blending at the level of inputs—an explicit model assumption, not a time-sharing of discrete actions in alternation.

\textbf{Theorem 8 (blend collapse).} \emph{On the witness datum of Section 4.5, over initial states \(X_0\):}

\emph{(i)} \emph{\(\text{BLEND}_\delta\) is typed-admissible at state \(z\) if and only if}
\[\delta \in \left[\,1 - \frac{s_2}{2},\; \frac{s_1}{2}\,\right].\]
\emph{This feasibility interval is non-empty if and only if \(s_1 + s_2 \ge 2\) (collapsing to a single point when equality holds), independent of \(x \ge 0\) and of the scalarization weight.}

\emph{(ii)} \emph{Augmenting the discrete menu with the blend family expands the typed accepted set to coincide exactly with the compensatory region:}
\[\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{typ}}^{\mathrm{blend}} \;=\; \{ x \ge 1 \} \cup \{ s_1 + s_2 \ge 2 \} \;=\; \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{weak}}.\]
\emph{Convexifying the action menu collapses the acceptance gap: \(\mathrm{FP}_{\mathrm{agg}}\) vanishes under the augmented menu, and the blend family reaches precisely the compensatory region and nothing beyond—across \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{phys}} \setminus \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{weak}}\), no blend is typed-admissible.}

\emph{(iii)} \emph{The admissible window for \(\delta\) depends solely on the service floors \((s_1, s_2)\). A single choice of \(\delta\) satisfies every weight \(w \in W_+\) simultaneously. On the impossibility region, continuous blending supplies the single unified action that the deterministic menu cannot provide. The acceptance gap is therefore a property of the deterministic menu, not of the assessment doctrines themselves.}

\emph{Proof.} 
(i) FAST's worst-case trajectory traces \([s_1 - 2, s_1]\) in coordinate \(s_1\) while holding \(s_2\) constant. SLOW's worst-case trajectory traces \([s_2 - 2, s_2]\) in coordinate \(s_2\) while holding \(s_1\) constant. Both actions leave the reserve stock \(x\) non-negative throughout (their deficits affect only \(s\)). 

The blended tube therefore has coordinates \([s_1 - 2\delta, s_1]\) in \(s_1\), \([s_2 - 2(1-\delta), s_2]\) in \(s_2\), and maintains non-negative \(x\)-coordinates (as convex combinations of non-negative quantities). Typed admissibility requires that the tube remain within \(S_0 = \{x \ge 0, s \ge 0\}\) and the successor in \(G\). The tube condition is precisely the displayed window:
\[s_1 - 2\delta \ge 0 \implies \delta \le \frac{s_1}{2}, \qquad \text{and} \qquad s_2 - 2(1-\delta) \ge 0 \implies \delta \ge 1 - \frac{s_2}{2}.\]
The successor is the mixture of the two successors, which the datum fixes to the same value: the action table of Section 4.5 gives both FAST and SLOW the successor \((1, x, s + e)\), so the blended successor is \((1, x, s + e) \in G\) (since \(x \ge 0\) and the destination label is \(1\)). 

The window is non-empty if and only if \(1 - s_2/2 \le s_1/2\), which is equivalent to \(s_1 + s_2 \ge 2\). At equality it is the singleton \(\delta = s_1/2 = 1 - s_2/2\), feasible because \(S_0\) is closed. On the boundary faces (\(s_1 = 0\) or \(s_2 = 0\)) the window is closed at the corresponding endpoint, and the classification follows the boundary conventions of Section 4.6.

(ii) If \(s_1 + s_2 \ge 2\), part (i) supplies a typed-admissible blend for any \(x \ge 0\). If \(s_1 + s_2 < 2\), no blend is typed-admissible by (i), and the deterministic menu contributes exactly \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{typ}} = \{x \ge 1\} \cup \{s_1 \ge 2\} \cup \{s_2 \ge 2\} \subseteq \{x \ge 1\} \cup \{s_1 + s_2 \ge 2\}\). Hence, the augmented typed set equals \(\{x \ge 1\} \cup \{s_1 + s_2 \ge 2\} = \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{weak}}\) by Theorem 5(2)—and nothing beyond, since every admissible blend satisfies \(s_1 + s_2 \ge 2\) and the deterministic actions remain within \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{typ}}\).

(iii) The feasibility window depends only on \(s_1\) and \(s_2\), with no dependence on the weight vector \(w\). Selecting any \(\delta\) from this window produces an action that is typed-admissible, and therefore \(w\)-admissible for every weight. \ensuremath{\square}

\textbf{Remark.} The collapse is exact and weight-independent: a single convexified plan serves every assessor across the impossibility region \(I\), resolving the conflict between the weight-dependent plans of Theorem 5(6). 

The nonconvexity at work is menu geometry—the finiteness and determinism of the action space—rather than the Pareto-frontier geometry under which weighted sums fail to reach nonconvex trade-off surfaces (Das and Dennis, 1997); the two mechanisms are distinct. The structure is precisely that of pure-versus-mixed strategies in the assessor--planner game (von Neumann, 1928; Sion, 1958): with the planner picking an action, the assessor a weight, and the payoff the worst-case aggregate, common-plan acceptance corresponds to the pure-strategy value and per-weight acceptance to the mixed value. The gap is the resulting pure-strategy duality gap that the convex (mixed) extension closes. This directly mirrors the distinction between here-and-now and wait-and-see formulations in adjustable robust optimization (Ben-Tal et al., 2004).

\subsubsection{4.11 The converse: discrete time-sharing does not erase the gap}\label{the-converse-discrete-time-sharing-does-not-erase-the-gap}

\textbf{Proposition 9 (sequential time-sharing does not erase the gap).} \emph{On the witness datum of Section 4.5, let a sequential time-shared policy alternate between FAST and SLOW over the interval, such that the set of visited states is the union of the two primitive worst-case trajectory tubes. Then such a sequential policy is typed-admissible if and only if \(s_1 \ge 2\) and \(s_2 \ge 2\). Across the impossibility region \(I\) (where \(s_1 < 2\), \(s_2 < 2\), and \(s_1 + s_2 > 2\)), no such policy is typed-admissible. The acceptance gap therefore survives discrete time-sharing.}

\emph{Proof.} 
A sequential policy that allocates any positive duration to FAST visits every \(s_1\) in \([s_1 - 2, s_1]\). Similarly, allocating any positive duration to SLOW visits every \(s_2\) in \([s_2 - 2, s_2]\). 

The visited set of the sequential policy contains the union of these two tubes. For this union tube to remain within \(S_0 = \{x \ge 0, s \ge 0\}\), both coordinates must remain non-negative throughout their respective deficits, which requires \(s_1 \ge 2\) and \(s_2 \ge 2\). 

Across the impossibility region \(I\), both coordinates are strictly below 2, so the union tube is never contained within \(S_0\), and no sequential policy is typed-admissible. Because every member of the finite deterministic menu is typed-admissible on \(I\) only in the per-weight sense of Theorem 5(6) (and never in the common sense), the noncompensatory accepted set cannot be extended to \(I\) via sequential time-sharing. \ensuremath{\square}

\textbf{Takeaway.} Together, Theorem 8 and Proposition 9 delimit the notion of mixing with mathematical precision. Continuous convexification—the fractional, simultaneous allocation of control flows—closes the acceptance gap exactly on the compensatory region \(s_1 + s_2 \ge 2\). By contrast, discrete time-sharing—alternating policies sequentially in time, whose visited set is the union of the action tubes—closes it only where the two deficits are independently absorbed (\(s_1 \ge 2\) and \(s_2 \ge 2\)). 

On the intermediate regime where \(s_1 + s_2 \ge 2\) with \(\min(s_1, s_2) < 2\) (which contains the impossibility region), the gap survives sequential time-sharing and closes only under convexification. The structural character of the gap is therefore a property of the \emph{convexity of the action space}, not of the assessment doctrines themselves and not of temporal sharing as such.

\subsection{5. Interpretation}\label{interpretation}

\subsubsection{5.1 The doctrinal reading}\label{the-doctrinal-reading}

The scalarized operators \(\{E_w\}_{w \in W_+}\) formalize \textbf{one} specific compensatory assessment doctrine. This doctrine comprises: a single aggregate index \(w \cdot s\); non-negative scalarization weights across capital forms; substitution across floors permitted at those weights; and robust protection against disturbances. We refer to this construction as \emph{the scalarized aggregate doctrine as formalized here}, not as weak sustainability in its entirety. The broader weak-sustainability literature encompasses concepts not modeled by our linear operators, including consumption smoothing, dynamic shadow pricing, intertemporal investment criteria, and endogenous discount rates (Neumayer, 2013; World Bank, 2011; Boos, 2015; Dasgupta and M\"aler, 2000; Asheim, 1994). 

Similarly, strong sustainability is not always equivalent to requiring every stock coordinate to remain non-negative at every instant. Frameworks based on critical natural capital frequently rely on safe operating spaces, systemic irreversibility, resilience metrics, or minimum service conditions (Ekins et al., 2003; Doyen and Gajardo, 2020). The typed operator \(E_{\mathrm{typ}}\) serves as a formal idealization of the separately-binding-floor reading, in the same sense that the scalarized operators idealize one linear aggregation doctrine.

Within this precise scope, Theorem 5 demonstrates that the two formalized doctrines can arrive at opposing verdicts on the exact same transition problem—sharing identical disturbance models, action sets, and robustness standards—across a relatively open region of state space. 

This disagreement is not an artifact of an unfortunate weight selection: every weight in the cone accepts, but each licenses a different physical transition. The licensed actions represent physically incompatible trajectories: FAST and SLOW violate different service floors at different times. This divergence is the dynamic formalization of substitution across floors. By Proposition 3(ii), the precise seat of the disagreement is the noncommutativity of ``choose an action'' with ``for all weights.''

Because full-cone aggregation along any fixed path is entirely lossless (Remark 2), the limitation of the aggregate doctrine does not lie in using an index per se. Rather, it lies in the \emph{policy dependence} of the aggregate-feasible transition: the index certifies a set of transitions, none of which the noncompensatory assessment accepts. 

This noncommutation also carries an information-theoretic interpretation. The ordering \(\exists a\, \forall w\) represents a commitment made before the evaluation weight is known—reflecting the strong doctrine's demand for precautionary robustness. Conversely, \(\forall w\, \exists a_w\) allows the action to be chosen after the weight is revealed. On our witness system, the acceptance gap between the two orders measures the value of information regarding the assessment weight. 

Theorem 8 qualifies this reading: because a single blended action satisfies every weight on the impossibility region, the value of weight information reflects the finite deterministic menu rather than an inherent property of the weights themselves. Proposition 9 sharpens this point: that value of information is removed by convexifying the action space, not by sharing actions in time—under discrete time-sharing, the gap (and hence the value of weight information) survives across the impossibility region.

Throughout this paper, elements of \(W_+\) are designated as non-negative scalarization weights, reserving the term ``prices'' for broader interpretive discussions. True economic prices may be strictly positive, endogenously determined, time-varying, state-dependent, or dimensionally heterogeneous—properties not required by our theorems. Our operators evaluate linear combinations with weights held constant across the review interval. Non-linear indices—such as constant elasticity of substitution (CES) functions or state-dependent weights that diverge near zero-stock thresholds—represent different mathematical operators and are outside the scope of our results.

\subsubsection{5.2 Positioning against established theory}\label{positioning-against-established-theory}

\textbf{Established foundations.} The backward recursion in Section 3 is a typed instance of established robust-predecessor, reach-avoid, capture-basin, and hybrid-reachability constructions (Aubin, 1991; Aubin, Bayen, and Saint-Pierre, 2011; Saint-Pierre, 1994; Lygeros, Tomlin, and Sastry, 1999). Proposition 3(i) expresses standard constraint monotonicity for viability kernels under nested constraint sets (Aubin, 1991; Frankowska, 1989). 

The thesis of weak comparability—that environmental values cannot be collapsed into a single metric without loss—was articulated by Martinez-Alier, Munda, and O'Neill (1998); our operators provide one formal dynamical model of the distinction that paper draws between strong and weak commensurability. The conditions under which sustainability goals admit scalar indicator representations, along with the operationalization of strong sustainability via maximin and maximum sustainable yield reasoning, are developed in Martinet (2011), Cairns and Martinet (2014), and Doyen and Gajardo (2020). In particular, Doyen and Gajardo show that multi-criteria maximin paths solve a static Pareto problem over a viability kernel—formalizing strong sustainability as constraint viability rather than dynamic optimization, precisely as our typed operator does here. 

The limitations of linear scalarization in static optimization are well known: weighted sums cannot access non-convex regions of a Pareto frontier (Das and Dennis, 1997). That limitation is a geometric property of trade-off surfaces under single-objective optimization, distinct from the dynamic quantifier noncommutation analyzed here. Finally, compensability analysis is well established in multi-criteria decision analysis, including the explicit mapping of compensatory aggregation to weak sustainability and outranking methods to strong sustainability (Cinelli, Coles, and Kirwan, 2014; Sch\"ar, Pohl, and Geldermann, 2025). Scalarization-dependent optimal policies are a standard feature of multi-objective optimization.

\textbf{Contributions of this paper.}
\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
\tightlist
\item The action-set identity \(E_{\mathrm{typ}} = \bigcap_{w \in W_+} E_w\), with the full-cone choice isolating the separation as purely dynamic (Proposition 3(ii)).
\item The general quantifier-separation principle (Remark 1).
\item The weight-family monotonicity theorem (Proposition 4).
\item The explicit exact-tube witness with an open region of strict separation and the rescue/impossibility split (Theorem 5).
\item The proof that the witness hierarchy persists under hold-prefix extension (Remark 6).
\item The explicit two-stage erasure datum delimiting that persistence (Theorem 7).
\item The blend-collapse theorem, showing that action-space convexification resolves the gap precisely across the compensatory region (Theorem 8), paired with the proof that discrete time-sharing closes it only where both deficits are independently absorbed—leaving the gap intact across the impossibility region (Proposition 9).
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Novelty qualification.} To our knowledge, this work offers the first explicit exact-tube witness demonstrating this dynamic quantifier separation within a compensatory/noncompensatory assessment setting. We assert no priority over the elementary quantifier fact \(\exists a\, \forall w \neq \forall w\, \exists a_w\), which is standard. Whether an equivalent dynamical separation has appeared in adjacent literatures (such as robust multi-objective control, viability theory, or multi-criteria decision analysis) represents a bounded absence in our review, not an established universal negative.

\subsubsection{5.3 Scope delimitations}\label{scope-delimitations}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{No scalarization blindness along fixed trajectories.} By Remark 2, evaluated along any fixed trajectory, full-cone linear aggregation preserves componentwise non-negativity without distortion.
\item
  \textbf{Separation is not universal.} Where a single action is safe for all weights, the compensatory and noncompensatory assessments coincide. The theorem is an existence separation establishing an open region of divergence, supported by structural monotonicity and localization.
\item
  \textbf{Model assumptions.} All formal statements apply to finite-horizon systems governed by exact-tube semantics and specified disturbance sets. They do not treat infinite horizons, stochastic chance constraints, partial state observation, or endogenous event timing.
\item
  \textbf{Weight cone generality.} We make no claim that the full non-negative cone is the only valid weight family. Proposition 4 shows that restricting the weight family enlarges the compensatory accepted set; the full cone is simply the strictest compensatory standard, and the separation persists a fortiori for restricted families.
\item
  \textbf{Non-normative weights.} Scalarization weights model an assessment doctrine; they do not imply a normative endorsement or welfare claim regarding market prices.
\item
  \textbf{Theoretical, not empirical.} The theorems characterize assessment operators on a specified mathematical datum; they imply no empirical claim about any specific resource system.
\item
  \textbf{Relationship to wealth accounting.} The operator \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{weak}}\) models a strict compensatory standard across all linear weights. It is not an exact implementation of genuine savings or inclusive wealth metrics, and the reserve stock in our witness model is excluded from the weighted aggregate by construction (Section 4.5), not by consequence of aggregation.
\item
  \textbf{Action-set relativity.} The impossibility region is certified as infeasible strictly relative to the defined four-action menu; with a larger menu it can shrink or vanish (see the completeness requirement in Section 5.5).
\item
  \textbf{No doctrinal ranking.} The inclusion \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{typ}} \subseteq \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{weak}} \subseteq \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{phys}}\) characterizes the mathematical behavior of these operators under shared action and disturbance classes, shared safe-set inclusion, shared horizons, exact-tube semantics, and terminal conditions. It does not establish a universal ranking among sustainability doctrines.
\item
  \textbf{Disturbance structure.} The witness system relies on an action-indexed disturbance convention (Section 4.5): each action's worst case is its own characteristic deficit. A datum whose disturbance class forces simultaneous deficits across all floors of every action degenerates the per-weight licensing structure—the primary actions fail together and the compensatory/noncompensatory divergence collapses into universal rejection. The separation is exhibited for, and scoped to, the specified action-indexed class.
\item
  \textbf{Convexification and time-sharing.} Fractional action policies are not members of the primitive action space, and every stated separation is scoped to the finite deterministic menu. Theorem 8 resolves the convexification question: the blend family collapses the typed accepted set exactly onto the compensatory region, showing that the separation is a property of the deterministic menu, not of the doctrines—a matter of menu geometry, distinct from the Pareto-frontier geometry of scalarization limits (Das and Dennis, 1997). The associated delimitation (Proposition 9) confirms that discrete time-sharing does not effect this collapse: across the impossibility region the gap survives alternation in time, so the structural character of the separation is due to the convexity of the action space, not to temporal sharing as such.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{5.4 Policy implications}\label{policy-implications}

The separation theorem yields four direct implications for sustainability governance and indicator design.

\textbf{First.} Aggregate indices alone cannot certify noncompensatory transition safety under the present assessment semantics. A scalarized assessment can certify its own criterion—aggregate feasibility; what it cannot certify is typed safety unless an additional bridge theorem is supplied. This follows directly from Theorem 5: the compensatory accepted set strictly contains the noncompensatory accepted set, and the excess region is where the two criteria diverge.

\textbf{Second.} When a noncompensatory dynamic recursion identifies an admissible rescue action whose feasibility is governed by a physical resource margin, investing in that margin is a candidate remedy; recalculating weights alone cannot substitute for it on the witness datum (Section 5.5). This applies wherever a resource-controlled rescue action exists; it is not a universal policy claim.

\textbf{Third.} Per-floor reporting alongside aggregate reporting is necessary for detecting this class of discrepancy. It is not universally necessary for every sustainability judgment; but where the question is whether a transition respects separately binding floors, an aggregate index alone cannot distinguish the rescue set from the impossibility region. A minimum reporting standard for such an assessment should disclose:
\begin{enumerate}[(1)]
\tightlist
\item typed floors and their legal, physical, or normative provenance;
\item aggregate weights or the permitted weight family;
\item policy quantifier structures;
\item disturbance sets and environmental bounds;
\item observation and implementation models;
\item exact-tube versus conservative tracking semantics;
\item terminal maintainability guarantees;
\item action-set completeness status;
\item uncertainty tolerances and approximation bounds; and
\item underlying normative premises.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Fourth.} Assessment regimes that inspect conditions only at periodic reporting boundaries (such as audited annual snapshots) evaluate only the weakest operator in our hierarchy (\(E_{\mathrm{end}}\)). Under these snapshot semantics, they license transitions that violate typed safety floors mid-interval without detection. On our witness datum, FAST is typed-endpoint-admissible from every state in \(X_0\), yet it is typed-tube-admissible only when \(s_1 \ge 2\) (by inspection of the action table in Section 4.5); the disaggregated reporting outlined in the third implication detects this breach. No empirical claim about any specific reporting regime is made here.

\subsubsection{5.5 Rescue as action synthesis, and data requirements}\label{rescue-as-action-synthesis-and-data-requirements}

\textbf{Rescue as action synthesis.} Rescue operations act directly on the impossibility region. Consider a resource-augmentation mapping:
\[\mathsf{Aug}_\kappa : (x, s, \mathcal{A}) \mapsto (x + \kappa,\; s,\; \mathcal{A} \cup \{\mathrm{STAGED}_\kappa\}),\]
and define the minimal rescue threshold as:
\[\kappa^* = \inf\{\, \kappa : \exists a \in \mathcal{A}_\kappa,\; a \in E_{\mathrm{typ}}(z) \,\},\]
where \(\mathcal{A}_\kappa\) denotes the augmented menu. 

\textbf{Proposition (rescue threshold).} \emph{On the witness datum, for the STAGED action and \(x < 1\), the minimal rescue threshold is \(\kappa^* = 1 - x\).} 

\emph{Proof.} STAGED is typed-admissible if and only if its reserve trajectory remains non-negative throughout the interval, which requires \(x + \kappa \ge 1\). The least such non-negative increment is \(1 - x\). \ensuremath{\square}

Injecting this capital increment converts an impossible state into a typed-transformable one. States already belonging to the rescue set \(R\) require no augmentation, because STAGED is already typed-admissible there; this explains why \(R\) is not part of the acceptance gap.

\textbf{Data requirements for empirical application.} The separation theorem characterizes assessment operators on a specified mathematical datum. Translating these results to empirical resource systems requires five additional specifications:

\begin{enumerate}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Action-set completeness status.} An impossibility certificate over a finite action set proves impossibility only relative to that set. An empirical application must explicitly state whether \(\mathcal{A}\) is exhaustive, a listed policy menu, a discrete sample, or an inner approximation. If incomplete, the verdict is ``no safe transition exists among the listed actions,'' not ``no safe transition exists.''
\item
  \textbf{Calibration and identifiability data.} Empirical models require parameter estimates, structured uncertainty sets, structural alternatives, validation data, observation error models, missing-data treatments, and disturbance dependencies. Specifying a formal disturbance set is necessary but not sufficient; it must be empirically justified, or the assessment verdict marked as conditional on model credibility.
\item
  \textbf{Policy and authority data.} Institutional context governs execution: who has the legal or operational authority to deploy each action, what information they observe, decision latencies, enforcement assumptions, compliance uncertainty, strategic responses, and legitimacy constraints.
\item
  \textbf{Threshold-uncertainty semantics.} Critical physical or normative thresholds are rarely known with certainty. Analysts must distinguish deterministic floors (\(s_i(z) \ge 0\)) from robustly bounded thresholds (\(s_i(z;\theta) \ge 0\) for all \(\theta \in \Theta\)) and probabilistic chance constraints.
\item
  \textbf{Destination maintainability status.} Evidence that future viability is maintainable within the destination set \(G\) must be documented—whether established through physical laws, dynamic simulation, or policy assumptions.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{6. Discussion}\label{discussion}

\subsubsection{6.1 Negative certificates}\label{negative-certificates}

In transition assessment, a negative certificate—a proof that every action in the available menu violates at least one non-negotiable constraint—is a complete and informative verdict, not an inconclusive one. Theorem 5(7) is a concrete instance: four actions, four exhibited constraint violations, certifying structural impossibility across region \(I\), supported by the resource threshold of Section 5.5 and the per-weight licensing thresholds of Theorem 5(6). 

Related forecast-evaluation studies apply assessment discipline of this kind to historical resource collapses, such as the Northern cod fishery and the Edwards Aquifer system (Abaee, 2026b, 2026c). The mathematical separations established here rest independently on their analytical proofs and on the machine-checked rational grid verification of Section 4.9.

\subsubsection{6.2 Limitations}\label{limitations}

\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
\tightlist
\item
  The separation theorem is an existence result establishing an open region of divergence; it does not measure the volume of this region in any specific empirical application, and where weak and strong assessments coincide, it yields no distinction.
\item
  Novelty assertions reflect a bounded review of adjacent literatures (Section 5.2).
\item
  The formal operators cover finite horizons under exact-tube semantics and specified disturbance sets; infinite horizons, partial state observation, stochastic chance constraints, and endogenous event times are not treated.
\item
  No empirical claims are made regarding specific ecosystems or economies.
\item
  Extensions addressing complex governance hierarchies, intergenerational planning, and composite system architectures are reported with partial status in the Supplementary Material and are not used in the formal proofs.
\item
  \textbf{Computational tractability.} On a finite state-action-disturbance graph with constant-time constraint evaluation, backward dynamic recursion runs in polynomial time relative to graph size and horizon length. However, for continuous, hybrid, or belief-state models, the reachable state space can become exponentially large, infinite, or undecidable; exact tube inclusion may be computationally hard; and grid cardinality scales exponentially with dimension (\(N_{\mathrm{grid}} = \prod_{i=1}^n N_i\)) when floors are state coordinates. The witness system is computationally tractable because its datum is low-dimensional, finite, and rational.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{7. Conclusions}\label{conclusions}

Weak and strong sustainability are traditionally compared as philosophical doctrines—as differing normative stances on the substitutability of natural capital. This paper demonstrates that the divergence between the two doctrines as formalized here—the scalarized-aggregate and typed operators of Section 3.1 operating on a shared action menu and disturbance class—survives translation into dynamic assessment mechanics as a concrete mathematical theorem. 

The theorem establishes no ranking between doctrines: Section 5.1 delimits the formalizations, and Theorem 8 proves that the structural character of the separation is a property of the finite deterministic menu, not an inherent divide between the traditions themselves (Sections 4.10--4.11). 

On a typed transition datum under exact-tube semantics, compensatory evaluation (per-weight acceptance) and noncompensatory evaluation (common-plan acceptance) differ by a quantifier exchange that fails to commute across an open region of state space. Where this noncommutation occurs, every scalarization weight certifies a viable transition, yet no single transition is certified by all weights simultaneously. The certified set splits into states rescuable through a dedicated, resource-backed action and states that represent structural impossibilities under the existing action menu. The mechanism is not static index blindness—along any fixed trajectory, full-cone aggregation is completely lossless—but the policy-dependent nature of aggregate certification.

For the construction and deployment of composite sustainability indicators, this finding has a direct practical consequence. An aggregate index can be methodologically sound as an accounting metric and yet over-certify safety as an assessment tool, because certification is a quantifier statement about dynamic transitions, not a static property of a composite score. 

Where separately binding safety thresholds are present, reporting disaggregated, per-floor trajectories alongside aggregate indicators is not a stylistic preference but an operational requirement: aggregate indices alone cannot distinguish the rescue set from the impossibility region. The bridge that any composite index requires—exhibiting a single physical transition that satisfies all admissible weights simultaneously (membership in \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{typ}}\))—is precisely what sustainability reporting conventions must be asked to provide. 

Theorem 8 clarifies the nature of this bridge: such a unified plan may not exist within a discrete deterministic menu, requiring instead a convexified control strategy. The central question for transition design is whether a system's physical controls admit continuous blending—because, as Proposition 9 confirms, simple temporal switching between discrete policies cannot close the gap.
