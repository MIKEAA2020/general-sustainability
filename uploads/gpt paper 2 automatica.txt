# Line-level technical review

## Overall verdict

The manuscript has a potentially publishable core, but it is not yet theorem-safe. The central idea—common-action infeasibility at an information state, embedded in finite-horizon belief recursion—is sound and useful. However, several claims currently overreach the stated assumptions, and two results are materially false as written:

1. **Theorem 6 (static-observation completeness)** is false because “static observation” does not imply a constant control; the controller may use time-dependent open-loop controls, and the result is also stated for the wrong set of initial beliefs.
2. **The certainty-equivalence claim in Remark 3** is false or undefined unless the policy class is reduced to a specified singleton or tightly constrained family. Injective biased observation by itself does not empty the output-feedback viability kernel.

There are also serious consistency problems involving continuous-time review semantics, belief sufficiency, the delayed-information theorem, the finite obstruction-tree interpretation, and the finite-checkability claims. I would not submit this version without restructuring the framework and re-proving the principal results.

---

# 1. Highest-priority fatal issues

## 1.1 Theorem 6 is false as stated

### Location
Section 7, Theorem 6:

> “A static observation makes every observation-based policy a nonanticipative function of a constant record, hence an open-loop constant \(u_0\).”

This implication is invalid. A policy may depend on time even when the observation record is constant. At minimum, it may implement a measurable open-loop signal \(u(t)\), not necessarily a constant action.

For example, with a constant observation and a known clock, the policy can use a periodic or piecewise-constant control. Such a control may preserve viability even when no single constant control satisfies the displayed tangency condition.

The theorem also conflates:

- the whole constraint set \(V\);
- the initial belief \(B_0\);
- the set of states reachable from \(B_0\) under an open-loop control.

The stated boundary condition on all of \(\partial V\) characterizes robust invariance of the entire set under one fixed action, not viability of an arbitrary initial belief \(B_0\).

### Required correction
Replace Theorem 6 by an exact open-loop statement. For a static observation and a given belief \(B_0\), define

\[
\mathcal U_{\mathrm{ol}}(B_0)=
\left\{u(\cdot): u(t)\in\bigcap_{x\in B_t}U(x)\ \text{a.e.}\right\},
\]

where \(B_t\) is the set of states generated by all compatible initial states and disturbances under the common control history. Then

\[
B_0\in\mathscr K_{\mathcal I}
\iff
\exists u(\cdot)\in\mathcal U_{\mathrm{ol}}(B_0)
\text{ such that every compatible trajectory remains in }V.
\]

If you want a constant-control theorem, explicitly impose that the policy class is restricted to constant controls. Then the conclusion is a statement about robust invariance under a constant control, not general static-observation viability.

A counterexample should be added to show why static observation does not imply constant control.

## 1.2 Remark 3 does not establish \(\operatorname{Viab}(V;\Pi_{CE})=\varnothing\)

### Location
Section 4.2, Remark 3.

You define \(\Pi_{CE}\) as:

> “the class of causal controllers obtained by taking a fixed perfect-information regulation law \(k\) of the plant and applying it directly to the uncorrected observation.”

Then you analyze the particular member \(k=g\). Showing that the particular controller

\[
u=g(\hat S)=g(S+b)
\]

fails does not show that the entire class \(\Pi_{CE}\) fails.

If \(\Pi_{CE}\) contains arbitrary perfect-information laws \(k\), it may contain a law that compensates for the known bias, for example \(k(r)=g(r-b)\), unless that law is explicitly excluded. It may also contain another law that maintains viability for the chosen interval.

### Required correction
Choose one of these formulations:

**Option A: singleton policy class**

Define

\[
\Pi_{\mathrm{uncorr}}=\{k\circ O:k=g\},
\]

and prove that this particular controller fails.

**Option B: explicitly restricted class**

Define a class of regulation laws that excludes bias compensation, for example by imposing a fixed parametric form, monotonicity, or calibration rule. Then prove failure for every member of that class.

**Option C: policy-specific statement**

State only:

> The uncorrected certainty-equivalence controller \(u=g(\hat S)\) fails, although the corrected output-feedback controller \(u=g(\hat S-b)\) succeeds.

This is a valid and useful example, but it is not a theorem that injective biased observation empties the output-feedback viability kernel.

Also replace the notation

\[
\operatorname{Viab}(V;U,\Pi_{CE})
\]

with a definition that makes clear whether this means viability under **some** policy in the class or viability under **all** policies in the class. The manuscript alternates between these readings.

## 1.3 The continuous-time information model is internally inconsistent

### Locations
Sections 2.3, 2.4, Theorems 2–4.

The manuscript simultaneously says:

- the plant evolves continuously;
- the policy observes \(y(t)=O(x(t))\);
- the action is selected at review times and held until the next review;
- the belief is updated from the entire continuous observation record;
- no informative observation arrives before the next action is selected.

These are different information structures. If \(O(x(t))\) is continuously observed, information can usually accrue inside a held-action interval even though the action cannot change. If observations arrive only at review times, the record should be indexed by review times rather than all \(s\le t\).

### Required correction
Choose one formal model:

1. **Sampled observation and sampled control:** observations \(y_k\) and actions \(a_k\), with a discrete transition map.
2. **Continuous observation with held control:** observations arrive continuously, the action is held, and the belief evolves continuously during the interval.
3. **Event-triggered informative observation:** formally define the observation stopping time and specify what records are identical before it.

The finite-horizon theorem is easiest to make rigorous in Model 1. The continuous-time local and timing certificates can then be presented as separate sampled-data or held-input results.

Do not use the phrase “no informative observation arrives” unless you define whether:

- no observation arrives at all;
- observations arrive but are identical across all relevant branches;
- observations arrive but cannot change the held action;
- the policy is prohibited from using within-interval information.

These lead to different theorems.

# 2. Belief-state and policy semantics

## 2.1 The belief definition is circular unless the common control history is explicit

### Location
Section 2.3:

\[
B_t=\{\xi(t):\xi(0)\in B_0,\ \dot\xi=f(\xi,u,d),\ O(\xi(s))=y(s)\}.
\]

The candidate trajectories use \(u(s)\), but under partial observation the relevant fact is that indistinguishable candidate trajectories must receive the **same applied control history**. The belief cannot be defined using an unspecified \(u\) unless the applied control is part of the observed record or is recursively generated by the policy.

### Required correction
Define the information history as

\[
h_t=(y|_{[0,t]},u|_{[0,t)}),
\]

or its sampled analogue. Then define

\[
B_t(h_t)=\{x_t:\text{there exists a compatible initial state, disturbance, and trajectory producing }h_t\}.
\]

State explicitly that the applied control history is known to the regulator and is identical across all trajectories compatible with the same history.

If you want to use \(B_t\) as a sufficient statistic, prove or cite the Markov/sufficiency property under your chosen observation and disturbance model. The sentence

> “under the set-membership semantics the information set is a sufficient statistic”

is not true without qualification when there are delays, hidden parameters, control constraints depending on history, non-Markov observations, or implementation memory.

## 2.2 The kernel notation and hierarchy need to be repaired

### Location
Section 2.1.

The manuscript uses \(\operatorname{Viab}\), \(\operatorname{RViab}\), \(\operatorname{ERViab}\), and \(\operatorname{IRViab}\) with shifting meanings. In particular:

- \(\operatorname{Viab}(V;U,\pi_{perf})\) appears to mean existence of a full-information policy against disturbances;
- \(\operatorname{RViab}(V)\) is also defined using a state-feedback policy against every disturbance;
- the distinction between the first two is therefore unclear;
- the existential projection \(\operatorname{proj}_\exists\) is introduced for collections of beliefs but then used in a hierarchy that mixes physical-state and information-state objects.

### Required correction
Define each object with explicit quantifiers. For example:

\[
\mathcal K_{\mathrm{FI}}
=\{x_0:\exists \pi_{\mathrm{FI}}\ \forall d(\cdot),\ x(t)\in V\},
\]

\[
\mathcal K_{\mathrm{OF}}
=\{B_0:\exists \pi_{\mathrm{OF}}\ \forall x_0\in B_0\ \forall d(\cdot),\ x(t)\in V\}.
\]

Then state separately whether the disturbance is open-loop, nonanticipative, or allowed to react to the current control. Do not call both full-information viability and robust full-information viability different kernels unless their quantifier structures differ.

The claim

\[
\operatorname{proj}_\exists(\mathcal K_{\mathrm{OF}})\subseteq \mathcal K_{\mathrm{FI}}
\]

also requires care: a belief-level policy may use information that is unavailable to a state-feedback policy only if the policy is correctly projected. State the exact mapping and prove the inclusion.

# 3. Theorems 1–4

## 3.1 Theorem 1: drift inequality and adverse selection

The basic idea is correct, but the proof currently assumes more than the hypotheses state.

### Problems

1. \(D_\varepsilon(x,u)\) is not formally defined.
2. Lower semicontinuity of \(D\) does not by itself guarantee all properties needed for a measurable adverse selection after composing with the state/control dependence.
3. The inequality
   \[
   \sup_u\inf_d D^+q(x;f(x,u,d))\le -\varepsilon
   \]
   must be converted carefully into a measurable disturbance selection along every measurable control.
4. The trajectory must remain in the strip until exit; this should be stated in the proof rather than assumed implicitly.
5. If \(q(x_0)=0\), the notation
   \[
   \inf\{t:q(x(t))<0\}\le q(x_0)/\varepsilon=0
   \]
   is formally an infimum statement, not an assertion that strict violation occurs at time zero. Clarify this distinction.

### Required correction
State a standard measurable-selection hypothesis, or formulate the result directly for a differential inclusion:

\[
\dot x(t)\in F(x(t),u(t)),
\]

with

\[
\sup_{u\in U(x)}\sup_{v\in F_{\mathrm{adv}}(x,u)}D^+q(x;v)\le -\varepsilon.
\]

Be precise about the order of \(\sup\) and \(\inf\). Under the disturbance-after-control convention, the relevant pointwise condition is

\[
\forall u\in U(x)\ \exists d\in D(x):
D^+q(x;f(x,u,d))\le -\varepsilon,
\]

which is represented by \(\sup_u\inf_d\), but the measurable realization is an additional theorem-level issue.

Also remove the sentence that the convexified inclusion “preserves the drift inequality because the constraint is convex in velocity” unless you explicitly note that for \(C^1\) \(q\), the directional derivative is affine in the velocity. Convexity of the constraint set itself is not the relevant fact.

---

## 3.2 Theorem 2: distinguish instantaneous tangency from finite-time failure

The common-action obstruction is the strongest central result, but its current proof overstates what a negative tangency derivative gives.

If \(a\notin R_V(\bar x)\), then there is a boundary point and disturbance with negative first-order derivative. Under suitable regularity, this gives local exit for a trajectory starting exactly at \(\bar x\). It does not automatically imply that every trajectory from every compatible state exits, nor that an arbitrary disturbance selection can remain in the required state-dependent disturbance set without additional hypotheses.

The theorem only needs one compatible state to refute robust viability, so state that explicitly:

\[
\exists \bar x\in B,\ \exists d(\cdot)
\quad\text{such that the trajectory from }\bar x\text{ exits.}
\]

Do not write as though the entire belief is driven out.

Also, if the selected action is held over a positive interval, the exact certificate is the tube set

\[
\mathcal A_{\mathrm{tube}}(B,\Delta)=\varnothing.
\]

The tangency test is only a sufficient local exclusion test. Make this distinction prominent in the theorem statement, not only in a later paragraph.

---

## 3.3 Proposition 3: check the quantifiers and the reach definition

The proposition is plausible, but its hypotheses need to say that for every common admissible action \(a\), the selected \(x_a\) is a state from which the held-action reachable set includes an adverse trajectory. The proof should not suggest that continuity alone extends a pointwise disturbance choice into a valid disturbance field.

Define explicitly whether

\[
\operatorname{Reach}_{[0,\Delta]}(B,a)
\]

contains all initial states in \(B\), all disturbances, and all solutions. Under nonunique solutions, specify whether reachability is existential or universal over solutions. For a robust safety condition it must be universal.

The phrase

> “Atube(B,\Delta)=\varnothing\ \forall\Delta>0”

also requires that the same adverse construction is valid for every sufficiently small interval and that the trajectory solution exists until exit.

---

## 3.4 Proposition 4: the ladder is not a completeness result

The inclusions

\[
\mathcal A_{\mathrm{tube}}(B,\Delta)
\subseteq R_V^B(B)
\subseteq U^B(B)
\]

are conceptually right under appropriate regularity, but the text sometimes treats the ladder as if emptiness at one rung characterizes the next. It does not.

In particular:

- nonempty common admissibility does not imply nonempty common tangency;
- nonempty common tangency does not imply tube safety;
- nonempty tube safety does not imply recursive viability.

Use “nested necessary conditions” rather than “nested response sets” unless all sets are defined on exactly the same action space and under the same held-action semantics.

The symbol \(\mathcal A_N(B)\) appears in the ladder but is not defined before use. Define it or remove it.

---

## 3.5 Theorem 3: the blind-window hypothesis is close to circular

### Location
Section 3.4.

Hypothesis (H4.2) quantifies over every open-loop control and assumes that an adverse state and disturbance enforce the stated drift along the realized trajectory. This can be a valid theorem template, but it is nearly the desired conclusion encoded as a hypothesis.

There are also three technical problems:

1. The admissible control class is written as
   \[
   u(\cdot):[0,T_{obs})\to\bigcup_{x\in B_0}U(x),
   \]
   but this only imposes pointwise membership in a union. A control implementable under partial observation must be admissible at every state compatible with the observation history, not merely at some state in the union.
2. The initial true state cannot be selected after observing the policy’s control unless the robust nonviability quantifiers are written carefully. The correct order is: for every policy, there exists a compatible initial state and disturbance.
3. “No informative observation” is not enough unless all branches relevant to the adversarial construction produce identical records under the policy.

### Required correction
Define the blind-window policy class directly:

\[
\Pi_{\mathrm{blind}}(B_0,T)
=\{u(\cdot):u(t)\text{ is measurable and admissible for every state compatible with the pre-}T\text{ record}\}.
\]

Then define

\[
\sigma^*(B_0,T)
=\sup_{u\in\Pi_{\mathrm{blind}}(B_0,T)}
\inf_{x_0\in B_0,\ d(\cdot)}\tau_V(x_0,u,d).
\]

The conclusion

\[
\sigma^*(B_0,T_{obs})<T_{obs}
\implies B_0\notin\mathscr K_{\mathcal I}
\]

is much cleaner than imposing a uniform drift condition for every open-loop control. The drift inequality can then be presented as one computable upper bound on \(\sigma^*\), not as the main theorem’s defining hypothesis.

Also correct the sentence saying that the common-action obstruction is a “zero-margin instance” of the delayed obstruction. These are not simply limits of one another: one is a local action-feasibility condition, while the other is a finite-time survival-value condition.

# 4. Finite-horizon recursion and obstruction trees

## 4.1 Theorem 4 needs an initial-observation convention

The recursion is broadly standard, but the model must say whether the initial observation is already incorporated into \(B_0\), and whether the action is chosen before or after observing the current state/output.

Also specify whether admissibility is checked before transition and whether an inadmissible action is a failure or simply unavailable. The current convention is acceptable, but it must be built into the predecessor definition:

\[
\operatorname{Pre}(\mathcal C)
=\left\{B\subseteq V:\exists a\in A(B),\ \forall y\text{ possible},\ \operatorname{Post}(B,a,y)\in\mathcal C\right\},
\]

where

\[
A(B)=\bigcap_{x\in B}U(x).
\]

If \(B\) is infinite, the condition is not finite-checkable merely because \(A\) and \(D\) are finite; the belief collection contains up to \(2^{|X|}\) beliefs, and the action intersection still needs to be evaluated.

## 4.2 The obstruction-tree description reverses some quantifiers

At a node \((B,k)\notin W_k\), it is true that for every candidate action there exists a successor observation leading outside \(W_{k-1}\). However, candidate actions are regulator branches, not adversarial branches. The tree should be described as an alternating tree:

- regulator chooses an action;
- nature chooses a compatible disturbance/state branch and resulting observation;
- the next belief is formed;
- the process continues.

Calling all branches “adversarial edges” obscures the game quantifiers. The finite certificate is a strategy tree for nature against every regulator action, not a single ordinary adversary tree.

The claim that the tree is “finite” is valid in finite state/action/observation systems, but not in continuous action spaces or infinite belief spaces. The abstract and introduction should not say “finitely checkable in the finite-fibre case” without defining what finite-fibre means and ensuring the relevant fibre is actually finite.

---

# 5. The finite-fibre and finite-checkability claims are overstated

The abstract says the certificates are finitely checkable in the “polyhedral, finite-fibre, and finite-horizon cases.” But Proposition 2’s displayed construction uses

\[
X=[1,2],\qquad O\equiv 0,
\]

so the observation fibre is an interval and therefore infinite. The polyhedral Farkas certificate is finite only when the family of constraints is finite or when a vertex, Helly, or semi-infinite duality theorem reduces it to a finite witness.

You correctly acknowledge this issue later, but the abstract and contribution claims still overstate it.

Replace the claim by something such as:

> In finite models, backward recursion yields finite obstruction trees. In polyhedral models, common-action infeasibility is finitely certified when the relevant robust constraints admit a finite representation; for infinite beliefs, additional vertex, Helly, or semi-infinite-duality assumptions are required.

Do not call the Farkas statement a substantial computational contribution until you provide one of:

- a finite-witness theorem;
- a vertex-reduction theorem;
- a semi-infinite duality result;
- or an algorithm with an exactness guarantee.

---

# 6. Proposition 5: correct the relative preimage notation

The fibre criterion is correct, but the equivalence

\[
K=O^{-1}(O(K))
\]

is only correct relative to the admissible domain \(Z\). In general it should be written

\[
K=Z\cap O^{-1}(O(K)),
\]

assuming \(K\subseteq Z\).

Also distinguish:

- existence of a set-theoretic function \(C:Y\to\{0,1\}\);
- measurability of \(C\);
- measurability of \(O(K)\).

The text mentions this issue briefly but the proposition itself does not. If the paper is about implementable certifiers, state the measurable-space assumptions and prove measurable factorization, or explicitly label the result set-theoretic.

The “certainly-safe set” is a set of observations, not a physical-state region. The sentence calling it “the region where the index may certify safety” should be changed to “the set of observation values that may soundly receive the safe label.”

# 7. Example 1 and the discrete one-step example need separation

The continuous-time hidden-mode example uses

\[
\dot z=\theta u,
\qquad u\in\{-1,+1\},
\qquad z\ge0,
\]

while the finite-horizon example uses

\[
z^+=z+\theta u,
\qquad z\in\{-1,0,1\},
\qquad z\ge0.
\]

These are related but not the same system. State clearly that the second is a discrete abstraction, not “Example 1 read as a finite system,” unless you define the discretization and explain what is preserved.

The discrete observation map is written as \(O(z,\theta)=z\), which reveals \(z\) but not \(\theta\). That is fine, but the initial belief must be described as the belief after observing \(z=0\), and the transition observation convention must be aligned with the earlier definition \(O(x,a,d,x^+)\).

# 8. Case study and coverage audit

## 8.1 The audit is not sufficiently reproducible

The manuscript says it audits the exact recursion, but the delayed hidden-regime model, observation timing, initial belief, and reveal mechanism are not formally incorporated into the recursion used for Table 2.

Specify:

- the augmented state including the hidden regime and reveal clock;
- the exact observation alphabet before and after reveal;
- the action timing;
- the safe set;
- the horizon or infinite-horizon criterion;
- the recursion used to classify each grid point.

Otherwise the table is an asserted calculation rather than a reproducible computational study.

## 8.2 The fishery example does not support the claimed computational contribution

The fishery model is continuous-state, clipped, and adversarial, but no belief-space approximation, algorithm, run time, or computed viability set is reported. The hidden-regime table is symbolic and one-dimensional.

For an Automatica-level claim, add a genuinely multidimensional numerical example with:

- a finite observation partition or delayed sensor;
- an explicit belief-space discretization;
- exact or certified action feasibility checks;
- extracted obstruction certificates;
- comparison with full-information viability;
- sensitivity to observation refinement and review interval.

If this cannot be added, narrow the paper to a theorem paper and remove the claims of a substantial computational calculus.

## 8.3 Check the numerical claim in the certainty-equivalence example

For

\[
\dot S=0.2S+0.01,
\]

starting from \(S(0)=1\), the hitting time of \(S=2\) is

\[
\frac{1}{0.2}\log\frac{0.41}{0.21}\approx 3.34,
\]

so the arithmetic is correct. But the example proves failure of the specified uncorrected controller, not failure of all injective-observation output-feedback controllers.

# 9. Probabilistic section

The finite-horizon belief recursion is standard, but the presentation should be tightened.

## 9.1 Define the value function’s time convention

With

\[
V_0(b)=b(V),
\]

state explicitly that the horizon counts the current state. If the horizon counts transitions only, the base case would instead be 1 on all safe-supported beliefs.

## 9.2 The chance constraint should include the initial condition explicitly

Write

\[
\mathbb P_b(x_0,\ldots,x_k\in V)\ge 1-\varepsilon.
\]

This avoids ambiguity about whether the event includes time zero.

## 9.3 The deterministic-limit claim is too strong

The sentence that \(V_k(b)\) converges to an indicator of whether \(\operatorname{supp}b\in W_k\) requires a precise family of transition and observation kernels, topology, and policy correspondence. It is not an immediate consequence of concentration. Either prove a finite-model convergence statement or present it as an interpretation rather than a theorem-level claim.

Also, “\(\operatorname{supp}b\in W_k\)” is type-ambiguous because \(W_k\) is a collection of beliefs, whereas \(\operatorname{supp}b\) is a subset of states. Use \(\operatorname{supp}b\in\mathcal W_k\) only after defining the identification.

# 10. Claims of novelty and literature positioning

The manuscript repeatedly says that the necessity side has received less systematic treatment. This may be directionally true, but the current novelty claim is not yet defensible because the main ingredients are recognizable as:

- belief-state or estimation-space dynamic programming;
- robust controlled invariance and viability;
- discriminating kernels and capture basins;
- tangency and barrier conditions;
- robust/semi-infinite feasibility duality;
- standard fibre-factorization facts.

The paper’s novelty should be narrowed to a precise theorem or algorithm. The strongest plausible claim is not “a calculus of obstructions” in the abstract, but something like:

> We provide a unified certificate interface for partial-observation viability and prove finite witness or dual-certificate results for a specified class of robust common-action constraints, together with a quantitatively sharp blind-window bound.

That claim is publishable only if at least one of the following is genuinely new and proved:

1. a sparse/Helly witness theorem with explicit cardinality bound;
2. a finite exact dual certificate for a nontrivial continuous belief class;
3. a rigorous sampled-data bridge from local drift margins to review-interval nonviability;
4. a new blind-window value characterization;
5. a complexity result for obstruction-tree extraction.

The fibre criterion and certainty-equivalence example should not be counted as principal theorem-level contributions.

Verify every bibliographic attribution, especially the claims about what Veliov, Cardaliaguet–Quincampoix–Saint-Pierre, Prajna–Rantzer, and Aubin–Catté establish. The sentence “what that literature does not supply is the necessity side” is too broad without a systematic comparison with partial-observation games, discriminating kernels, reach-avoid games, and formal-methods literature.

# 11. Structural and editorial problems

## 11.1 The manuscript is overextended

The paper contains at least six conceptual layers:

- full-information drift failure;
- common-action feasibility;
- tube safety;
- delayed information;
- static certification;
- certainty-equivalence policy restriction;
- finite-horizon recursion;
- probabilistic safety values;
- sustainability governance.

The result is that the central theorem is difficult to identify. For SCL, remove or relegate:

- the broad sustainability motivation;
- the fibre-certification section;
- the certainty-equivalence section;
- the probabilistic development;
- the institutional discussion;
- most continuous-time extensions.

For Automatica, retain them only if the main new theorem and computational method are strengthened substantially.

## 11.2 Numbering and organization are inconsistent

Examples:

- Section 6.3 is referenced in places where Section 6.2 or 6.4 appears intended.
- Section 6.5 says continuous-time completeness is open, while Section 7 presents Theorem 6 as a continuous-time completeness result; this is not necessarily contradictory, but the scope distinction must be explicit.
- The introduction says “five mechanisms” and then lists six, while later calling one a policy-class mechanism and the others observation mechanisms. Use a stable taxonomy.
- The abstract says “two finitely checkable, two closed-form conditional, one minimal construction, with a sixth…” but the correspondence to the numbered results is unclear.
- \(\mathcal A_N\) is used before definition.
- Several symbols are reused with incompatible local meanings, despite the notation section claiming to prevent this.

## 11.3 The extracted PDF contains mathematical typography errors

Before review or submission, correct:

- missing commas in constraint-index ranges;
- malformed inequalities and integrals;
- inconsistent use of \(D^+q\), \(\nabla q\cdot f\), and directional derivatives;
- “finite-fibre” terminology;
- the typo in Mathematics Subject Classification: “91B761” should be checked;
- the title spacing: “ObservationAmin”;
- inconsistent notation for \(\mathcal I\), \(I\), \(\mathcal J\), and \(J\);

The main text should not rely on the Supplementary Material for definitions needed to understand the theorem hypotheses.

# 12. Recommended revised theorem spine

A technically safer paper would be organized around four results.

## Theorem A: exact finite-horizon belief recursion

State and prove this first for finite state, action, disturbance, and observation spaces, with exact timing and quantifiers.

## Theorem B: common-action nonviability

For a belief \(B\), if

\[
\bigcap_{x\in B}U(x)=\varnothing
\]

or

\[
\bigcap_{x\in B}S(x)=\varnothing,
\]

where \(S(x)\) is the one-step robust safe-action set, then \(B\) is not one-step viable. Give the Farkas certificate only under a genuinely finite linear representation.

## Theorem C: blind-window survival bound

Define the implementable pre-observation policy class and the survival value \(\sigma^*\). Prove

\[
\sigma^*(B_0,T)<T
\implies B_0\notin\mathscr K_{\mathcal I}.
\]

Present drift inequalities as computable upper bounds on \(\sigma^*\).

## Theorem D: sparse or dual certificate

This should be the actual novelty theorem. For example:

> If the common safe-action sets are convex subsets of \(\mathbb R^m\), emptiness is witnessed by at most \(m+1\) compatible states under specified compactness assumptions.

Or prove a finite vertex-reduction result for a stated class of polyhedral beliefs.

Everything else should be an example, corollary, or discussion.

# 13. Concrete revision sequence

1. **Freeze the information structure.** Decide between discrete-time observations, sampled-data observations, and continuous observations with held controls.
2. **Rewrite all kernels using explicit quantifiers.** Separate full-information, robust, output-feedback, and policy-restricted viability.
3. **Repair the belief definition.** Include applied controls and prove the sufficient-statistic property under the chosen model.
4. **Delete or replace Theorem 6.** The current theorem is false.
5. **Downgrade Remark 3 to a policy-specific counterexample** unless a genuinely restricted policy class is defined.
6. **Rewrite Theorem 3 using a blind-window value function.** Make the policy class implementable rather than a union-valued open-loop class.
7. **Recast Theorem 4 as the foundational exact result.** Correct the alternating-tree quantifiers.
8. **Remove “finite-fibre” and broad finite-checkability claims** unless supported by an explicit finite-witness theorem.
9. **Strengthen the Farkas/polyhedral contribution** with Helly, vertex reduction, semi-infinite duality, or complexity analysis.
10. **Make the case study reproducible** or remove the coverage-audit claims.
11. **Shorten the abstract and introduction** so that the actual new theorem is visible within the first page.
12. **Perform a complete citation and notation audit.**

## Bottom line

The manuscript has a clear organizing insight: under partial observation, viability requires a response common to all states compatible with the information history, and nonviability can be certified when the relevant common response set is empty. That insight is correct and worth developing.

The current version, however, presents standard belief recursion and elementary selector/fibre facts as a broad new calculus, while several continuous-time and policy-class claims are not valid under the stated assumptions. The paper should be narrowed around an exact finite-horizon theorem, a rigorously defined blind-window certificate, and one genuinely new finite or sparse certificate result. Once those are repaired, the sustainability interpretation and probabilistic extension can be retained as applications or appendices rather than carrying the main novelty claim.
