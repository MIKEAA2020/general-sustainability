qwen:
It over-qualifies itself, overloads notation, uses some misleading language about “necessity,” and mixes control theory with sustainability rhetoric in a way that may distract referees.

# 1. Overall verdict

This paper is **significantly stronger** than the previous one.

Its core idea is clear:

> Under perfect observation, viability theory has a mature sufficiency theory. Under incomplete observation, we need instruments that certify when no observation-based policy can be viable. This paper develops such obstruction certificates.

That is a legitimate applied-control/viability question. It is also well positioned relative to:

- Aubin’s viability theory;
- robust viability;
- Veliov’s output-feedback condition;
- Cardaliaguet–Quincampoix–Saint-Pierre estimation tubes;
- barrier certificates;
- sustainability monitoring and indicator design.

This is much better than the previous paper’s “two sustainability doctrines as quantifier orders” framing. Here, the mathematics is not merely illustrating a conceptual distinction; it is doing real work.

However, the paper still has several serious presentation and positioning problems.

---

# 2. What is good

## 2.1 The problem is well chosen

The distinction between:

- perfect-information viability;
- robust viability;
- observation-based viability;
- institutionally restricted viability;

is natural and useful.

The paper’s central object, the **robust epistemic kernel**, is a sensible object:

\[
\mathrm{ERViab}_{\mathcal I}(\mathcal V)
\]

as the set of information states from which some observation-based policy keeps all compatible trajectories inside the constraint set against all admissible disturbances.

That is a clean partial-observation analogue of the viability kernel.

---

## 2.2 The obstruction-certificate idea is useful

The paper is strongest when it says:

> We do not claim a complete characterization of the epistemic kernel. We give checkable certificates that prove nonviability.

That is a reasonable and publishable contribution.

The five mechanisms are sensible:

1. finite-time exit under adverse drift;
2. admissibility-induced epistemic emptiness;
3. instantaneous common-action obstruction;
4. delayed-information obstruction;
5. observation-fibre certification limit;
6. certainty-equivalence restriction.

Of these, the most interesting are probably:

- the **common-action obstruction**;
- the **delayed-information obstruction**;
- the **fibre certification criterion**.

The finite-time exit certificate is more standard. The certainty-equivalence trap is useful but feels secondary.

---

## 2.3 The literature positioning is much better than in the first paper

The references are appropriate:

- Aubin;
- Aubin, Bayen, Saint-Pierre;
- Aubin and Frankowska;
- Frankowska;
- Veliov;
- Cardaliaguez, Quincampoix, Saint-Pierre;
- Prajna, Jadbabaie, Rantzer;
- Maghenem and Sanfelice;
- Béné, Doyen, De Lara, Gajardo.

This gives the paper a recognizable home in viability theory and safety verification.

That is a major improvement over the previous paper, which mixed ecological economics, MCDA, robust optimization, and quantifier logic without a single clear disciplinary center.

---

## 2.4 The proofs are mostly in the right style

The paper gives actual proofs, not just assertions. The proofs use recognizable tools:

- Dini derivatives;
- measurable selection;
- Nagumo-type tangency;
- Farkas certificates;
- Lyapunov/ω-limit arguments in the appendix.

This is closer to professional mathematical writing than the first manuscript.

# 3. Main weaknesses

The main problem is not the basic idea. The problem is that the paper still reads like a highly defensive preprint trying to preempt every possible objection.

---

## 3.1 The language “necessity side” is misleading

The introduction says:

> What is missing, and what this paper supplies, is the necessity side of the viability question itself under incomplete observation: a calculus of obstruction certificates.

This is too strong and potentially confusing.

What the paper actually gives are **sufficient certificates for nonviability**.

That is, if a certificate applies, then the information state is not viable.

Equivalently, the negation of each certificate is a necessary condition for viability. But the collection of certificates is not a complete necessary-and-sufficient characterization.

The paper itself admits this later:

> The certificates are sound but not complete.

So the phrase “necessity side” should be softened.

Better phrasing:

> This paper develops one-sided obstruction certificates for viability under incomplete observation. Each certificate is a sufficient condition for nonviability; equivalently, its negation is a necessary condition for observation-based viability. The certificates are not claimed to be complete.

That is more precise and less likely to irritate referees.

---

## 3.2 The paper over-qualifies itself

There are too many sentences like:

- “no theorem of this paper is stated for it”;
- “this is a definition, not a theorem”;
- “the contrast class only”;
- “site-local meanings”;
- “statement-local”;
- “appendix-local”;
- “the class for which the proof below is carried out”;
- “not re-derived here”;
- “we do not claim the underlying elementary facts as new”.

Some qualification is good. Too much makes the paper sound anxious.

A professional paper usually states assumptions cleanly and moves on.

For example, instead of repeatedly saying:

> no theorem of this paper is stated for the non-robust counterpart,

write once:

> The paper studies the robust epistemic kernel. The non-robust variant is mentioned only for contrast.

Then do not return to it.

---

## 3.3 The notation section is too long and too defensive

The notation section is one of the weakest parts.

It tries to solve a real problem — many symbols are used — but it does so by cataloguing overloaded symbols:

- \(a\) is strip width in one theorem and held action elsewhere;
- \(\varepsilon\) is drift margin, estimation error, erosion radius;
- \(\lambda\) is Farkas multiplier and observer decay rate;
- \(d\) is disturbance and patch-coupling coefficient;
- \(H\) is observation-error map and harvest vector;
- \(K\) is safe set, robust kernel, buffered set, carrying capacity.

This is not ideal.

A better solution is not to announce “site-local meanings.” A better solution is to **rename symbols** so that they do not collide.

For example:

- use \(\delta\) or \(\gamma\) for patch coupling, not \(d\);
- use \(h\) for harvest, not \(H\);
- use \(C\) or \(\mathcal S\) for safe set, not \(K\) if \(K\) is also kernel;
- use \(\mu\) for Farkas multiplier;
- use \(\eta\) or \(e_{\mathrm{obs}}\) for estimation error;
- use \(\alpha\) for observer decay if \(\lambda\) is already used elsewhere.

A notation section should be short. It should list only the main global symbols.

---

## 3.4 Some results are elementary and should be demoted

Not all six mechanisms have the same weight.

### Theorem 1: finite-time exit certificate

This is basically a Dini-derivative/Isaacs drift argument. It is useful, but it is not highly novel. It should be presented as the base case, not as a major novelty.

### Theorem 5: fibre criterion

This is essentially the statement:

> A function factors through an observation map iff it is constant on observation fibres.

That is a basic fact. It is useful in the sustainability-index context, but it should not be presented as one of the major mathematical theorems. It should be a proposition or lemma.

### Remark 1: certainty-equivalence trap

This is also useful, but it is more of a warning about policy-class restriction than an observation obstruction. It could be moved to a discussion section or an example section.

The strongest parts are Theorems 3 and 4, and possibly Theorem 2 if reframed as an example.

---

## 3.5 Theorem 2 is artificial

Theorem 2 constructs a scalar system with:

\[
U(S)=\{0,r(S)\},
\]

constant observation, and forced common action \(u=0\).

It works, but it feels engineered. The point is valid: non-injective observation can merge states with incompatible admissible controls. But the construction is so minimal that it may seem contrived.

Example 1 is better:

\[
\dot z = \theta u,\quad \theta\in\{-1,+1\},\quad z\ge0.
\]

That is a clean hidden-mode conflict.

I would demote Theorem 2 to an example or combine it with Example 1.

A possible structure:

- Theorem: common-action obstruction.
- Example: admissibility-only minimal example.
- Example: hidden-mode conflict.

That would be cleaner.

---

## 3.6 The delayed-information theorem needs sharper formulation

Theorem 4 is conceptually strong, but hypothesis (H4.2) is heavy:

> For every open-loop control on the blind window, there are a compatible initial state and disturbance enforcing negative drift.

This is a strong assumption and may be hard to verify.

The theorem would be stronger if it were connected to a more standard object, such as:

- a worst-case exit-time value function;
- a differential game value;
- a reachability/avoidability computation;
- a Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman–Isaacs characterization.

For example, one could define a worst-case exit time:

\[
T^*(B_0)
=
\inf_{u(\cdot)}
\sup_{x_0\in B_0,d(\cdot)}
\{ t : q(x(t)) < 0 \}.
\]

Then a cleaner timing condition would be something like:

\[
T_{\mathrm{obs}} > T^*(B_0)
\]

implies nonviability.

That would make the result more checkable and more recognizable to control theorists.

As written, Theorem 4 is correct in spirit, but the hypothesis feels hand-built.

---

## 3.7 The sustainability framing is sometimes too rhetorical

Passages like this are too essayistic for a control/viability journal:

> Such a base can erode while measured output is maintained. The degradation is not yet reflected in the measured quantity, or it is offset by a higher per-unit service, because use may draw on the base rather than on its yield.

This is a nice idea, but it should be compressed.

A mathematical journal would prefer:

> In resource systems, constraints often apply to the productive base rather than to observed output. Because output can be maintained temporarily by drawing down the base, partial observation may fail to detect erosion until a floor is breached.

That is enough.

The rhetorical version may be good for an interdisciplinary journal, but even there it should be shorter.

---

## 3.8 Appendix A is interesting but tangential

Appendix A gives bounded constructions about coupled patch systems.

These are interesting, but they are not really about incomplete observation. They concern:

- coupling creating viability;
- coupling failing to preserve viability at MSY.

That is a different theme.

If the paper is about observation-based viability, Appendix A distracts from the main contribution.

Options:

1. remove it;
2. move it to a separate paper;
3. keep it only as a short remark labeled “unrelated to observation: coupling effects”.

I would not include it in the main submission unless it directly illustrates an obstruction certificate.

# 4. How to improve the paper in a non-superficial way

Here are the changes I would prioritize.

---

## 4.1 Rename the central object

“Robust epistemic kernel” is not bad, but it sounds philosophical.

For control theorists, a more standard name would be:

- robust observation-based viability kernel;
- robust belief-state viability kernel;
- output-feedback viability kernel;
- information-state viability kernel.

I would use something like:

\[
\mathrm{Viab}_{\mathcal I}^{\mathrm{rob}}(\mathcal V)
\]

and call it the **robust observation-based viability kernel**.

You can keep “epistemic” as a secondary term if you want, but do not make it the main terminology.

---

## 4.2 State the unifying idea early

The paper should say early on:

> An observation-based policy can choose only actions that are jointly admissible and jointly safe for all states compatible with the current observation. Therefore, emptiness of the common admissible-action set or common safe-action set is a local obstruction to observation-based viability.

That is the core idea.

Then the theorems become variations:

- instantaneous version;
- delayed version;
- tube version;
- certification version.

That would make the paper much more coherent.

---

## 4.3 Convert the obstruction calculus into a clean hierarchy

I would organize the results as follows.

### Level 1: Dynamic obstruction

If the disturbance can force exit against every control, observation cannot help.

This is Theorem 1.

### Level 2: Instantaneous information obstruction

If the current belief contains compatible states with no common safe action, then no observation-based policy can be viable at that belief.

This is Theorem 3.

### Level 3: Timing obstruction

If the first informative observation arrives after the worst-case exit time, then no observation-based policy can be viable.

This is Theorem 4.

### Level 4: Certification obstruction

If safe-set membership is not constant on observation fibres, then no exact observation-only certifier exists.

This is Theorem 5.

### Level 5: Policy-class obstruction

If the policy class is restricted, for example to certainty-equivalence controllers, viability may be lost even with injective observation.

This is Remark 1.

That hierarchy is much clearer than listing “five mechanisms plus one.”

---

## 4.4 Add a computational section

The paper would be much stronger if it included a section on computation.

For polyhedral systems, the common safe-action condition becomes a linear feasibility problem.

Suppose for each compatible state \(x_i\), safe controls satisfy affine inequalities:

\[
A_i u \le b_i.
\]

Then the common safe-action set is:

\[
\bigcap_i \{u : A_i u \le b_i\}.
\]

Emptiness can be certified by Farkas’ lemma.

This is already mentioned, but it should be elevated.

A good computational section would include:

1. finite-state belief approximation;
2. polyhedral safe-control sets;
3. LP feasibility test;
4. Farkas certificate;
5. timing-bound computation for simple drift conditions;
6. certainly-safe set computation:

\[
\mathcal Y_{\mathrm{safe}}
=
\{ y : O^{-1}(y)\subseteq K \}.
\]

That would make the paper more useful and more publishable.

---

## 4.5 Add figures

This paper currently has no figures. It would benefit greatly from simple diagrams.

### Figure 1: observation fibre crossing a safe boundary

Show state space with safe set \(K\). An observation fibre intersects both \(K\) and its complement. Caption: no exact observation-only certifier exists.

### Figure 2: incompatible safe controls

Show two states \(x_1,x_2\) in the same belief. At \(x_1\), safe controls are \(U_1\); at \(x_2\), safe controls are \(U_2\); the intersection is empty.

### Figure 3: delayed information

Show constraint margin \(q(t)\) decreasing from \(q(0)\) to zero before \(T_{\mathrm{obs}}\). The first informative observation arrives too late.

These figures would make the paper much more accessible without weakening it.

---

## 4.6 Strengthen the relation to estimation tubes

The paper says the obstruction calculus is “the local certificate layer” of the estimation-tube programme.

That is a good phrase. But the relation should be made more precise.

The estimation-tube approach transforms the problem into a viability problem on belief states. The paper should say explicitly:

> The robust epistemic kernel is the viability kernel of a set-valued belief dynamic. The obstruction certificates are local tangency or predecessor tests for that belief dynamic.

If possible, define the belief predecessor map:

\[
\mathcal P(B)
=
\{ B' : \exists u \in U^B(B'),\ \mathrm{Reach}(B',u)\subseteq \mathcal V,\ \text{successor belief admissible} \}.
\]

Then the common-action obstruction becomes:

\[
U^B(B)=\varnothing
\quad\text{or}\quad
\mathcal R^B(B)=\varnothing
\implies
B \notin \mathrm{Fix}(\mathcal P).
\]

That would make the paper much more mathematically coherent.

---

## 4.7 Be careful with “complete proof” and existence assumptions

Theorem 1’s hypothesis (H1.2) is messy:

> The disturbance correspondence is lower semicontinuous, or constant, on the strip, so that the state-dependent adverse selection constructed in the proof is realized by an admissible control–disturbance pair for which the Carathéodory existence theorem applies. Alternatively, without (H1.2), the certificate is read against the convexified relaxed inclusion...

This should be cleaned.

A cleaner version would be:

> Assume either that the adverse-selection correspondence admits a measurable feedback selection for which closed-loop solutions exist, or work in the relaxed convexified system where existence follows from standard Marchaud conditions.

Then state the theorem under that clean assumption.

Do not put long alternatives inside the theorem statement.

---

## 4.8 Reduce the number of named mechanisms

The phrase “obstruction calculus” is fine, but the list of mechanisms is crowded.

I would present:

- three main theorems: dynamic, informational, timing;
- one certification proposition: fibre criterion;
- one remark: certainty equivalence.

That is enough.

Calling all six “mechanisms” makes the paper sound like a taxonomy rather than a theorem-driven article.

### 1.1 The belief-state semantics need a cleaner formal definition

The paper defines the belief set as:

\[
B_t
=
\left\{
\xi(t):
\xi(0)\in B_0,\ 
\dot \xi(s)=f(\xi(s),u(s),d(s)),\ 
O(\xi(s))=y(s)
\right\}.
\]

But this leaves several things under-specified:

- What exactly is the admissible policy class?
- Is the policy a map from full observation records to controls?
- Is it nonanticipative?
- How are records generated when the disturbance changes the trajectory?
- Is the belief required to be compact, measurable, or nonempty?
- Does the same policy have to work for all compatible initial states and disturbances?
- What happens if different compatible states generate different observation records under the same policy?

A stronger answer would have said:

> The paper should define the robust observation-based viability kernel as the viability kernel of a set-valued belief dynamic, not merely through an informal belief-set formula.

For example, in sampled time, one could define a belief predecessor operator:

\[
\operatorname{Pre}_{\Delta}(\mathcal C)
=
\left\{
B:
\exists a \in \bigcap_{x\in B} U(x)
\text{ such that for all compatible disturbances,}
\right.
\]

\[
\left.
\text{the trajectory remains in } \mathcal V \text{ on } [0,\Delta]
\text{ and the terminal belief belongs to } \mathcal C
\right\}.
\]

Then the observation-based viability kernel can be described as the greatest fixed point of this predecessor map.

That would make the paper much more rigorous and would connect it directly to standard viability theory.

My previous answer should have proposed this explicitly.

---

### 1.2 Theorem 1 needs cleaner existence assumptions

The finite-time exit theorem says:

\[
\sup_{u\in U(x)} \inf_{d\in D(x)} D^+q(x;f(x,u,d)) \le -\varepsilon.
\]

This is a sensible Isaacs-type drift condition. But the proof needs a state-dependent adverse disturbance selection:

\[
d_\varepsilon(x,u)
\in
\arg\{d\in D(x): D^+q(x;f(x,u,d))\le -\varepsilon\}.
\]

The current hypothesis (H1.2) is messy:

> lower semicontinuous, or constant, or alternatively read against the convexified relaxed inclusion...

A better answer would have said:

> Either assume the adverse selection is regular enough to generate closed-loop solutions, or state the theorem directly for the relaxed convexified differential inclusion. Do not bury the alternative inside the theorem statement.

A cleaner version would be:

> Assume that the adverse-selection correspondence admits a measurable feedback selection for which Carathéodory solutions exist, or work in the standard relaxed Marchaud inclusion where solutions exist and the drift inequality is preserved.

That is shorter and more professional.

---

### 1.3 Theorem 3 should be formulated as a sampled-data or tube obstruction

The common-action obstruction is intuitively correct, but the instantaneous version is delicate.

If the policy can observe continuously, or if the observation structure changes immediately, then the claim needs qualification. The paper tries to handle this with hypothesis (H3.1), but the formulation is awkward.

A stronger answer would have said:

> Theorem 3 should be stated as a one-step tube obstruction over a review interval \(\Delta>0\), not as an instantaneous condition except in the limit \(\Delta\downarrow0\).

The natural object is:

\[
\mathcal A_{\mathrm{tube}}(B,\Delta)
=
\{a:
\operatorname{Reach}_{[0,\Delta]}(B,a)
\subseteq
\mathcal V
\}.
\]

Then the theorem becomes:

> If \(\mathcal A_{\mathrm{tube}}(B,\Delta)=\varnothing\), then \(B\) is not viable for any observation-based policy with review interval \(\Delta\).

This is cleaner, more rigorous, and more useful.

The instantaneous condition

\[
\bigcap_{x\in B} \mathcal R_{\mathcal V}(x)=\varnothing
\]

can then be presented as a local sufficient condition for tube obstruction when \(\Delta\) is small.

# 11. The single most important improvement

If I had to identify the single most important improvement, it would be this:

> The paper should stop presenting itself as supplying “the necessity side” of viability under incomplete observation. It should instead present itself as a certificate framework: a family of sufficient, checkable obstruction tests for observation-based nonviability, with clear local conditions, computational forms, and design consequences.

That reframing solves several problems at once:

- it avoids overclaiming;
- it makes the contribution honest;
- it allows the elementary results to remain useful without pretending to be deep;
- it makes the paper easier to review;
- it connects naturally to monitoring design.

---
A useful generalization of this paper should **not** mean adding more domains or buzzwords. It should generalize the *mechanism* that makes the paper interesting:

> obstruction certificates are checkable local or finite-horizon tests showing that an observation-based viability kernel is empty or smaller than the full-information kernel.

The paper becomes much stronger if it moves from a collection of separate mechanisms to a general theory of **belief-state viability under information constraints**.

Below are non-decorative, meaningful ways to generalize it.

# 1. Generalize the central object: belief-state viability kernels

The paper currently defines the “robust epistemic kernel” somewhat informally. A meaningful generalization is to define it as the **viability kernel of a set-valued belief dynamic**.

For sampled observations or review intervals, let \(\mathcal B\) be a space of compact information sets \(B\subseteq X\). Define a robust belief predecessor operator:

\[
\operatorname{Pre}_{\Delta}(\mathcal C)
=
\left\{
B\in \mathcal B:
\exists a\in U^B(B)
\text{ such that for every compatible disturbance,}
\right.
\]

\[
\left.
\text{the trajectory remains in } \mathcal V \text{ on } [0,\Delta]
\text{ and every possible successor belief belongs to } \mathcal C
\right\},
\]

where

\[
U^B(B)=\bigcap_{x\in B}U(x).
\]

Then the robust observation-based viability kernel is the greatest fixed point:

\[
\mathcal K_{\mathcal I}^{\Delta}
=
\nu Z.\ \operatorname{Pre}_{\Delta}(Z).
\]

This is a serious generalization because it does three things:

1. It makes the object rigorous.
2. It connects the paper directly to viability theory.
3. It turns the obstruction certificates into local tests for exclusion from a fixed point.

Then the common-action obstruction becomes:

\[
U^B(B)=\varnothing
\quad\text{or}\quad
\mathcal A_{\mathrm{tube}}(B,\Delta)=\varnothing
\implies
B\notin \mathcal K_{\mathcal I}^{\Delta}.
\]

That is cleaner than treating the mechanisms as separate phenomena.

# 2. Generalize from a static observation map to general information structures

The paper mostly uses a static observation map:

\[
O:X\to Y.
\]

That is fine, but the theory becomes much more useful if it allows:

- delayed observations;
- intermittent observations;
- quantized indicators;
- noisy set-valued observations;
- hidden parameters;
- event-triggered observations;
- asynchronous review schedules.

A clean way to do this is to treat an information structure as a filtration of observation records.

Let the observation record up to time \(t\) be:

\[
y_{[0,t]}.
\]

The belief is:

\[
B_t
=
\{x(t): x_0\in B_0,\ \exists d(\cdot),\ \text{trajectory compatible with } y_{[0,t]}\}.
\]

Then the obstruction calculus can be stated for arbitrary information structures, not only static maps.

Useful theorem:

> If information structure \(\mathcal I_1\) is finer than \(\mathcal I_2\), then  
> \[
> \mathcal K_{\mathcal I_2}
> \subseteq
> \mathcal K_{\mathcal I_1}.
> \]
> Refining observations can only increase the observation-based viability kernel.

This gives a monotonicity law for monitoring design.

Even better, one can define a **separation requirement**:

> An observation structure removes the common-action obstruction at belief \(B\) only if every observation fibre inside \(B\) contains states with nonempty common safe-action set.

That gives a precise sensor-design criterion.

# 3. Generalize the timing obstruction using an exit-time value function

The delayed-information theorem currently uses a condition like:

\[
D^+q \le -\varepsilon
\]

and gives the bound:

\[
T_{\mathrm{obs}} > \frac{\inf q}{\varepsilon}.
\]

That is useful but restrictive. A stronger generalization is to define a worst-case exit-time value function.

For an initial belief \(B_0\), define:

\[
\tau^*(B_0)
=
\inf_{u(\cdot)}
\sup_{x_0\in B_0,d(\cdot)}
\inf\{t: q(x(t))<0\},
\]

where the infimum is taken over controls that are indistinguishable to the regulator before the first informative observation.

Then the delayed-information obstruction becomes:

\[
T_{\mathrm{obs}} > \tau^*(B_0)
\implies
B_0\notin \mathcal K_{\mathcal I}.
\]

This is much more general and more recognizable to control theorists.

It also allows a comparison-function version. Instead of constant drift \(\varepsilon\), suppose:

\[
D^+q(x;f(x,u,d)) \le -\alpha(q(x))
\]

for some positive function \(\alpha\). Then the exit-time bound becomes:

\[
t_{\mathrm{exit}}
\le
\int_{0}^{q(x_0)} \frac{ds}{\alpha(s)}.
\]

This generalizes the constant-\(\varepsilon\) case and gives sharper timing requirements.

This is meaningful because monitoring frequency becomes a computable design variable:

\[
T_{\mathrm{obs}}
<
\tau^*(B_0).
\]

---

# 4. Generalize from one floor to multiple floors and vector constraints

The paper often discusses sustainability floors, but many results are stated for a single constraint \(q\ge0\). A natural and useful generalization is to handle:

\[
\mathcal V
=
\{x: q_j(x)\ge0,\ j=1,\dots,m\}.
\]

The safe-control correspondence becomes:

\[
\mathcal R_{\mathcal V}(x)
=
\left\{
u\in U(x):
\forall d\in D(x),\ 
\nabla q_j(x)\cdot f(x,u,d)\ge0
\text{ for every active } j
\right\}.
\]

The common safe-action set is:

\[
\mathcal R_{\mathcal V}^B(B)
=
\bigcap_{x\in B}\mathcal R_{\mathcal V}(x).
\]

Then the obstruction is:

\[
\mathcal R_{\mathcal V}^B(B)=\varnothing
\implies
B\notin \mathcal K_{\mathcal I}.
\]

For polyhedral systems this becomes a finite linear feasibility problem. If each safe-control set is polyhedral,

\[
\mathcal R_{\mathcal V}(x_i)
=
\{u: A_i u\le b_i\},
\]

then the common safe-action set is empty iff the stacked system

\[
A u \le b
\]

is infeasible. Farkas’ lemma gives a certificate:

\[
\exists \lambda\ge0:
\lambda^\top A=0,\quad \lambda^\top b<0.
\]

That is a genuine, checkable obstruction certificate.

This generalization is especially useful for sustainability because sustainability constraints are rarely single-floor constraints. They are usually multiple floors:

- minimum stock;
- maximum pollution;
- service level;
- institutional limit;
- resilience margin.

The paper becomes much more relevant if the obstruction calculus is stated for vector floors.

# 5. Generalize from sufficient certificates to finite-horizon complete certificates

The current paper gives sufficient certificates for nonviability. That is honest, but it leaves an “open middle ground.”

A meaningful generalization is to obtain **complete certificates on finite horizon**.

For discrete-time or sampled systems, define safe belief sets backward:

\[
\mathcal W_0
=
\{B: B\subseteq \mathcal V_T\},
\]

\[
\mathcal W_{k+1}
=
\operatorname{Pre}(\mathcal W_k).
\]

Then an initial belief \(B_0\) is viable for \(N\) steps iff:

\[
B_0\in \mathcal W_N.
\]

If \(B_0\notin \mathcal W_N\), the backward recursion gives a finite certificate: a tree of disturbance choices and compatible states that force violation.

This is valuable because:

- it gives completeness in finite horizon;
- it connects the paper to safety games and reachability;
- it gives an algorithm;
- it makes the obstruction calculus computationally meaningful.

For finite state, action, disturbance, and observation spaces, this is especially clean.

The theorem could be:

> For finite transition systems with finite observation partitions, the backward belief-safety recursion is sound and complete for finite-horizon observation-based viability. Nonviability is certified by a finite counterstrategy tree.

That would significantly strengthen the paper.

# 6. Generalize the computational side: certificates as dual objects

The paper already mentions Farkas certificates, but this can be made central.

A meaningful generalization is to treat obstruction certificates as **dual feasibility certificates**.

Examples:

### Common-action obstruction

If the common safe-action set is polyhedral, emptiness is certified by Farkas multipliers.

### Timing obstruction

If the dynamics are linear and constraints polyhedral, the worst-case exit-time condition can be certified by linear programming or reachability computations.

### Fibre certification obstruction

If the observation is linear,

\[
y=Cx,
\]

and the safe set is polyhedral, then one can check whether an observation fibre crosses the safe-set boundary by solving linear programs.

### Barrier-style obstruction

For nonlinear systems, one can use sum-of-squares or barrier-like dual certificates to show that no observation-based policy can prevent exit.

This gives the paper a much stronger applied-math identity:

> The obstruction calculus produces primal-dual certificates of nonviability under information constraints.

That is attractive for journals like JOTA, SVVA, or control journals with a computational orientation.

# 7. Generalize the policy class systematically

The paper currently distinguishes:

- perfect-information policies;
- observation-based policies;
- certainty-equivalence policies;
- institutionally restricted policies.

This can be generalized into a single parameterized framework.

Let \(\Pi\) be a policy class. Define:

\[
\mathrm{Viab}_{\Pi}(\mathcal V)
\]

as the viability kernel under policies in \(\Pi\).

Then one can prove monotonicity:

\[
\Pi_1\subseteq \Pi_2
\implies
\mathrm{Viab}_{\Pi_1}(\mathcal V)
\subseteq
\mathrm{Viab}_{\Pi_2}(\mathcal V).
\]

Similarly:

- finer observation structures enlarge the kernel;
- larger action sets enlarge the kernel;
- smaller disturbance sets enlarge the kernel;
- institutional restrictions shrink the kernel.

This gives a clean hierarchy:

\[
\mathrm{Viab}_{\text{institutional, partial obs}}
\subseteq
\mathrm{Viab}_{\text{partial obs}}
\subseteq
\mathrm{Viab}_{\text{robust full obs}}
\subseteq
\mathrm{Viab}_{\text{full obs}}.
\]

More importantly, it allows a diagnostic question:

> Which restriction is responsible for the loss of viability?

One could define a “distance to viability”:

- minimum sensor refinement needed;
- minimum reduction in observation delay needed;
- minimum expansion of the action set needed;
- minimum relaxation of the disturbance class needed.

That is highly meaningful for governance and monitoring design.

# 8. Generalize from obstruction to monitoring design

Right now the paper mostly says: given an observation structure, here is why viability fails.

A stronger generalization is to reverse the question:

> Given a system and a safety set, what observation structure is needed to make viability possible?

This turns obstruction into design.

For example, define a sensor design problem:

\[
\min_{\mathcal I}
\mathrm{Cost}(\mathcal I)
\]

subject to:

\[
B\in \mathcal K_{\mathcal I}
\]

for a target set of beliefs \(B\).

Or more modestly:

> Find the coarsest observation structure that removes all common-action obstructions on a target region.

For static observations, a necessary condition for exact safety certification is:

\[
K = O^{-1}(O(K)).
\]

For observation-based viability, a stronger condition is needed:

> Every observation fibre must contain states whose safe-control sets have nonempty intersection.

That gives a precise design rule:

\[
O(x_1)=O(x_2)
\implies
\mathcal R_{\mathcal V}(x_1)\cap \mathcal R_{\mathcal V}(x_2)\neq\varnothing.
\]

For multiple states in a fibre:

\[
\bigcap_{x\in O^{-1}(y)} \mathcal R_{\mathcal V}(x)
\neq
\varnothing.
\]

This is a meaningful generalization because it tells monitoring systems what they must measure.

It also gives a clean interpretation for sustainability indicators:

- an aggregate index is acceptable only if its fibres do not merge states requiring incompatible policies;
- a delayed indicator is acceptable only if its delay is shorter than the worst-case exit time;
- a biased indicator is acceptable only if the controller applies the correction.

That is exactly the kind of consequence that makes the paper useful.

# 9. Generalize the dynamic obstruction beyond constant drift

The finite-time exit certificate currently assumes:

\[
\sup_u \inf_d D^+q \le -\varepsilon.
\]

This can be generalized in several useful ways.

### 9.1 State-dependent drift

Suppose:

\[
\sup_u \inf_d D^+q(x;f(x,u,d))
\le
-\varepsilon(x).
\]

Then the exit time bound becomes:

\[
t_{\mathrm{exit}}
\le
\int_{x_0}^{\partial \mathcal V}
\frac{dq}{\varepsilon(q)}.
\]

### 9.2 Comparison functions

Suppose:

\[
D^+q \le -\alpha(q)
\]

where \(\alpha\) is positive on \((0,a]\). Then:

\[
t_{\mathrm{exit}}
\le
\int_0^{q(x_0)} \frac{ds}{\alpha(s)}.
\]

### 9.3 Multiple-constraint exit

Define the minimum slack:

\[
q_{\min}(x)
=
\min_j q_j(x).
\]

Then a drift condition on \(q_{\min}\) gives a certificate that at least one floor is breached.

These are not decorative. They make the timing certificates applicable to nonlinear resource systems where decline accelerates near thresholds.

# 10. Generalize to hidden parameters and adaptive observation

The paper already has a hidden-mode example:

\[
\dot z=\theta u,\quad \theta\in\{-1,+1\}.
\]

This can be generalized meaningfully.

Let the augmented state be:

\[
(x,\theta)\in X\times \Theta.
\]

The observation may not reveal \(\theta\). The belief then lives in \(X\times\Theta\).

The obstruction becomes:

\[
\bigcap_{(x,\theta)\in B}
\mathcal R_{\mathcal V}(x,\theta)
=
\varnothing.
\]

This is exactly the problem of uncertain parameters in sustainability:

- unknown regeneration rate;
- unknown climate sensitivity;
- unknown stock level;
- unknown institutional effectiveness;
- unknown shock persistence.

One can then generalize to **learning**:

- if \(\theta\) becomes observable after time \(T_{\mathrm{learn}}\), then the timing obstruction applies with \(T_{\mathrm{obs}}=T_{\mathrm{learn}}\);
- if learning is endogenous, the belief dynamic includes parameter updating.

This is meaningful because many sustainability failures are not caused by noisy data alone, but by structural uncertainty that is resolved too late.

# 11. Generalize to hybrid and discrete-continuous systems carefully

The paper’s hidden-mode example is already hybrid-like: a discrete parameter \(\theta\) affects continuous dynamics.

A meaningful generalization is to formulate the system as a hybrid inclusion:

\[
\dot x \in F(x,u,d),
\]

with discrete modes:

\[
\theta^+\in G_\theta(\theta,x,u,d).
\]

Then the belief state includes both continuous states and discrete modes.

The common-action obstruction becomes:

\[
\bigcap_{(x,\theta)\in B}
\mathcal R_{\mathcal V}(x,\theta)
=
\varnothing.
\]

This is useful because many sustainability systems have discrete regimes:

- normal vs crisis;
- healthy vs collapsed;
- high vs low productivity;
- cooperative vs noncooperative institutional regime.

But this should be done only if the paper is targeting a control journal that values hybrid systems. Otherwise it may become decorative.

# 12. Generalize to approximate and buffered viability

Exact viability is fragile. A useful generalization is to define buffered viability:

\[
\mathcal V_\delta
=
\{x: q_j(x)\ge \delta,\ \forall j\}.
\]

Then obstruction certificates can be stated with margins.

For example:

\[
D^+q \le -\varepsilon
\]

certifies exit from the buffered set even faster.

This is meaningful for three reasons:

The current terminology alternates among *epistemic kernel*, *robust epistemic kernel*, *belief-space viability*, and *viability of a collection of information states*. These should be unified before adding generalizations.

Let \(\mathcal B\) be an admissible space of compact beliefs \(B\subseteq X\). A belief is viable if one observation-based policy can keep **every state and every disturbance realization compatible with the evolving observation record** inside the constraint set.

For a review interval \(\Delta>0\), define the robust belief predecessor of a collection \(\mathscr C\subseteq\mathcal B\) by

\exists a\in U^B(B)\ \text{such that}
\Big.

\Big\}.

and the successor beliefs must be generated by the same applied action and by the observation-update rule of the model.

## 2. Make the information structure general, but do not overclaim

The static map \(O:X\to Y\) should be replaced by an observation mechanism that generates records. A useful general form is

\[
y_t\in H_t(x_{[0,t]},v_t),

where \(H_t\) may represent exact observations, noisy observations, delayed observations, quantization, missing observations, or event-triggered measurements.

The belief at time \(t\) is then

\[
B_t(y_{[0,t]})
=
\left\{

 \begin{array}{l}
 x(0)\in B_0,\ 
 \text{there exist admissible controls and disturbances,}\\
 \text{and a compatible trajectory producing }y_{[0,t]} 
 \end{array}
\right\}.
\]

This makes the static-fibre results special cases of a record-based framework.

### Observation refinement theorem

A useful theorem is the monotonicity of viability under information refinement. If \(\mathcal I_1\) is at least as informative as \(\mathcal I_2\), in the sense that every \(\mathcal I_2\)-record can be obtained from an \(\mathcal I_1\)-record by a measurable coarsening, then, under the same action and disturbance conventions,

\[
\mathscr K_{\mathcal I_2}^{\Delta}
\subseteq
\mathscr K_{\mathcal I_1}^{\Delta}.
\]

This result should be stated with care. Refining observations enlarges the set of available policies, but it does not necessarily make every particular policy safer. The monotonicity is a statement about the **maximal viability kernel**, not about a fixed controller.

The corresponding design implication is:

## 3. Generalize the timing theorem through a value function, but retain the drift theorem

The current constant-drift condition is valuable because it is transparent and checkable. It should not be replaced entirely. Instead, present it as a computable special case of a broader exit-time formulation.

Define the worst-case blind-window exit time by

\[
\tau_{\mathrm{blind}}(B_0)
=
\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{blind}}}
\inf_{x_0\in B_0,\ d\in\mathcal D}
\tau(x_0,\pi,d),
\]

only if this quantifier order matches the intended game. If the regulator chooses the action to delay exit and the adversary chooses the compatible branch and disturbance to accelerate it, the more natural quantity is

\[
\tau^*(B_0)
=
\sup_{u(\cdot)\in\mathcal U_{\mathrm{blind}}}
\inf_{x_0\in B_0,\ d(\cdot)\in\mathcal D(x_0,u)}
\tau(x_0,u,d),
\]

or its corresponding infimum version, depending on whether \(\tau\) denotes an exit time or a worst-case guaranteed survival time. The paper must fix this quantifier order explicitly; the notation cannot be chosen independently of the game semantics.

A safe general statement is:

> If every blind-window control admits a compatible state and disturbance whose exit time is strictly less than \(T_{\mathrm{obs}}\), then no observation-based policy can maintain robust viability until the first informative observation.

In value-function notation, if

\[
\sup_{u(\cdot)\in\mathcal U_{\mathrm{blind}}}
\inf_{x_0\in B_0,\ d(\cdot)}
\tau(x_0,u,d)
<T_{\mathrm{obs}},
\]

then \(B_0\) is not viable over the blind window.

### Comparison-function extension

The constant bound

\[
D^+q\le -\varepsilon
\]

can be generalized to

\[
D^+q(x;f(x,u,d))
\le -\alpha(q(x)),
\]

where \(\alpha(s)>0\) on the relevant interval. The comparison bound is then

\[
\tau_{\mathrm{exit}}(x_0)
\le
\int_{0}^{q(x_0)}\frac{ds}{\alpha(s)},
\]

provided the comparison argument and strict-violation convention are stated correctly.

The paper should distinguish three claims:

1. a local drift certificate;
2. an upper bound on exit time;
3. a global worst-case exit-time value function.

The first does not automatically establish the third.

## 4. Generalize the common-action obstruction to multiple constraints

The multi-floor extension is important and should be included in the core theory, not left as an optional embellishment.

\mathcal V=\{x:q_j(x)\ge0,\ j=1,\ldots,m\},

where

I(x)=\{j:q_j(x)=0\}

\mathcal R_{\mathcal V}^{B}(B)

\bigcap_{x\in B}\mathcal R_{\mathcal V}(x)

If the intersection is empty, the belief is excluded from the robust observation-based kernel under the relevant review convention.

\right\}.

\[

is the exact one-step obstruction under the stated held-action semantics. The common tangency condition is a local, small-\(\Delta\) certificate, not automatically the exact finite-review test.

\mathcal R_{\mathcal V}(x_i)=\{u:A_i u\le b_i\},

then the common safe-action problem is

\[
Au\le b,
\]

where the inequalities are stacked across \(i\). Infeasibility can be certified by a Farkas vector \(\lambda\ge0\) satisfying

\[
\lambda^\top A=0,
\qquad
\lambda^\top b<0.

- the individual safe-action sets;
- the stacked linear system;
- the Farkas multipliers;

## 5. Make finite-horizon completeness the main strengthening

The proposed finite-horizon extension is the most valuable addition because it gives the paper one setting in which the theory is both sound and complete.

\[
\mathscr W_0
=
\{B:B\subseteq\mathcal V_T\},
\]

and recursively

\[
\mathscr W_{k+1}
=
\left\{
B:
\exists a\in U^B(B)\ 
\text{such that every admissible successor belief lies in }\mathscr W_k
\right\}.
\]

Then prove:

> An initial belief \(B_0\) admits a robust observation-based policy that remains safe for \(N\) steps if and only if \(B_0\in\mathscr W_N\).

The proof is a backward-induction argument. Nonmembership produces a finite obstruction tree whose nodes are beliefs and whose branches are compatible disturbances and observations.

This lets the paper distinguish clearly between:

- **complete finite-horizon certificates**, supplied by backward recursion;
- **local analytical certificates**, such as drift and common-action tests;
- **incomplete infinite-horizon tests**, where the paper does not claim a full characterization.

That hierarchy is more rigorous than describing all certificates simply as parts of one calculus.

For infinite horizon, one may define:

\[
\mathscr K_{\mathcal I}^{\Delta}
=
\nu Z.\operatorname{Pre}_{\Delta}(Z),
\]

but the paper should not claim this is always equal to

\[
\bigcap_{k\ge0}\mathscr W_k
\]

without assumptions.

A safe statement is:

> In finite systems, the decreasing sequence \(\mathscr W_k\) reaches its limit in finitely many steps, and the limit is the greatest fixed point. In general set-valued settings, \(\bigcap_{k\ge0}\mathscr W_k\) is a natural infinite-horizon candidate, but equality with the greatest fixed point requires additional continuity or compactness assumptions.

This avoids overclaiming.

---

# 4. State finite-horizon completeness with exact assumptions

Finite-horizon completeness is the most valuable strengthening. It should be stated as a theorem in a finite-system setting.

---

## 4.1 Finite-system assumptions

Assume:

- finite state space \(X\);
- finite action set \(A\);
- finite disturbance set \(D\);
- finite observation set \(Y\);
- transition relation

\[
x^+ \in F(x,a,d);
\]

- observation relation

\[
y \in O(x,a,d,x^+);
\]

- safe state set \(\mathcal V\subseteq X\);
- belief \(B\subseteq X\).

For a belief \(B\) and action \(a\), define the post-belief for observation \(y\):

\[
\operatorname{Post}(B,a,y)
=
\{x^+\in X:
\exists x\in B,\exists d\in D,\exists y\in O(x,a,d,x^+),
\ x^+\in F(x,a,d)
\}.
\]

An observation \(y\) is possible under \((B,a)\) if:

\[
\operatorname{Post}(B,a,y)\neq\varnothing.
\]

Then define the finite predecessor:

\[
\operatorname{Pre}(\mathscr C)
=
\left\{
B\subseteq \mathcal V:
\exists a\in \bigcap_{x\in B}U(x)
\text{ such that for every possible }y,
\operatorname{Post}(B,a,y)\subseteq \mathcal V
\text{ and }
\operatorname{Post}(B,a,y)\in\mathscr C
\right\}.
\]

---

## 4.2 Completeness theorem

The theorem should be:

> An initial belief \(B_0\) admits an observation-based policy that keeps all compatible trajectories inside \(\mathcal V\) for \(N\) steps if and only if  
> \[
> B_0\in \mathscr W_N.
> \]

The proof is by backward induction.

---

## 4.3 Finite obstruction tree

If

\[
B_0\notin \mathscr W_N,
\]

then there exists a finite obstruction tree.

At each node:

- the current belief \(B\) and remaining horizon \(k\) are known;
- for every admissible action \(a\in U^B(B)\), the adversary can choose a possible observation \(y\) such that

\[
\operatorname{Post}(B,a,y)\notin \mathscr W_{k-1}.
\]

The tree is a complete finite certificate of nonviability.

This is important because it turns the paper’s “obstruction calculus” into a rigorous object in at least one setting.

---

## 4.4 Continuous systems

For continuous systems, the paper should say:

> Finite-horizon completeness holds exactly for finite transition systems and finite observation partitions. For continuous systems, the same recursion can be used with reachable-set operators, but completeness depends on the exactness of the reachable-set representation and may fail under approximation.

That is honest and useful.

---

# 5. Clarify the relation between instantaneous common-action obstruction and tube safety

The current paper sometimes treats the instantaneous condition

\[
\bigcap_{x\in B}\mathcal R_{\mathcal V}(x)=\varnothing
\]

as if it were the exact one-step obstruction. It is not always exact for positive review intervals.

The exact one-step obstruction under held actions is:

\[
\mathcal A_{\mathrm{tube}}(B,\Delta)
=
\{a:
\operatorname{Reach}_{[0,\Delta]}(B,a)\subseteq \mathcal V
\}
=
\varnothing.
\]

The instantaneous tangency condition is a local sufficient condition for this.

A clean theorem would be:

> Suppose \(U^B(B)\) is compact, the dynamics and constraints are continuous, and there exists \(\eta>0\) such that for every \(a\in U^B(B)\) there are a boundary state \(x\in B\cap\partial\mathcal V\), an active constraint \(j\), and a disturbance \(d\in D(x)\) with  
> \[
> \nabla q_j(x)\cdot f(x,a,d)\le -\eta.
> \]
> Then there exists \(\Delta^*>0\) such that for every \(0<\Delta\le\Delta^*\),  
> \[
> \mathcal A_{\mathrm{tube}}(B,\Delta)=\varnothing.
> \]

This makes the instantaneous condition rigorous.

If the paper does not want to prove this uniform-margin version, it should present the instantaneous condition only as a heuristic or as a zero-review-interval limit.

---

# 6. Fix the quantifier order in the timing theorem

Your revised version correctly identifies the need for a value-function formulation. The remaining gap is the quantifier order.

The regulator wants to maximize guaranteed survival time before the first informative observation. The adversary chooses the compatible initial state and disturbance that minimize survival time.

A natural definition is:

\[
\sigma^*(B_0)
=
\sup_{u(\cdot)\in\mathcal U_{\mathrm{blind}}}
\inf_{x_0\in B_0,\ d(\cdot)\in\mathcal D}
\tau(x_0,u,d),
\]

where:

- \(u(\cdot)\) ranges over controls that an observation-based policy can apply before the first informative observation;
- the infimum is taken over compatible branches that remain observation-equivalent to the policy’s record until exit or until \(T_{\mathrm{obs}}\);
- \(\tau(x_0,u,d)\) is the first time at which \(q(x(t))<0\).

Then the delayed-information obstruction is:

\[
\sigma^*(B_0) < T_{\mathrm{obs}}
\implies
B_0\notin \mathscr K_{\mathcal I}^{\Delta}.
\]

This is the clean game-theoretic statement.

The current constant-drift theorem becomes a sufficient condition for this inequality. If, for every blind-window control \(u(\cdot)\), there exists a compatible state and disturbance such that:

\[
D^+q \le -\varepsilon,
\]

then:

\[
\tau \le \frac{q(x_0)}{\varepsilon},
\]

and therefore:

\[
\sigma^*(B_0)
\le
\frac{\inf_{x\in B_0}q(x)}{\varepsilon}.
\]

If:

\[
T_{\mathrm{obs}}
>
\frac{\inf_{x\in B_0}q(x)}{\varepsilon},
\]

the timing obstruction applies.

---

# 7. Generalize the drift theorem with comparison functions carefully

The comparison-function extension is useful, but it needs a precise statement.

Suppose:

\[
D^+q(x;f(x,u,d))
\le
-\alpha(q(x)),
\]

where:

\[
\alpha(s)>0
\quad\text{for }s\in(0,a].
\]

If the scalar comparison equation

\[
\dot r = -\alpha(r),
\qquad
r(0)=q(x_0),
\]

reaches zero in finite time, then the exit time satisfies:

\[
\tau_{\mathrm{exit}}(x_0)
\le
\int_{0}^{q(x_0)}\frac{ds}{\alpha(s)}.
\]

But the paper should note:

- if \(\alpha(0)=0\) and the integral diverges, the condition may imply only asymptotic approach, not finite exit;
- the strict-violation convention \(q<0\) must be preserved;
- the comparison argument requires standard regularity, for example continuity or local Lipschitz conditions.

This prevents the comparison extension from becoming decorative.

---

# 8. Make the multi-floor extension part of the core theory

The multi-floor extension should not be optional. Sustainability floors are usually multiple.

For:

\[
\mathcal V
=
\{x:q_j(x)\ge0,\ j=1,\dots,m\},
\]

define the active set:

\[
I(x)=\{j:q_j(x)=0\}.
\]

The robust safe-control correspondence is:

\[
\mathcal R_{\mathcal V}(x)
=
\left\{
u\in U(x):
\nabla q_j(x)\cdot f(x,u,d)\ge0
\quad
\forall d\in D(x),\ \forall j\in I(x)
\right\}.
\]

The belief-level common safe-action set is:

\[
\mathcal R_{\mathcal V}^B(B)
=
\bigcap_{x\in B}\mathcal R_{\mathcal V}(x).
\]

Then:

\[
\mathcal R_{\mathcal V}^B(B)=\varnothing
\]

is a local obstruction condition under the uniform-margin assumption.

For finite \(\Delta\), the exact condition is:

\[
\mathcal A_{\mathrm{tube}}(B,\Delta)=\varnothing.
\]

The paper should maintain this distinction.

---

# 9. Strengthen the polyhedral computational certificate

The Farkas certificate is one of the most practically useful parts of the paper. It should be developed more fully.

Suppose for finitely many compatible states \(x_i\) the safe-action sets are polyhedral:

\[
\mathcal R_{\mathcal V}(x_i)
=
\{u:A_i u\le b_i\}.
\]

The common safe-action problem is:

\[
A u \le b,
\]

where \(A\) and \(b\) stack the constraints over \(i\).

Infeasibility is certified by a Farkas vector \(\lambda\ge0\) such that:

\[
\lambda^\top A=0,
\qquad
\lambda^\top b<0.
\]

To make this robust numerically, the paper should add:

1. a normalization, for example

\[
\|\lambda\|_1=1;
\]

2. a statement of the exact Farkas variant being used;
3. a worked example showing the stacked system and the multiplier vector.

The worked example should include:

- the belief \(B\);
- the compatible states;
- each state’s safe-action set;
- the stacked inequalities;
- the Farkas multiplier;
- the interpretation: why no common safe action exists.

This would turn the computational claim into a concrete contribution.

---

# 10. Add a section on conservative computation

The paper should explain how certificates behave under approximation.

This is important because continuous systems cannot usually be handled exactly.

---

## 10.1 Certifying nonviability

To certify nonviability, it is enough to identify a subset of the belief that is already obstructed.

If:

\[
B^-\subseteq B
\]

and \(B^-\) is nonviable, then \(B\) is nonviable, because robust viability requires protecting every compatible state in \(B\).

Thus, for obstruction certificates, one may use adversarial subsets of the belief.

---

## 10.2 Certifying viability

To certify viability, one needs the opposite direction.

If:

\[
B\subseteq B^+
\]

and \(B^+\) is viable, then \(B\) is viable.

But the paper is mainly about nonviability certificates, so it should emphasize the first direction.

---

## 10.3 Reachability approximations

For tube safety:

- an over-approximation of the reachable tube can prove safety if it remains inside \(\mathcal V\);
- an under-approximation of the reachable tube can prove violation if it exits \(\mathcal V\).

The paper should state this explicitly if it discusses computation.

---

# 11. Turn monitoring design into a precise but modest problem

The monitoring-design section should avoid sounding like a general optimal sensor-design theory unless the paper proves one.

A good formal design problem is:

\[
\min_{\mathcal I} c(\mathcal I)
\quad
\text{subject to}
\quad
B_0\in \mathscr K_{\mathcal I}^{\Delta}.
\]

But because this may be hard, the paper can give local design conditions.

---

## 11.1 Static exact certification

For static safety certification, exact certification through \(O\) exists iff:

\[
K=O^{-1}(O(K)).
\]

This is necessary and sufficient.

---

## 11.2 One-step viability

For one-step observation-based viability, a necessary local condition is:

\[
\mathcal A_{\mathrm{tube}}(O^{-1}(y),\Delta)
\neq
\varnothing
\]

for every observation fibre that can arise.

For instantaneous tangency, a weaker local condition is:

\[
\bigcap_{x\in O^{-1}(y)}
\mathcal R_{\mathcal V}(x)
\neq
\varnothing.
\]

The paper should say that these are necessary conditions for one-step viability, not sufficient conditions for infinite-horizon viability.

---

## 11.3 Minimal fibre splitting

A useful small theorem would be:

> If a fibre \(F\) is obstructed because two states \(x_1,x_2\in F\) have disjoint safe-action sets, then any observation structure that restores one-step viability must separate \(x_1\) and \(x_2\).

This gives a concrete minimal refinement requirement.

It is simple, but it converts the obstruction into a design rule.

---

# 12. Keep hidden parameters as state augmentation

The hidden-parameter extension should be presented as state augmentation.

Let:

\[
\tilde x=(x,\theta)\in X\times\Theta.
\]

For static hidden parameters:

\[
\dot \theta=0.
\]

The observation map becomes:

\[
\tilde O(x,\theta).
\]

The safe-action correspondence becomes:

\[
\mathcal R_{\mathcal V}(x,\theta).
\]

The common-action obstruction becomes:

\[
\bigcap_{(x,\theta)\in B}
\mathcal R_{\mathcal V}(x,\theta)
=
\varnothing.
\]

This covers:

- hidden ecological regimes;
- unknown regeneration rates;
- unknown productivity states;
- hidden institutional modes.

If learning is introduced, it should be treated as belief update on \(\theta\). The first time at which the belief separates the modes becomes the first informative observation time \(T_{\mathrm{obs}}\).

The paper should not introduce Bayesian probability unless it is willing to change the entire set-membership framework.

---

# 13. Recast the mechanisms as predecessor-exclusion certificates

The certificate taxonomy is good. It can be improved by explicitly tying every certificate to the predecessor operator.

---

## A. Dynamic certificates

These show that for some compatible state, no control can maintain safety.

They imply:

\[
B\notin \operatorname{Pre}_{\Delta}(\mathscr C)
\]

for every \(\mathscr C\), because tube safety fails independently of successor beliefs.

---

## B. Belief-action certificates

These show that the belief has no jointly admissible or jointly safe action.

They imply:

\[
U^B(B)=\varnothing
\]

or

\[
\mathcal A_{\mathrm{tube}}(B,\Delta)=\varnothing,
\]

and therefore:

\[
B\notin \operatorname{Pre}_{\Delta}(\mathscr C)
\]

for every \(\mathscr C\).

---

## C. Timing certificates

These show that the first informative observation arrives after the worst-case guaranteed exit time.

They imply that no observation-based policy can maintain safety through the blind window.

---

## D. Certification certificates

These concern exact observation-based verdicts rather than policies.

They show that no exact certifier exists because safe-set membership is not constant on observation fibres.

---

## E. Policy-class certificates

These show that viability is lost because the policy class is restricted, not because the observation is incomplete.

The certainty-equivalence example belongs here.

---

# 14. Add a certificate-checking algorithm

A practical algorithm would make the paper much stronger.

A possible algorithm is:

---

### Input

- system dynamics;
- safe set \(\mathcal V\);
- observation structure \(\mathcal I\);
- initial belief \(B_0\);
- horizon \(N\) or review interval \(\Delta\).

---

### Step 1: Dynamic obstruction

Check whether an adverse-drift certificate applies.

If yes, return a dynamic certificate.

---

### Step 2: Finite-horizon recursion

If the system is finite, compute \(\mathscr W_N\) by backward recursion.

If \(B_0\notin \mathscr W_N\), return a finite obstruction tree.

---

### Step 3: Tube obstruction

For continuous or polyhedral systems, check whether:

\[
\mathcal A_{\mathrm{tube}}(B_0,\Delta)=\varnothing.
\]

If the system is polyhedral, use linear programming or reachability tools.

If empty, return a tube certificate, preferably with dual multipliers.

---

### Step 4: Timing obstruction

Compute or bound:

\[
\sigma^*(B_0).
\]

If:

\[
\sigma^*(B_0)<T_{\mathrm{obs}},
\]

return a timing certificate.

---

### Step 5: Certification obstruction

Check whether the safe set is saturated with respect to observation fibres.

If not, return a fibre-certification obstruction.

---

This algorithm makes the taxonomy operational.

---

# 15. Add at least one integrated worked example

The paper currently has examples, but it would benefit from one integrated worked example that passes through several layers of the framework.

A good example would include:

1. two possible hidden modes;
2. a common observation that merges them;
3. incompatible safe actions;
4. a delayed indicator;
5. a polyhedral Farkas certificate;
6. a monitoring refinement that removes the obstruction.

For instance, one could construct a two-state resource system where:

- in mode 1, the safe action is high effort reduction;
- in mode 2, the safe action is low effort reduction;
- the aggregate indicator cannot distinguish the modes;
- the common action violates one mode’s floor;
- a delayed stock assessment arrives after the worst-case depletion time;
- adding a binary regime indicator or shortening the review interval removes the obstruction.

This would make the theory concrete and would help interdisciplinary readers.

# 16. Strengthen the monotonicity framework

Your monotonicity recommendation is correct. It should be stated as a partial-order result, not as a single linear hierarchy.

Define:

\[
\operatorname{Viab}(\Pi,\mathcal I,U,D,\mathcal V).
\]

Then, under comparable assumptions:

\[
\Pi_1\subseteq\Pi_2
\implies
\operatorname{Viab}(\Pi_1,\mathcal I,U,D,\mathcal V)
\subseteq
\operatorname{Viab}(\Pi_2,\mathcal I,U,D,\mathcal V).
\]

\[
U_1(x)\subseteq U_2(x)
\quad\forall x
\implies
\operatorname{Viab}(\Pi,\mathcal I,U_1,D,\mathcal V)
\subseteq
\operatorname{Viab}(\Pi,\mathcal I,U_2,D,\mathcal V).
\]

For disturbances:

\[
D_1(x)\subseteq D_2(x)
\quad\forall x
\implies
\operatorname{Viab}(\Pi,\mathcal I,U,D_2,\mathcal V)
\subseteq
\operatorname{Viab}(\Pi,\mathcal I,U,D_1,\mathcal V).
\]

For observations, if \(\mathcal I_1\) is finer than \(\mathcal I_2\), then:

\[
\operatorname{Viab}(\Pi,\mathcal I_2,U,D,\mathcal V)
\subseteq
\operatorname{Viab}(\Pi,\mathcal I_1,U,D,\mathcal V).
\]

The paper should emphasize that these objects are partially ordered. Not every pair of policy classes or observation structures is comparable.

# 17. Add a “distance to viability” diagnostic

A useful and non-decorative extension is to define minimal repair quantities.

For an obstructed belief \(B\), define:

### Minimal observation refinement

The coarsest refinement of \(\mathcal I\) that removes the common-action obstruction.

### Minimal timing improvement

The largest admissible observation delay:

\[
T_{\max}(B)
=
\sigma^*(B).
\]

Then the monitoring requirement is:

\[
T_{\mathrm{obs}} < T_{\max}(B).
\]

### Minimal action expansion

The smallest enlargement of \(U\) that makes

\[
\mathcal A_{\mathrm{tube}}(B,\Delta)\neq\varnothing.
\]

### Minimal institutional relaxation

The smallest enlargement of \(U_{\mathcal J}\) that restores one-step viability.

These quantities may be hard to compute in general, but defining them gives the governance section mathematical meaning.

---

# 18. Add a partial converse in special cases

The paper should not claim a general converse theorem. But it can state partial converses.

---

## 18.1 Finite-horizon converse

In finite systems, if \(B_0\notin\mathscr W_N\), then there exists a finite obstruction tree.

This is a complete converse for finite horizon.

---

## 18.2 Polyhedral one-step converse

In one-step polyhedral problems, if the common safe-action set is empty, then a Farkas certificate exists.

This is a complete dual certificate for that one-step feasibility problem.

---

## 18.3 No general infinite-horizon converse

The paper should explicitly say:

> Outside finite or specially structured settings, we do not claim that every nonviable belief admits one of the certificates developed here.

This keeps the contribution honest.

# 19. Revised contribution statement

Your proposed contribution statement is good. I would tighten it slightly as follows:

> We formulate robust observation-based viability as a viability problem on a space of information states. The central object is a robust belief predecessor operator. From this operator we derive sound obstruction certificates for exclusion from the observation-based viability kernel: adverse-drift certificates, common-action certificates, delayed-information certificates, and observation-fibre certification limits. In finite-horizon finite systems, backward belief recursion is sound and complete and yields finite obstruction trees. For polyhedral one-step problems, common-action failures admit Farkas or linear-programming certificates. These results translate information constraints into design requirements for observation refinement, review timing, aggregation, and bias correction.

This is defensible and precise.

# Bottom line

The improved recommendations are strong, but they become fully defensible only after the paper fixes the underlying semantics of observation, belief update, and policy admissibility. Once that is done, the paper can be organized around one rigorous object:

\[
\operatorname{Pre}_{\Delta}
\]

and every certificate becomes an answer to the same question:

> Why does this belief fail to belong to the robust observation-based viability kernel?

That is the non-decorative generalization. It does not make the paper broader by adding unrelated domains. It makes the paper deeper by turning a set of mechanisms into a coherent belief-space viability theory with finite-horizon completeness, computable local certificates, and explicit monitoring-design consequences.
