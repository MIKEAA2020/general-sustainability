# Part 1 — Intuitive and graphical package: "Two obligations and one bridge"

*Insert as a standalone subsection (it becomes Section 4 of the JEDC rewrite). All notation is as in the model section.*

## 1.1 The plain-language picture

Normalize two separate obligations — service floors $s_1, s_2$ — so that zero is the legal or physical minimum. A transition occupies one review interval and must end in a destination configuration $G$ (think: a renovated, productive state). An adverse disturbance dips, by up to 2 units, whichever obligation the chosen plan stresses. The menu is:

- **FAST** — restructure quickly through sector 1: $s_1$ traces a V that bottoms at $s_1 - 2$ mid-interval; both margins end higher ($+e$).
- **SLOW** — the mirror plan through sector 2: $s_2$ dips to $s_2 - 2$.
- **STAGED** — restructure gradually, financed out of a liquid reserve $x$ at cost 1: no floor dips; the reserve traces $[x-1, x]$.
- **NO-SWITCH** — stay put: perfectly safe, but never reaches $G$.

A **scalarized assessor** watches only the weighted index $w \cdot s(t)$ along the path. Under FAST the index's worst-case dip is $2w_1$, so the index stays nonnegative iff $w_1 s_1 + w_2 s_2 \ge 2 w_1$, i.e. iff $r = w_2/w_1 \ge \rho_1 = (2-s_1)/s_2$. Symmetrically, SLOW passes the index iff $r \le \rho_2 = s_1/(2-s_2)$. Whenever $s_1 + s_2 \ge 2$, these two endorsement intervals cover every $r$: **every assessor endorses some plan.**

A **floor-by-floor assessor** asks a different question: does one plan keep *each* obligation above zero along the whole path? FAST is lawful only if $s_1 \ge 2$ (it must survive its own dip); SLOW only if $s_2 \ge 2$; STAGED only if $x \ge 1$ (the reserve must cover its cost). Inside the triangle
$\mathcal{Q} = \{ s_1 < 2,\ s_2 < 2,\ s_1 + s_2 \ge 2 \}$
none of FAST/SLOW is lawful, and STAGED is lawful only if the reserve is deep enough.

## 1.2 The impossibility region $I$ (the triangle at $x < 1$)

Every weighting votes "yes," but each votes for a *different* plan; no plan receives a unanimous "yes"; and no plan is lawful floor-by-floor. The committee analogy is exact: the agenda is approved unanimously, yet no project is. The aggregate's certification is therefore a **false positive** — it certifies a transition that no admissible plan realizes. Figure 2 shows the mechanism path-by-path: for $r = 2$ the index line stays above zero while $s_1(t)$ crosses below its floor; for $r = 1/2$ the mirror image holds under SLOW; and the only plan that protects both floors (STAGED) sinks the reserve below zero.

**One-sentence reading:** *enough in aggregate, too little in each, and no buffer to phase — the index certifies what no plan can do.*

## 1.3 The rescue set $R$ (the same triangle at $x \ge 1$)

Nothing about the margins has changed; only the reserve has. With $x \ge 1$, the staged, reserve-financed plan is lawful: both floors stay intact, the cost 1 is paid out of $x$, and the destination is reached. The same triangle is now a **true positive**: the aggregate's endorsement is vindicated by a single plan that every assessor endorses. The reserve is the witness.

**One-sentence reading:** *enough in aggregate, too little in each, but a buffer that pays for phasing — the index is right, and the reserve is why.*

Economically: $I$ and $R$ are the same covenant-violation triangle seen at two levels of liquidity. A consolidated balance-sheet test can look satisfactory under every relative valuation while no lawful operating schedule exists; liquidity does not change the test, it changes the feasible set of schedules.

# Part 2 — Rewrite for *Journal of Economic Dynamics and Control*

## 2.1 Front matter

**Title:** Aggregate indices and transition safety: a quantifier-order separation between compensatory and non-compensatory sustainability criteria

**Abstract.** Sustainability criteria divide into compensatory criteria, which permit substitution through a scalarized aggregate, and non-compensatory criteria, which impose coordinate-wise floors. We compare the two as robust viability problems on a common finite-horizon transition datum with path-wise (exact-tube) constraints. At any fixed path, scalarization over the full nonnegative cone is lossless; the criteria nevertheless separate dynamically, because common-plan acceptance ($\exists a\, \forall w$) and per-weight acceptance ($\forall w\, \exists a_w$) do not commute. On an explicit rational datum the acceptance gap is a region with nonempty interior: every nonnegative weighting licenses some transition, no transition is licensed by all, and no transition satisfies the floors path-wise. The gap coincides exactly with the states whose reserve stock cannot finance a staged rescue; menu convexification closes it exactly, while discrete time-sharing does not. We give exact per-weight licensing thresholds, a closed-form rescue threshold, and horizon-persistence and erasure results. Implications for index construction and per-floor reporting are discussed.

**Keywords:** sustainability; viability theory; robust control; scalarization; multi-criteria decision analysis; quantifier order.
**JEL codes:** C61; C62; D81; Q01; Q56.

**Highlights.**
- Compensatory and non-compensatory sustainability criteria are compared as robust viability problems.
- Aggregate certification can fail path-wise floors on a region with nonempty interior.
- The gap is identified with the failure of $\exists\forall$/$\forall\exists$ interchange over actions and weights.
- Exact licensing thresholds and a closed-form reserve (rescue) threshold are derived.
- Menu convexification closes the gap; discrete time-sharing does not.

## 2.2 Introduction (rewritten)

Sustainability assessment has been organized since its foundation around two criteria. The compensatory criterion, formalized in the genuine-savings and inclusive-wealth frameworks (Solow, 1974; Dasgupta and Mäler, 2000; World Bank, 2011; Asheim, 1994), evaluates transitions through a scalarized aggregate and permits substitution across capital forms at nonnegative weights. The non-compensatory criterion holds that certain stocks and services bind separately — critical natural capital whose breach cannot be offset at any price (Daly, 1990; Ekins et al., 2003) — and its foundation statement in ecological economics is weak comparability: the values relevant to environmental decisions may not be commensurable in a single metric (Martinez-Alier, Munda, and O'Neill, 1998). The indicator theory of both criteria is well developed, including the maximin formulation in which strong sustainability emerges from the viability of constraints rather than from an optimality criterion (Martinet, 2011; Cairns and Martinet, 2014; Doyen and Gajardo, 2020), and the compensability distinction is standard in multi-criteria decision analysis (Cinelli, Coles, and Kirwan, 2014; Schär, Pohl, and Geldermann, 2025).

That literature is predominantly static: it compares criteria evaluated at stocks or at instantaneous flows. The question this paper addresses is dynamic and certification-oriented. A transition is a path, and path-wise constraints can bind mid-interval even when every audited snapshot is compliant. During a sustainability transition, when can a compensatory aggregate certify a path that a non-compensatory assessment rejects? We answer by formulating both criteria as robust viability problems on a common datum — a state space carrying normalized floors $s_i \ge 0$, an action set, a disturbance set, and exact-tube semantics under which a transition is safe only if every state visited along it satisfies the constraints (Aubin, 1991; Aubin, Bayen, and Saint-Pierre, 2011) — and comparing the two quantifier orders they induce: common-plan acceptance, $\exists a\, \forall w:\ a \in E_w(z)$, and per-weight acceptance, $\forall w\, \exists a_w:\ a_w \in E_w(z)$.

Three results organize the paper. First, at a fixed path the full-cone aggregate is lossless (componentwise nonnegativity is equivalent to nonnegativity of every weighted sum), and the non-compensatory admissible set is exactly the intersection of the scalarized admissible sets over the cone; the separation between the criteria is therefore purely dynamic, a matter of quantifier order rather than of scalarization blindness. Second, on an explicit rational datum the acceptance gap $\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{weak}} \setminus \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{typ}}$ is a region with nonempty interior — the impossibility region — on which every weighting licenses a transition, each licensing a different one, while no transition satisfies the floors path-wise; the same region, at a deeper reserve stock, is the rescue set, where a single reserve-financed plan serves every weighting. We give the exact per-weight licensing thresholds and a closed-form minimal rescue threshold. Third, the gap is a property of the action space's geometry: convexifying the menu closes it exactly at the compensatory region, whereas discrete time-sharing closes it only where both dips are independently subsumed.

The mechanism connects to four established strands. In viability and reach-avoid theory, our operators are typed instances of robust predecessor and capture-basin constructions (Aubin, 1991; Frankowska, 1989; Saint-Pierre, 1994; Cardaliaguet, 1996; Lygeros, Tomlin, and Sastry, 1999). In game theory, the two quantifier orders are the two orders of a minimax interchange for the binary payoff "action $a$ is admissible at $z$ under $w$"; the gap is a pure-strategy duality gap that the convex (mixed) extension closes (von Neumann, 1928; Sion, 1958). In robust optimization it is the here-and-now versus wait-and-see separation (Ben-Tal et al., 2004). It is distinct from the frontier-geometry limitation of weighted sums in multi-objective optimization (Das and Dennis, 1997), which concerns a single optimization rather than the admissibility quantifiers. Relative to these strands, the contribution is an explicit exact-tube witness of the separation in a compensatory/non-compensatory assessment setting, with exact thresholds and a convexification converse; the elementary quantifier fact itself is standard, and we claim no priority over it.

## 2.8 Section 9 — Discussion (rewritten, ~450 words)

**(a) What the operators formalize.** The scalarized family formalizes one compensatory criterion — a single index, nonnegative weights, substitution at those weights, disturbances respected — not "weak sustainability" simpliciter, which also encompasses comprehensive consumption, shadow prices, and intertemporal investment conditions (Neumayer, 2013; Dasgupta and Mäler, 2000). Likewise $E_{\mathrm{typ}}$ idealizes the separately-binding-floor reading of strong sustainability; critical-natural-capital frameworks may use thresholds, safe operating spaces, or resilience conditions (Ekins et al., 2003; Doyen and Gajardo, 2020). Within this scope, Theorem 1 states that the two criteria can disagree on the same datum, same robustness standard, same menu, in the direction compensatory-accepts / non-compensatory-rejects, on a relatively open region — and that the seat of the disagreement is the quantifier order, not any particular weight.

**(b) Reporting implications.** An index can be sound at the level of accounting and over-certify at the level of assessment, because certification is a quantifier statement about transitions. Where separately-binding floors matter, per-floor reporting is a detection requirement, not a presentation preference — the same logic that underlies safe-and-just-space assessments, which evaluate social floors and biophysical ceilings independently and find no country satisfying all (O'Neill et al., 2018; Fanning et al., 2022). A minimum report should state: floors and provenance; weights or weight family; policy quantifiers; disturbance set; tube versus endpoint status; terminal maintainability; action-set completeness; and normative premises. Regimes that audit endpoints only evaluate the weakest operator of the chain and license mid-interval breaches undetected.

**(c) Limitations.** Existence result with an open region, not a magnitude claim; finite horizon, exact tubes, specified disturbances; no empirical transfer; negative certificates are menu-relative; novelty statements reflect bounded search; computational tractability holds on finite explicit graphs but grid cardinality grows exponentially in the number of floors.

**(d) Computational check.** A deterministic exact-integer artifact checks the finite rational instance of Propositions 1–5, Theorem 1 and Remark 4 on a $31^3$ grid (29,791 states), including the critical ratios $\rho_1, \rho_2$ and their midpoint per state; all 25 checks reproduce exactly (Appendix B). This validates the symbolic classification on the enumerated instance; it does not prove the continuum statements.

## 2.9 Section 10 — Conclusion (~200 words)

The compensatory and non-compensatory traditions are usually compared as doctrines. Translated into assessment mechanics on a common datum, their divergence survives as a theorem about operators: common-plan and per-weight acceptance are related by a quantifier commutation that can fail on an open region, where every weighting certifies a transition, no transition is certified by all, and the certified set splits into states rescuable by a reserve-financed plan and states impossible under every weight. The mechanism is not scalarization blindness — at fixed paths the full-cone aggregate is lossless — but the policy dependence of the aggregate-feasible transition, and by Theorem 3 it is a property of the menu's convexity rather than of either tradition. For index construction the consequence is concrete: the bridge a composite index needs is a single transition serving every admissible weight, and the relevant question for a reporting convention is whether its action set admits the convexification that supplies one — temporal alternation alone does not.

## 1. The biggest improvement: replace the “bespoke witness” with a general geometric theorem

The previous response kept the paper centered on a particular four-action witness. That makes the result look like a clever but artificial example. A stronger JEDC version would show that the witness is an instance of a general geometric fact about finite menus, scalarization, and convexification.

### The improved mathematical core

Suppose each action \(a\) can be summarized by a **required-margin vector** \(d_a \in \mathbb{R}^n_+\). For the witness:

\[
d_{\mathrm{FAST}} = (2,0), \qquad d_{\mathrm{SLOW}} = (0,2).
\]

A plan is noncompensatorily feasible if its required dip is dominated coordinatewise by the available margins:

\[
d_a \le s.
\]

The scalarized test asks whether, for every nonnegative weight vector \(w\), some action keeps the weighted margin nonnegative:

\[
w \cdot d_a \le w \cdot s.
\]

Define the menu’s required-margin set:

\[
D = \{d_a : a \in \mathcal A\}.
\]

Then the following proposition becomes the real center of the paper.

### Proposition: scalarized feasibility equals convexified common feasibility

Let

\[
C(D) = \operatorname{conv}(D) + \mathbb{R}^n_+
\]

be the comprehensive convex hull of the menu’s required-margin vectors. Then for any margin vector \(s\),

\[
\forall w \in \mathbb{R}^n_+ \setminus \{0\} \ \exists d \in D
\text{ such that }
w \cdot d \le w \cdot s
\]

if and only if

\[
\exists d \in \operatorname{conv}(D)
\text{ such that }
d \le s,
\]

i.e.

\[
s \in \operatorname{conv}(D) + \mathbb{R}^n_+.
\]

The original nonconvex menu is feasible if and only if

\[
\exists d \in D \text{ such that } d \le s,
\]

i.e.

\[
s \in \bigcup_{d \in D} (d + \mathbb{R}^n_+).
\]

Therefore the acceptance gap is exactly

\[
\left[\operatorname{conv}(D) + \mathbb{R}^n_+\right]
\setminus
\left[\bigcup_{d \in D} (d + \mathbb{R}^n_+)\right].
\]

### Why this is a genuine improvement

This does several important things:

1. It turns the example into a general theorem.
2. It explains why convexification closes the gap.
3. It gives a clean geometric picture of the impossibility region.
4. It answers the obvious referee objection: “Isn’t this just \(\exists \forall \neq \forall \exists\)?”  
   Yes, but the paper then adds a structural characterization of the gap.
5. It makes the witness feel like a canonical two-dimensional illustration, not a contrived construction.

For the witness,

\[
D = \{(2,0), (0,2)\}.
\]

Then

\[
\bigcup_{d \in D} (d + \mathbb{R}^2_+)
=
\{s_1 \ge 2\} \cup \{s_2 \ge 2\},
\]

while

\[
\operatorname{conv}(D) + \mathbb{R}^2_+
=
\{s_1 + s_2 \ge 2,\ s_1,s_2 \ge 0\}.
\]

The difference is exactly the discrepancy triangle. That is much more intuitive than merely stating Theorem 5.

w_1 + w_2 = 1, \qquad w_i \ge 0.

\[
r = \frac{w_2}{w_1} \in [0,\infty],
\]

with \(r=\infty\) when \(w_1=0\).

For each action, define its licensing interval:

\[
R_a(s) = \{ r : a \text{ is admissible under weight ratio } r \}.
\]

At the witness point

\[
(x,s_1,s_2) = \left(\frac12,\frac65,\frac65\right),
\]

the intervals are:

\[
R_{\mathrm{FAST}} = \left[\frac23,\infty\right],
\]

\[
R_{\mathrm{SLOW}} = \left[0,\frac32\right].
\]

Their union covers the whole weight space:

\[
\left[\frac23,\infty\right] \cup \left[0,\frac32\right] = [0,\infty].
\]

But neither interval alone covers the whole weight space. Therefore:

\[
\forall r \ \exists a
\]

holds, while

\[
\exists a \ \forall r
\]

fails.

This figure would show:

- a horizontal line representing \(r \in [0,\infty]\);
- one interval for FAST;
- one interval for SLOW;
- their overlap;
- no interval spanning the whole line;
- in the rescue case, a third interval for STAGED spanning the whole line.

That is a much clearer graphical explanation of the impossibility region than only showing the triangle in \((s_1,s_2)\)-space.

### Suggested caption

*Weight-interval view at the witness point. FAST is licensed for \(r \ge 2/3\); SLOW is licensed for \(r \le 3/2\). Every weight ratio licenses at least one action, but no single action is licensed at every weight ratio. In the rescue case, STAGED supplies the full interval.*

This should have been in the previous response.

## 7. The reserve stock assumption should be handled more carefully

> The scalarized criterion aggregates the service floors \(s_1,s_2\) only. The reserve stock \(x\) is a bridge asset and is not counted in the aggregate service index.

- \(s_1,s_2\) are service-flow margins or covenant ratios;
- \(x\) is liquidity or budget reserve;
- the aggregate index measures service performance, not financial assets;
- the reserve is usable only through a specific staged action.

A better response would have added a robustness remark:

That is a genuine modeling clarification.
