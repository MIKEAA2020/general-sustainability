## 2. Should the paper be reworked for broader journals?

Yes — but not merely by simplifying the language. The paper needs a structural reorientation if the target is a broader journal.

As written, the abstract is aimed at specialists in viability theory and control. Terms like:

- “epistemic kernel”;
- “Isaacs-type drift condition”;
- “finite-fibre cases”;
- “certainty-equivalence trap”;
- “estimation-tube reduction”;
- “polyhedral finite-fibre cases”;

are meaningful to specialists, but they will obscure the contribution for a broader audience.

For a broader interdisciplinary journal, the paper should be reorganized around the problem, not around the six mechanisms.

A broader version should answer:

1. What is the practical problem?
   - Monitoring systems are imperfect.
   - Sustainability constraints must be respected.
   - Policymakers need to know whether failure is due to bad policy or impossible information.

2. What does the paper provide?
   - A set of rigorous tests that certify when no observation-based policy can succeed.

3. Why should non-specialists care?
   - It tells managers when better data can help and when the problem is structurally impossible.
   - It gives design rules for monitoring frequency, indicator resolution, aggregation, and bias correction.

4. What are the main mechanisms?
   - Do not list all six as equal headline results.
   - Present three core ideas:
     - no common safe action under merged information;
     - information arrives too late;
     - indicators cannot separate safe from unsafe states.
   - Treat the other mechanisms as supporting cases.

5. What is the practical implication?
   - Monitoring design is not just about more data.
   - The right data must arrive early enough and separate the states that require different actions.

- at least one concrete environmental or resource example;
- one or two intuitive figures;
- a short policy implications section;
- fewer theorem names in the abstract;
- proofs moved to an appendix.

- define the observation-based viability kernel clearly;
- present the certificates as exclusion tests for that kernel;
- add finite-horizon completeness;
- add computational or algorithmic content;
- connect the results to safety games, reachability, or barrier certificates.

So the answer is:

# 4. The core Automatica-level formulation

Define the problem in standard control language.

Consider:

\[
\dot x(t)=f(x(t),u(t),d(t)),
\]

with:

\[
u(t)\in U(x(t)),\qquad d(t)\in D(x(t)).
\]

Let the safe set be:

\[
\mathcal V=\{x: q_i(x)\ge 0,\ i=1,\dots,m\}.
\]

The state is partially observed through:

\[
y(t)=O(x(t))
\]

or more generally through an observation record:

\[
y_{[0,t]}.
\]

The belief at time \(t\) is:

\[
B_t
=
\{x(t): \text{there exists a compatible trajectory producing } y_{[0,t]}\}.
\]

An output-feedback policy chooses actions based on the belief:

\[
u(t)=\pi(B_t).
\]

Then define the robust output-feedback viability kernel:

\[
\mathcal K_{\mathcal I}
=
\{B_0:
\exists \pi
\text{ such that for all } x_0\in B_0
\text{ and all admissible } d(\cdot),
\ x(t)\in\mathcal V
\ \forall t\ge0
\}.
\]

That is the central object.

This is much cleaner than “epistemic kernel.”

# 5. The main theorem structure for Automatica

For Automatica, I would build the paper around four theorems.

## Theorem 1: Belief predecessor and kernel characterization

For sampled-data or discrete-time systems, define a predecessor operator:

\[
\operatorname{Pre}(\mathscr C)
=
\{B:
\exists a\in U^B(B)
\text{ such that all successor beliefs under }a
\text{ lie in }\mathscr C
\text{ and the tube remains safe}
\}.
\]

Here:

\[
U^B(B)=\bigcap_{x\in B}U(x).
\]

Then:

\[
\mathscr W_0=\{B:B\subseteq \mathcal V\},
\]

\[
\mathscr W_{k+1}=\operatorname{Pre}(\mathscr W_k).
\]

For finite horizon \(N\):

\[
B_0
\text{ is viable for }N\text{ steps}
\iff
B_0\in \mathscr W_N.
\]

For infinite horizon, under appropriate assumptions:

\[

=
\nu Z.\operatorname{Pre}(Z).
\]

This gives the paper a rigorous central object.

## Theorem 2: Finite-horizon completeness and obstruction trees

For finite systems, prove:

> If \(B_0\notin\mathscr W_N\), then there exists a finite obstruction tree certifying that every output-feedback policy fails within \(N\) steps.

This is important because it gives a complete certificate in a meaningful class.

- nodes are beliefs;
- controller branches are admissible actions;
- adversary branches are disturbances and observations;
- every leaf violates safety or leaves the viable set.

## Theorem 3: Local common-action obstruction

For continuous systems with review interval \(\Delta\), define the tube-safe action set:

\[
\mathcal A_{\mathrm{tube}}(B,\Delta)
=
\{a:
\operatorname{Reach}_{[0,\Delta]}(B,a)\subseteq\mathcal V
\}.
\]

Then:

\[
\mathcal A_{\mathrm{tube}}(B,\Delta)=\varnothing
\implies
B\notin\mathcal K_{\mathcal I}.
\]

A local sufficient condition is:

\[
\bigcap_{x\in B}\mathcal R_{\mathcal V}(x)=\varnothing,
\]

where:

\[
\mathcal R_{\mathcal V}(x)
=
\{u\in U(x):
\nabla q_i(x)\cdot f(x,u,d)\ge0
\ \forall d\in D(x),\ \forall i\in I(x)
\}.
\]

Here \(I(x)\) is the active-constraint set.

For polyhedral systems, this becomes a linear feasibility problem. If:

\[
\mathcal R_{\mathcal V}(x_j)=\{u:A_j u\le b_j\},
\]

then the common safe-action set is empty iff the stacked system:

\[
Au\le b
\]

is infeasible. A Farkas multiplier gives a finite certificate.

This is one of the most publishable parts.

## Theorem 4: Delayed-information obstruction

Define the worst-case blind-window survival time:

\[
\sigma^*(B_0)
=
\sup_{u(\cdot)}
\inf_{x_0\in B_0,d(\cdot)}
\tau(x_0,u,d),
\]

where \(\tau\) is the first exit time from \(\mathcal V\), and the infimum is taken over branches that remain observation-equivalent before the first informative observation.

Then:

\[
\sigma^*(B_0)<T_{\mathrm{obs}}
\implies
B_0\notin\mathcal K_{\mathcal I}.
\]

The current drift condition becomes a computable special case:

\[
D^+q\le -\varepsilon
\implies
\sigma^*(B_0)
\le
\frac{\inf_{x\in B_0}q(x)}{\varepsilon}.
\]

This is much stronger and more control-theoretic than the current presentation.

# 4. Build the paper around a theorem spine

The paper should have four principal results, with all examples and special mechanisms attached to them.

## Theorem 1: Exact finite-horizon information-state recursion

For a sampled-data or discrete-time model, define

\[
\operatorname{Pre}(\mathscr C)
=
\left\{
B:
\exists \alpha\in\mathfrak R_{\mathcal I}(B)
\text{ such that }
\begin{array}{l}
\text{all compatible trajectories are safe}\\
\text{during the current stage, and}\\
\Phi(B,\alpha,y^+)\in\mathscr C\\
\text{for every possible }y^+
\end{array}
\right\}.
\]

Let

\[
\mathscr W_0
=
\{B:B\subseteq\mathcal V_T\},
\qquad
\mathscr W_{k+1}
=
\operatorname{Pre}(\mathscr W_k).
\]

Then, in a finite model,

\[
B_0\in\mathscr W_N
\iff
\text{an observation-based policy keeps the system safe for }N\text{ stages}.
\]

For general spaces, this equivalence requires measurable-selection, compactness, and closedness assumptions. Those assumptions should be stated rather than hidden.

For infinite horizon, the expression

\[
\mathscr K_{\mathcal I}
=
\nu Z.\operatorname{Pre}(Z)
\]

is valid only under an appropriate complete-lattice and policy-selection framework. In finite systems,

\[
\mathscr K_{\mathcal I}
=
\bigcap_{N\ge0}\mathscr W_N
\]

is straightforward. In general spaces, finite-horizon viability for every \(N\) need not automatically produce one infinite-horizon measurable policy.

## Theorem 2: Obstruction-tree completeness

For finite state, action, disturbance, and observation spaces:

\[
B_0\notin\mathscr W_N
\]

if and only if the adversary has a finite-depth strategy proving failure within \(N\) stages.

The extracted obstruction tree should contain:

- information-state nodes;
- regulator action branches;
- adversarial disturbance/observation branches;
- terminal safety violations or terminally nonviable beliefs.

Be careful with continuous action spaces: a literal finite tree need not exist unless compactness, discretization, convex duality, or a finite-witness theorem is available.

## Theorem 3: Nested local obstruction certificates

For a held action over a review interval \(\Delta\), define

\[
U^B(B)=\bigcap_{z\in B}U(z),
\]

\[
\mathcal R_{\mathcal V}^{B}(B)
=
\bigcap_{z\in B}
\left\{
u\in U(z):
Dq_i(z;f(z,u,d))\ge0,\
\forall d,\ i\in I(z)
\right\},
\]

and

\[
\mathcal A_{\mathrm{tube}}(B,\Delta)
=
\left\{
u\in U^B(B):
\operatorname{Reach}_{[0,\Delta]}(B,u)
\subseteq\mathcal V
\right\}.
\]

Then, under appropriate tangency assumptions,

\[
\mathcal A_{\mathrm{tube}}(B,\Delta)
\subseteq
\mathcal R_{\mathcal V}^{B}(B)
\subseteq
U^B(B).
\]

Therefore each of the following is a sound exclusion certificate:

\[
U^B(B)=\varnothing,
\]

\[
\mathcal R_{\mathcal V}^{B}(B)=\varnothing,
\]

\[
\mathcal A_{\mathrm{tube}}(B,\Delta)=\varnothing.
\]

Do not imply that instantaneous tangency and finite-interval tube safety are equivalent. A common tangential action can still produce exit later in the review interval.

For a finite-\(\Delta\) theorem based on derivatives, introduce a uniform adverse margin and regularity assumptions. For example, if every common action produces

\[
D^+q_i(z;f(z,u,d))\le-\eta
\]

for some compatible active branch and a uniform \(\eta>0\), then one can establish finite-time exit or tube infeasibility.

## Theorem 4: Blind-window or delayed-information obstruction

Define the class of controls implementable before the first branch-separating observation:

\[
\mathcal U_{\mathrm{blind}}(B_0,T_{\mathrm{obs}}).
\]

Then define

\[
\sigma^*(B_0)
=
\sup_{u\in\mathcal U_{\mathrm{blind}}}
\inf_{\substack{z_0\in B_0\\ d(\cdot)}}
\tau_{\mathcal V}(z_0,u,d).
\]

The principal timing certificate is

\[
\sigma^*(B_0)<T_{\mathrm{obs}}
\implies
B_0\notin\mathscr K_{\mathcal I}.
\]

The admissible controls in this definition should not automatically be called open-loop. They are strategies adapted to the observations available during the blind interval; they reduce to open-loop controls only if no informative observations arrive.

# 5. Strengthen the polyhedral result

The Farkas certificate is one of the most promising parts, but it should be developed beyond the elementary statement that an infeasible linear system has a dual certificate.

Suppose

\[
\mathcal R_{\mathcal V}(z_j)
=
\{u:A_j u\le b_j\}.
\]

After stacking constraints,

\[
Au\le b,
\]

common safe-action emptiness is certified by

\[
\lambda\ge0,
\qquad
\lambda^\top A=0,
\qquad
\lambda^\top b<0.
\]

To make this a stronger contribution, consider adding one or more of the following.

## Finite-witness theorem

If the safe-action sets are convex subsets of \(\mathbb R^{m_u}\), use a Helly-type result to show that emptiness can be witnessed by at most \(m_u+1\) branchwise sets, subject to the required finite-family or compactness assumptions.

This would give a powerful conclusion:

> Even when the belief contains many compatible states, common-action nonviability may admit a sparse witness involving only a small number of states.

## Vertex reduction

If the belief is polyhedral and the constraints depend affinely on the state, prove conditions under which checking the vertices of the belief polytope is sufficient.

Without such a result, “stacking the constraints for all \(z\in B\)” is not a finite computation when \(B\) is infinite.

## Semi-infinite duality

For continuously parameterized beliefs, formulate common-action feasibility as a robust or semi-infinite program and derive conditions for strong duality and finite certificate extraction.

These additions would make the computational contribution more defensible for Automatica.

A strong introduction should separate:

# 10. Revised contribution statement

A defensible contribution paragraph would be:

> This paper studies robust safety under partial observation through an information-state viability formulation. First, an information-state predecessor operator is introduced, yielding an exact finite-horizon viability characterization under finite-model assumptions and finite adversarial certificates when viability fails. Second, local exclusion tests are derived from common admissibility, robust tangency, and review-interval tube safety. Third, delayed observations are treated through a blind-window survival-time value function. Finally, for convex and polyhedral safe-action sets, common-action nonviability is reduced to tractable feasibility problems with finite dual certificates. This wording avoids overclaiming while clearly identifying the framework.

# 11. Revised Automatica-style abstract

> We study robust viability of constrained systems under partial observation. The state available to the regulator is an information set containing all trajectories compatible with the observation and control histories. We define an information-state predecessor operator and characterize finite-horizon output-feedback viability by backward recursion. In finite systems, the characterization is exact and nonviability admits finite adversarial obstruction certificates. For continuous-time systems, we derive sound local exclusion tests based on common admissibility, robust tangency, review-interval tube safety, and survival before informative observations arrive. For convex and polyhedral safe-action sets, common-action infeasibility admits finite dual certificates, including Farkas multipliers. These results provide checkable conditions showing that no admissible output-feedback policy can maintain safety, including cases in which every compatible state is individually viable under full information.

# 12. Highest-priority revision sequence

The manuscript should be revised in this order:

1. **Fix the policy, information, and disturbance semantics.**
2. **Define the information-state predecessor precisely.**
3. **Prove the finite-horizon theorem with exact assumptions.**
4. **Identify what is genuinely new relative to safety games.**
5. **Strengthen the convex/polyhedral result with finite-witness or duality theory.**
6. **Prove the delayed-information theorem using an implementable blind-window policy class.**
7. **Add an algorithm and a nontrivial numerical study.**
8. **Move fibre certification and certainty equivalence to remarks or examples.**
9. **Reduce sustainability rhetoric and reorganize around the central kernel.**
10. **Choose SCL or Automatica only after determining whether the new theorem supports the broader claim.**

The most important substantive improvement is therefore not merely to replace six mechanisms with one framework. It is to ensure that the framework produces at least one result that is both **nonstandard and technically consequential**—ideally a finite or sparse obstruction theorem, a rigorous continuous-to-discrete certificate, or a scalable dual computation method.
