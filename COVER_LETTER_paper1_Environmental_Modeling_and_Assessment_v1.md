# Cover Letter — Paper 1 (*Environmental Modeling & Assessment*)

**Title:** Aggregate Indices and Transition Safety: A Quantifier-Order Separation Between Scalarized and Coordinate-Wise Feasibility
**Author:** Amin Abaee (Independent Researcher)
**Journal:** *Environmental Modeling & Assessment* (Springer)

---

Dear Editor,

I am submitting the manuscript **"Aggregate Indices and Transition Safety"** for consideration in *Environmental Modeling & Assessment*.

**Context and importance.** Environmental policy is increasingly steered by quantitative sustainability assessment: on one side, composite indices and aggregate accounts; on the other, separately-binding environmental floors — critical natural capital, planetary boundaries, safe operating spaces. These are two reading conventions for the same underlying question: is a planned transition sustainable? The paper addresses a foundational gap in that practice: when a weighted aggregate index certifies a transition as sustainable, does any single transition actually keep every floor safe along the way? It proves that the two readings can separate — the aggregate can pass while no available plan passes — and it locates exactly where and why.

**What the paper does.** The manuscript models both readings as robust transition-safety problems on a common datum and proves a separation: there are explicit, finite transition data on which every nonnegative weighting individually accepts some transition, yet no single transition is accepted under all weightings at once. The acceptance gap is identified precisely with an impossibility region, which a reserve threshold splits into states that a resource-controlled action can rescue and states that it cannot. Two bounding results complete the picture: convexifying the action menu — running plans simultaneously as a fractional mix — closes the gap exactly, whereas merely alternating between plans over time does not. The separation is therefore a property of the assessment's action space, not of any particular doctrine. The finite witness is verified independently by exact-integer computation, with no floating-point arithmetic.

**An environmental picture.** Consider two water reservoirs, each with a legal minimum level, and a limited reserve. One operating mode briefly draws down the first reservoir mid-interval and then refills it; a second mode does the same to the other; a third avoids both drawdowns but requires the reserve. An assessor who reads only a weighted total of the two levels may find that, for every weighting, some mode keeps the total above the floor — while no single mode keeps both reservoirs above their minima, and the reserve is too small to phase the change gradually. That is the gap the paper formalizes, and it is precisely the situation created when an assessment compresses several separately-binding environmental standards into one number.

**Fit with the journal.** The paper sits squarely within the journal's remit of environmental problems with "a significant quantitative modeling or analytic component, interpreted broadly." Its methods — viability and reachability analysis (control theory), convex geometry, and mathematical economics — are among the techniques the journal explicitly invites, and the results are generic: they hold for any finite menu of plans on a typed transition datum, not for a single resource system. The environmental relevance is direct: the two floors formalize separately-binding environmental standards, and the practical message — that where floors matter they should be reported individually, because an aggregate alone cannot tell a fixable case from an impossible one — is a concrete, decision-relevant lesson for composite-index design in environmental reporting. The paper is primarily theoretical, and it is offered in the category the journal itself provides for such work: results that deliver conceptual insight into an environmental problem.
