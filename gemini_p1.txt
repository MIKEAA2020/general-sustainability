\subsection{1. Introduction}\label{introduction}

\subsubsection{1.1 Two failure modes}\label{two-failure-modes}

Sustainability assessment sits on a conceptual fault line that has divided the field since its inception. The weak-sustainability tradition, formalized in the genuine-savings and inclusive-wealth frameworks (World Bank, 2011; Boos, 2015; Neumayer, 2013), treats distinct forms of capital as mutually substitutable through shadow prices. Under this view, the depletion of natural capital poses no threat to sustainability as long as the aggregate monetary value of the total capital base does not decline. The strong-sustainability tradition argues, by contrast, that critical natural assets and ecosystem services are separately binding constraints whose loss cannot be compensated at any price (Daly, 1990; Ekins et al., 2003). Its foundational premise in ecological economics is the thesis of weak comparability: the diverse values at stake in environmental decisions cannot be reduced to a single, commensurable metric (Martinez-Alier, Munda, and O'Neill, 1998). This debate is well established in environmental policy and multi-criteria decision analysis (Cinelli, Coles, and Kirwan, 2014; Sch\"ar, Pohl, and Geldermann, 2025; Hanley et al., 1999; Usubiaga-Lia\~no, 2025), and the mathematical theory of sustainability indicators is correspondingly mature (Martinet, 2011; Cairns and Martinet, 2014), including the maximin formulation where strong sustainability arises from constraint viability rather than an optimality criterion (Solow, 1974; Doyen and Gajardo, 2020).

Through the lens of this paper, these two traditions diverge on whether compensatory substitution can keep pace with depletion over time. The material-cycle reading of this distinction—viewing waste as an open loop, requiring cycles to close at the rate of throughput, and ruling out substitution against deep-time renewal—is analyzed in a companion study (Abaee, 2026a) and is not re-argued here; no theorem in this paper addresses material cycles directly. Instead, we compare the formal assessment operators defined in Section 3.1 and interpreted doctrinally in Section 5.1.

Here, we examine how that conceptual distinction manifests in dynamic certification. Our blend-collapse result (Theorem 8) shows that the gap between weak and strong assessment vanishes precisely across the compensatory region where convexified substitution—the continuous, fractional allocation of control effort—keeps pace with depletion. Conversely, the impossibility region of Theorem 5(4) identifies where substitution fails, even under discrete time-sharing. In our framework, substitution is admissible only along explicit, physically grounded pathways; we do not assume compensation is possible simply because an aggregate index remains non-negative. The broader question of aggregate maintenance—whether total capital can be preserved—turns out to be ambiguous between two distinct quantifier orderings, which we formalize in Remark 1 and Section 4.5.

This paper addresses a question that the indicator literature has left comparatively open: the \emph{dynamic} question. During a sustainability transition, when can a compensatory aggregate certify a trajectory that a noncompensatory assessment rejects? Two practical failure modes motivate this inquiry.

The first is \emph{commensurability drift}. Sustainability assessments routinely bundle disparate stocks, flows, liabilities, and safety thresholds into single composite indices whose underlying substitution rules are rarely made mathematically explicit. Composite indices are widely criticized on this basis: the weights they assign reflect implicit value judgments disguised as objective measurement (Hickel, 2020; Martinez-Alier, Munda, and O'Neill, 1998). However, standard critiques usually focus on the specific \emph{choice} of weights. Here, we establish a stronger structural result: no choice of weights can resolve the underlying tension. The failure is not an artifact of poor calibration; it is embedded in the logic of aggregation itself.

The second failure mode is the widespread tendency to treat conditional findings as unconditional certificates. To avoid this, every result presented below explicitly defines its boundary conditions, model assumptions, and verification status.

The dynamic masking formalized here is concrete: a weighted aggregate of two typed service floors can remain safely non-negative along an entire worst-case trajectory tube, even while each individual floor undergoes a temporary deficit that no single control policy can prevent. This is distinct from the \emph{productivity illusion}—where adequate service delivery conceals a degrading resource base, as analyzed in Abaee (2026a). On the witness system constructed below, the aggregate is defined directly over two service floors, \(s_1\) and \(s_2\). What the aggregate misses is that each floor drops below its threshold mid-interval. This dynamic blind spot constitutes the acceptance gap characterized in Theorem 5(4).

\subsubsection{1.2 The central result}\label{the-central-result}

The paper's core finding is a failure of quantifiers to commute. Consider a typed transition system: a state space constrained by typed floors \(s_i \ge 0\) (normalized to zero), an action set, a disturbance set, and exact-tube semantics under which a transition is safe only if every state visited along its trajectory—not merely its endpoint—satisfies the constraints. For each non-negative weight vector \(w\) in the punctured positive orthant \(W_+ = \mathbb{R}^n_+ \setminus \{0\}\), let \(E_w(z)\) denote the set of actions admissible at state \(z\) under the aggregate floor \(w \cdot s \ge 0\), and let \(E_{\mathrm{typ}}(z)\) denote the set of actions admissible when each typed floor \(s_i \ge 0\) is enforced separately. Two distinct acceptance criteria arise, differing solely in the order of their quantifiers:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Common-plan acceptance (noncompensatory).} A single action is safe under every scalarization weight:
  \[\exists a\; \forall w:\; a \in E_w(z).\]
\item
  \textbf{Per-weight acceptance (compensatory).} For each weight, there exists some safe action, which may vary with the weight:
  \[\forall w\; \exists a_w:\; a_w \in E_w(z).\]
\end{itemize}

Common-plan acceptance implies per-weight acceptance, but the converse fails. This failure is not a flaw of any particular weight choice; it reflects the noncommutation of the existential quantifier over actions with the universal quantifier over weights. This noncommutation is the assessment-theoretic counterpart of the strict minimax inequality in game theory: treating the condition ``action \(a\) is admissible at \(z\) under weight \(w\)'' as a binary payoff, the two quantifier orders correspond to the two sides of the minimax interchange. Section 4.5 provides an explicit system where this interchange strictly fails. 

Remark 1 formalizes the general inclusion; Proposition 3(ii) establishes that the noncompensatory operator coincides with the common-plan set over the full cone; and Theorem 5 constructs an explicit rational witness where the acceptance gap \(\mathrm{FP}_{\mathrm{agg}} = \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{weak}} \setminus \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{typ}}\) forms a region with a non-empty interior—the \emph{impossibility region} (Theorem 5(4)). The witness further partitions the discrepancy region \(\mathcal{Q}\) (where the aggregate and individual floors diverge) into this impossibility region and a \emph{rescue set}, whose states can be safely transitioned using a dedicated, resource-backed action (Theorem 5(7)). The rescue operation analyzed in Section 5.5—resource augmentation—operates specifically on the impossibility region, not on the rescue set.

\subsubsection{1.3 Contribution and scope}\label{contribution-and-scope}

\textbf{Contributions.} 
\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
\tightlist
\item An action-set identity, \(E_{\mathrm{typ}}(z) = \bigcap_{w \in W_+} E_w(z)\), showing that evaluating over the full non-negative cone isolates the separation as purely dynamic rather than static (Proposition 3(ii)).
\item A general quantifier-separation principle (Remark 1).
\item A proof that the accepted-state hierarchy contracts monotonically as the family of permitted weights expands (Proposition 4).
\item An explicit rational witness exhibiting an open region of strict separation, the discrepancy partition \(\mathcal{Q} = R \cup I\), the identification \(\mathrm{FP}_{\mathrm{agg}} = I\), closed-form per-weight licensing thresholds (Theorem 5), and the exact rescue threshold \(\kappa^* = 1 - x\) (Section 5.5).
\item A demonstration that this separation persists when the planning horizon is extended by prepending hold intervals (Remark 6).
\item An explicit two-stage erasure model showing that downstream recourse can eliminate the gap, confirming that propagation across stages depends on the system architecture (Theorem 7).
\item The blend-collapse theorem: convexifying the action menu eliminates the acceptance gap precisely across the compensatory region (Theorem 8), whereas discrete time-sharing closes it only where both floors can survive their deficits independently—leaving the gap intact across the impossibility region (Proposition 9).
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Scope.} This paper does not assert a universal hierarchy between weak and strong sustainability doctrines; it does not claim that any specific empirical boundary must be managed noncompensatorily; nor does it evaluate real-world resource systems. Extensions addressing governance structures, intergenerational equity, and composite system architectures are reported with partial status in the Supplementary Material.

To our knowledge, this work offers the first explicit, exact-tube witness demonstrating dynamic quantifier separation between compensatory and noncompensatory sustainability assessments. The elementary logical property behind the separation is standard; its novelty and boundaries are delineated in Section 5.2.

\subsection{2. A Typed Framework for Transition Assessment}\label{a-typed-framework-for-transition-assessment}

This section defines the mathematical objects and semantics required for the analysis.

\subsubsection{2.1 Types and physical state}\label{types-and-physical-state}

Physical state variables are strictly typed. Each state variable represents a \emph{moiety}—a distinct, conserved physical substance—measured in explicit units, and typed fluxes connect typed stocks. Conservation laws hold strictly per moiety: the framework does not permit adding biomass, currency, and biodiversity into a single conserved scalar. Service flows, critical thresholds, informational states, and institutional variables carry distinct types. Quantities of different types are never combined without an explicit physical bridge. 

This typing discipline serves two purposes: it clarifies the domain of every physical conservation law, and it records the provenance of every floor—whether derived from physical dynamics, contractual commitments, or normative targets—as an intrinsic part of its definition.

\subsubsection{2.2 The assessment datum}\label{the-assessment-datum}

Our results are defined over a typed exact-tube transition datum: a state space \(Z\) carrying typed floors \(s_i \ge 0\) (normalized to zero), a discrete action menu, a disturbance set \(D\), a typed safe set \(S\), a destination set \(G\), and deterministic tube and successor mappings \(\mathrm{Tube}(a,d)\) and \(\mathrm{Succ}(a,d)\) as formalized in Section 3.1. Under exact-tube semantics, a transition is certified as safe if and only if every state visited along the entire continuous-time trajectory—not just the endpoint—remains within the safe set. The witness constructed in Section 4.5 is a concrete instance of this datum.

\subsubsection{2.3 Quantifier discipline}\label{quantifier-discipline}

Every assessment operator places the disturbance quantifier innermost: an action is admissible only if it guarantees safety against \emph{every} disturbance \(d \in D\). The separation analyzed in Section 3 introduces an outer quantifier over the scalarization weights, examining the noncommutation between \(\exists a\, \forall w\) and \(\forall w\, \exists a_w\).

\subsubsection{2.4 Notation}\label{notation}

The following notation is used throughout the paper:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \(z \in Z\): system state; \(a\): an action; \(d \in D\): an environmental disturbance; \(s = (s_1, \ldots, s_n)\): typed service floors (normalized such that \(s_i \ge 0\) represents the threshold); \(x\): reserve stock (bridge capital) in the witness system.
\item
  \(W_+ = \mathbb{R}^n_+ \setminus \{0\}\): the positive orthant of non-negative scalarization weights excluding the origin (Section 3.1); \(w \in W_+\): a scalarization weight vector; \(W \subseteq W_+\): a restricted weight family (Proposition 4); \(r = w_2 / w_1\): the weight ratio used in the two-floor witness; \(\kappa\): the resource augmentation increment (Section 5.5).
\item
  \(S = S^{\mathrm{phys}} \cap \{ s_i \ge 0,\ i = 1,\dots,n \}\) and \(G = G^{\mathrm{phys}} \cap \{ s \ge 0 \}\): typed safe and destination sets; \(S^w, G^w\): their scalarized counterparts under weight \(w\); \(S^{\mathrm{phys}}, G^{\mathrm{phys}}\): their underlying physical bounds; on the witness system, \(S = S_0\).
\item
  \(\mathrm{Tube}(a,d)\): the exact set of states visited throughout the review interval under action \(a\) and disturbance \(d\); \(\mathrm{End}(a,d)\): the set of terminal states reached at the end of the interval; \(\mathrm{Succ}(a,d)\): the successor state set following destination reset.
\item
  \(E_{\mathrm{typ}}(z)\), \(E_w(z)\), \(E_{\mathrm{tube,phys}}(z)\), \(E_{\mathrm{end}}(z)\), \(E_{\mathrm{end,typ}}(z)\): the five assessment operators defined in Section 3.1.
\item
  \(\mathcal{V}[E] = \{ z : E(z) \neq \varnothing \}\): the set of states accepted by operator \(E\). Specifically, \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{typ}} = \mathcal{V}[E_{\mathrm{typ}}]\), \(\mathcal{V}_w = \mathcal{V}[E_w]\), \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{weak}} = \bigcap_{w \in W_+} \mathcal{V}_w\), \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{phys}} = \mathcal{V}[E_{\mathrm{tube,phys}}]\), and \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{end}} = \mathcal{V}[E_{\mathrm{end}}]\).
\item
  Witness parameters (Section 4.5): state vector \((q, x, s_1, s_2)\); terminal gain vector \(e = (1/4, 1/4)\); transition cost \(c = 1\); worst-case dip depth \(2\); action menu \{NO-SWITCH, FAST, SLOW, STAGED\}.
\item
  Discrepancy sets (Section 4.6): \(\mathcal{Q}\): aggregate-versus-direct discrepancy region; \(R\): rescue set; \(I\): impossibility region; \(\mathrm{FP}_{\mathrm{agg}} = \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{weak}} \setminus \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{typ}}\): acceptance gap; \(\rho_1, \rho_2\): weight-ratio licensing thresholds.
\item
  \(\mathcal{A}\): the action menu; \(\mathsf{Aug}_\kappa\): the resource augmentation mapping, with minimal rescue threshold \(\kappa^*\).
\end{itemize}

Note that the gain vector \(e\) of the witness system is distinct from the standard basis vector \(e_k\) used in Remark 2.

\subsection{3. Assessment Operators}\label{assessment-operators}

\subsubsection{3.1 Five operators}\label{five-operators}

Consider a typed exact-tube transition datum with transition-safe sets and a destination target. For any state \(z\) and candidate action \(a\), let \(\mathrm{Tube}(a,d)\) be the continuous set of states traversed over the interval under disturbance \(d\), let \(\mathrm{End}(a,d)\) be the terminal state reached at the interval's boundary, and let \(\mathrm{Succ}(a,d)\) be the successor set following any post-transition reset. 

We distinguish five assessment operators. All five share the same robust disturbance quantifier ($\forall d \in D$), differing only in their constraint geometry and temporal scope. The first four operate either on full trajectory tubes or on isolated endpoints; the fifth is a typed-endpoint operator used in Section 5.4.

The \textbf{noncompensatory typed} operator enforces every physical and service constraint separately along the entire trajectory:
\[E_{\mathrm{typ}}(z) = \{ a : \forall d,\; \mathrm{Tube}(a,d) \subseteq S \ \text{ and } \ \mathrm{Succ}(a,d) \subseteq G \},\]
where \(S = S^{\mathrm{phys}} \cap \{ s_i \ge 0,\ i = 1,\dots,n \}\) and \(G = G^{\mathrm{phys}} \cap \{ s \ge 0 \}\).

The \textbf{scalarized aggregate} operator evaluates constraints against a single composite index defined by weight \(w \in W_+\):
\[E_w(z) = \{ a : \forall d,\; \mathrm{Tube}(a,d) \subseteq S^w \ \text{ and } \ \mathrm{Succ}(a,d) \subseteq G^w \},\]
where \(S^w = S^{\mathrm{phys}} \cap \{ w \cdot s \ge 0 \}\) and \(G^w = G^{\mathrm{phys}} \cap \{ w \cdot s \ge 0 \}\).

The \textbf{exact-tube physical} operator enforces only physical constraints, ignoring service floors, across the full trajectory:
\[E_{\mathrm{tube,phys}}(z) = \{ a : \forall d,\; \mathrm{Tube}(a,d) \subseteq S^{\mathrm{phys}} \ \text{ and } \ \mathrm{Succ}(a,d) \subseteq G^{\mathrm{phys}} \}.\]

The \textbf{endpoint-only physical} operator evaluates physical constraints strictly at the terminal boundary:
\[E_{\mathrm{end}}(z) = \{ a : \forall d,\; \mathrm{End}(a,d) \subseteq S^{\mathrm{phys}} \ \text{ and } \ \mathrm{Succ}(a,d) \subseteq G^{\mathrm{phys}} \}.\]

The endpoint operator reflects periodic snapshot audits (such as annual reporting), whereas typed floors continuously govern the full trajectory: it is the difference between inspecting a photograph and reviewing the entire film. Terminal snapshots cannot, in general, verify whether the system maintained its structural integrity along the way (see the analysis of the productivity illusion in Abaee, 2026a). 

Because \(\mathrm{End}(a,d) \subseteq \mathrm{Tube}(a,d)\), the endpoint operator imposes the least restrictive requirement. Consequently, the inclusion chain
\[E_{\mathrm{typ}}(z) \;\subseteq\; E_w(z) \;\subseteq\; E_{\mathrm{tube,phys}}(z) \;\subseteq\; E_{\mathrm{end}}(z)\]
holds for every weight \(w \in W_+\) (Proposition 3(i)). While the final inclusion is strict in general dynamical systems, it collapses to an equality on our specific witness system because its only physical constraint governs a monotonically decreasing reserve stock.

\textbf{Definition (typed-endpoint operator).} The \textbf{typed-endpoint} operator tests typed floors exclusively at the terminal boundary:
\[E_{\mathrm{end,typ}}(z) = \{ a : \forall d,\; \mathrm{End}(a,d) \subseteq S \ \text{ and } \ \mathrm{Succ}(a,d) \subseteq G \}.\]
It satisfies \(E_{\mathrm{typ}}(z) \subseteq E_{\mathrm{end,typ}}(z) \subseteq E_{\mathrm{end}}(z)\), because \(\mathrm{End}(a,d) \subseteq \mathrm{Tube}(a,d)\), \(S \subseteq S^{\mathrm{phys}}\), and \(G \subseteq G^{\mathrm{phys}}\). 

On the witness datum of Section 4.5, the action FAST restores \(s_1\) to its initial value by the end of the interval, and its successor lands in \(G\) whenever \(x \ge 0\) (see the action table in Section 4.5 and the proof of Theorem 5(1)). Thus, \(E_{\mathrm{end,typ}}(z) \neq \varnothing\) across all states in \(X_0\), whereas true typed safety, \(E_{\mathrm{typ}}(z) \neq \varnothing\), strictly requires \(s_1 \ge 2\). This observation is immediate from inspection of the dynamics and is treated separately from the machine-checked rational proofs described in Section 4.9.

\subsubsection{3.2 Accepted-state operators}\label{accepted-state-operators}

For any operator \(E\), we define its accepted-state set as:
\[\mathcal{V}[E] \;=\; \{ z : E(z) \neq \varnothing \}.\]
This yields \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{typ}} = \mathcal{V}[E_{\mathrm{typ}}]\), \(\mathcal{V}_w = \mathcal{V}[E_w]\), \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{phys}} = \mathcal{V}[E_{\mathrm{tube,phys}}]\), and \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{end}} = \mathcal{V}[E_{\mathrm{end}}]\). 

The compensatory accepted set across all non-negative weights is:
\[\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{weak}} \;=\; \bigcap_{w \in W_+} \mathcal{V}_w .\]
The central noncommutation between common-plan and per-weight acceptance is then expressed as:
\[\underbrace{\{ z : \textstyle\bigcap_w E_w(z) \neq \varnothing \}}_{\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{typ}} \ \text{(common plan)}} \;\subseteq\; \underbrace{\bigcap_w \{ z : E_w(z) \neq \varnothing \}}_{\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{weak}} \ \text{(per weight)}}.\]

\subsection{4. Results: The Separation Theorem}\label{results-the-separation-theorem}

\subsubsection{4.1 A general quantifier-separation remark}\label{a-general-quantifier-separation-remark}

\textbf{Remark 1.} \emph{Let \(\{E_\lambda(z)\}_{\lambda \in \Lambda}\) be an arbitrary family of action sets. Then the following inclusion always holds:}
\[\Big\{ z : \textstyle\bigcap_{\lambda} E_\lambda(z) \neq \varnothing \Big\} \;\subseteq\; \bigcap_{\lambda} \big\{ z : E_\lambda(z) \neq \varnothing \big\}.\]
\emph{Equality holds if and only if, for every \(z\) in the right-hand set, the family \(\{E_\lambda(z)\}_\lambda\) admits a common selector. No topological structure on the action space is assumed. When the action menu is finite, this condition can be verified combinatorially: only finitely many distinct sets \(E_\lambda(z)\) can arise, reducing the search for a common selector to finite enumeration (on our witness system, \(|\mathcal{A}| = 4\)). For infinite action spaces, standard sufficient conditions—such as the finite intersection property paired with compactness—apply directly, though they are not required here.}

\emph{Proof.} If an action \(a\) belongs to \(\bigcap_\lambda E_\lambda(z)\), then every \(E_\lambda(z)\) contains \(a\) and is therefore non-empty, placing \(z\) in the right-hand intersection. The inclusion fails to reverse at \(z\) precisely when every \(E_\lambda(z)\) is individually non-empty but their intersection is empty—that is, when no single action satisfies all constraints simultaneously. \ensuremath{\square}

The separation demonstrated in Section 4.5 is a strict instance of this gap, occurring where the actions certifying safety vary with the chosen weight.

\subsubsection{4.2 The full-cone identity}\label{the-full-cone-identity}

\textbf{Remark 2 (full-cone pointwise equivalence).} \emph{For any vector \(v \in \mathbb{R}^n\),}
\[v \ge 0 \ \text{componentwise} \iff w \cdot v \ge 0 \ \text{for every } w \in W_+ = \mathbb{R}^n_+ \setminus \{0\}.\]

\emph{Proof.} 
\((\Rightarrow)\) If \(w \ge 0\), \(w \neq 0\), and \(v \ge 0\), the inner product satisfies \(w \cdot v = \sum_i w_i v_i \ge 0\). 

\((\Leftarrow)\) Suppose \(v_k < 0\) for some coordinate \(k\). Selecting the standard basis vector \(w = e_k \in W_+\) yields \(w \cdot v = v_k < 0\), establishing the contrapositive. \ensuremath{\square}

Remark 2 shows that evaluated along any \emph{fixed} trajectory, scalarization over the full non-negative cone is completely lossless: enforcing every non-negative linear combination is strictly equivalent to enforcing each constraint separately. The divergence established below is therefore entirely dynamic—a product of quantifier ordering—rather than a static limitation of linear indexing.

\subsubsection{4.3 The assessment identity and hierarchy}\label{the-assessment-identity-and-hierarchy}

\textbf{Proposition 3 (assessment identity and hierarchy).}

\emph{(i) Hierarchy.} \emph{For every weight \(w \in W_+\) and every state \(z\),}
\[E_{\mathrm{typ}}(z) \;\subseteq\; E_w(z) \;\subseteq\; E_{\mathrm{tube,phys}}(z) \;\subseteq\; E_{\mathrm{end}}(z).\]

\emph{(ii) Localization.} \emph{For every state \(z\),}
\[E_{\mathrm{typ}}(z) \;=\; \bigcap_{w \in W_+} E_w(z).\] 
\emph{Consequently, \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{typ}} = \{ z : \bigcap_w E_w(z) \neq \varnothing \}\).}

\emph{Proof.} 
(i) Let \(a \in E_{\mathrm{typ}}(z)\). For every disturbance \(d\), the trajectory satisfies \(\mathrm{Tube}(a,d) \subseteq S = S^{\mathrm{phys}} \cap \{s \ge 0\}\). By Remark 2, every point along the tube satisfies \(w \cdot s \ge 0\) for all \(w \in W_+\), and every successor state lies in \(G^w\); hence \(a \in E_w(z)\). The remaining inclusions follow immediately from \(S^w \subseteq S^{\mathrm{phys}}\), \(G^w \subseteq G^{\mathrm{phys}}\), and \(\mathrm{End}(a,d) \subseteq \mathrm{Tube}(a,d)\). 

(ii) Taking the intersection of the first inclusion in (i) over all \(w \in W_+\) yields \(E_{\mathrm{typ}}(z) \subseteq \bigcap_{w \in W_+} E_w(z)\). For the reverse inclusion, suppose \(a \in \bigcap_{w \in W_+} E_w(z)\). For every disturbance \(d\) and every trajectory point \(p \in \mathrm{Tube}(a,d)\), the condition \(w \cdot s(p) \ge 0\) holds for all \(w \in W_+\). Remark 2 implies that \(s(p) \ge 0\) componentwise, so \(\mathrm{Tube}(a,d) \subseteq S\). Applying the same reasoning to \(\mathrm{Succ}(a,d)\) places all successor states in \(G\). Therefore, \(a \in E_{\mathrm{typ}}(z)\). \ensuremath{\square}

Part (i) reflects standard constraint monotonicity over nested safe sets (Aubin, 1991; Frankowska, 1989). Part (ii) shows that noncompensatory safety can be localized as the common-plan core across the full weight cone; Theorem 5 demonstrates that this core can be strictly smaller than the compensatory accepted set.

\subsubsection{4.4 Weight-family monotonicity}\label{weight-family-monotonicity}

\textbf{Proposition 4 (weight-family monotonicity).} \emph{For any subfamily of weights \(W \subseteq W_+\), define the accepted set \(\mathcal{V}_W = \bigcap_{w \in W} \mathcal{V}_w\). Then}
\[\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{typ}} \;\subseteq\; \mathcal{V}_W \;\subseteq\; \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{phys}},\]
\emph{and if \(W_1 \subseteq W_2\), then \(\mathcal{V}_{W_2} \subseteq \mathcal{V}_{W_1}\).}

\emph{Proof.} 
For the upper bound: by Proposition 3(i), \(E_w(z) \subseteq E_{\mathrm{tube,phys}}(z)\) for every \(w\), so \(\mathcal{V}_w \subseteq \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{phys}}\). Intersecting over all \(w \in W\) preserves this inclusion. 

For the lower bound: if \(z \in \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{typ}}\), Proposition 3(ii) guarantees an action \(a \in \bigcap_{w \in W_+} E_w(z)\). This action necessarily belongs to \(\bigcap_{w \in W} E_w(z)\), meaning \(E_w(z)\) is non-empty for every \(w \in W\), and thus \(z \in \mathcal{V}_W\). 

Monotonicity follows directly: intersecting conditions over a larger weight family \(W_2\) imposes more restrictions, shrinking the resulting set of accepted states. \ensuremath{\square}

Proposition 4 highlights an important property: \emph{restricting} the set of permissible weights \emph{expands} the compensatory accepted set. The full cone \(W_+\) therefore represents the most demanding compensatory standard possible. Any separation between compensatory and noncompensatory acceptance established on the full cone persists a fortiori for any restricted weight family.

\subsubsection{4.5 The witness datum}\label{the-witness-datum}

To ground these theoretical relations, we construct an explicit, rational transition system that exhibits the separation proven in Theorem 5. 

The system transitions between two regimes—an extractive phase (\(q = 0\)) and a regenerative phase (\(q = 1\))—over a single normalized review interval \([0,1]\). The state vector is \((q, x, s_1, s_2)\), consisting of a physical reserve stock \(x\) and two typed service floors: \(s_1\) (protected-group service surplus) and \(s_2\) (remediation liability coverage), both normalized such that non-negativity is the binding constraint. The transition-safe set is \(S_0 = \{ x \ge 0, s_1 \ge 0, s_2 \ge 0 \}\). The target destination set is \(G = \{ (1, x, s) : x \ge 0, s \ge 0 \}\), within which future viability is secured by an invariant hold policy. Successful transitions apply a strictly positive terminal gain \(e = (1/4, 1/4)\) to both service floors; this ensures successor states lie strictly in the interior of \(G\), so none of the inequalities in Theorem 5 bind on the magnitude of \(e\). Deploying the reserve-backed transition (STAGED) incurs a physical capital cost of \(c = 1\).

\textbf{Disturbance convention (action-indexed).} The disturbance set is \(D = \{\beta, \alpha\}\). Disturbance \(\alpha\) induces a worst-case deficit of fixed depth 2 in the coordinate directly modified by the active policy (\(s_1\) for FAST, \(s_2\) for SLOW). Disturbance \(\beta\) acts analogously on the alternate coordinate. Disturbances do not affect coordinates that an action leaves untouched. 

Consequently, each action's worst-case trajectory tube reflects its own characteristic deficit. Even if a disturbance label is shared across actions, it operates only on the coordinate dynamically engaged by the policy: FAST leaves the \(s_2\)-tube undisturbed, and SLOW leaves the \(s_1\)-tube undisturbed.

The system provides four actions from any initial state \((0, x, s)\) with \(x \ge 0\) and \(s \ge 0\). Trajectories are continuous and piecewise-linear across breakpoints \(\{0, \tfrac12, 1\}\), and monotonic on each segment, ensuring that each calculated tube matches the exact set of visited states without approximation:

STAGED & \(x(t) = x - t\) on \([0,1]\); \newline floors grow monotonically to \(s + e\) & \(\{(1, x - 1, s + e)\}\) \\
\end{longtable}

Under worst-case disturbances, FAST traces the interval \([s_1 - 2, s_1]\) in coordinate \(s_1\) while holding \(s_2\) and \(x\) fixed; SLOW traces \([s_2 - 2, s_2]\) in coordinate \(s_2\) while holding \(s_1\) and \(x\) fixed; and STAGED draws the reserve stock down through \([x - 1, x]\) while maintaining or improving both service floors.

\subsubsection{4.6 Witnessed separation}\label{witnessed-separation}

\textbf{Theorem 5 (witnessed separation).} \emph{On the witness datum of Section 4.5, over initial states \(X_0 = \{(0, x, s) : x \ge 0, s \ge 0\}\), the following characterizations hold:}

\textbf{(1)}
\(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{typ}} = \{ x \ge 1 \} \cup \{ s_1 \ge 2 \} \cup \{ s_2 \ge 2 \}.\)

\textbf{(2)}
\(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{weak}} = \bigcap_{w \in W_+} \mathcal{V}_w = \{ x \ge 1 \} \cup \{ s_1 + s_2 \ge 2 \}.\)

\textbf{(3)}
\(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{phys}} = \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{end}} = X_0.\)

\emph{Define:}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item \(\mathcal{Q} = \{ s_1 < 2,\ s_2 < 2,\ s_1 + s_2 \ge 2 \}\): the discrepancy region where aggregate and individual floor requirements diverge (with \(x\) unconstrained). This set is not entirely composed of false positives, because whenever \(x \ge 1\), STAGED provides a viable noncompensatory plan.
\item \(R = \mathcal{Q} \cap \{ x \ge 1 \}\): the \textbf{rescue set}, containing states within the discrepancy region that can already achieve safe transition via STAGED at physical cost \(c = 1\).
\item \(I = \mathcal{Q} \cap \{ x < 1 \}\): the \textbf{impossibility region}.
\item \(\mathrm{FP}_{\mathrm{agg}} = \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{weak}} \setminus \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{typ}}\): the true acceptance gap between weak and strong assessment.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{(4)} \(\mathrm{FP}_{\mathrm{agg}} = I.\) \emph{The true acceptance gap coincides exactly with the impossibility region. It is non-empty, and its relative interior within the \(q = 0\) slice is given by \(\{ 0 < x < 1,\ 0 < s_1 < 2,\ 0 < s_2 < 2,\ s_1 + s_2 > 2 \}\). The rescue set \(R\) belongs entirely to \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{typ}}\) and does not contribute to the acceptance gap.}

\textbf{(5)} \emph{Both inclusions in the assessment hierarchy are strict: every state in \(I\) belongs to \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{weak}} \setminus \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{typ}}\), and the state \((x, s_1, s_2) = (\tfrac12, \tfrac1{10}, \tfrac1{10})\) lies in \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{phys}} \setminus \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{weak}}\).}

\textbf{(6) Per-weight plan disagreement.} \emph{On the relative interior of \(\mathcal{Q}\) (where \(s_2 > 0\) and \(s_2 < 2\)), let \(r = w_2 / w_1\) denote the relative weight ratio. Then FAST is licensed precisely when \(r \ge \rho_1\) and SLOW is licensed precisely when \(r \le \rho_2\), where}
\[\rho_1 = \frac{2 - s_1}{s_2}, \qquad \rho_2 = \frac{s_1}{2 - s_2}, \qquad \text{and} \qquad \rho_2 \ge \rho_1 \iff s_1 + s_2 \ge 2.\]
\emph{Assessors who place high relative value on the second floor (\(r > \rho_2\)) license only FAST; assessors placing low relative value on it (\(r < \rho_1\)) license only SLOW; intermediate assessors license both. On the impossibility region \(I\), no single action is valid for all weights simultaneously—meaning \(\bigcap_w E_w(z) = E_{\mathrm{typ}}(z) = \varnothing\). On the rescue set \(R\), STAGED is typed-admissible and satisfies all weights simultaneously.}

\textbf{(7) The rescue split.} \emph{States in \(R\) are transformable under the strong doctrine via STAGED: investing one unit of physical reserve stock (\(c = 1\)) preserves both floors throughout the transition and lands safely in \(G\). States in \(I\), while certified as feasible by the aggregate doctrine under every possible weight vector, admit no valid noncompensatory action. The failure on \(I\) is exhaustive: FAST violates the \(s_1\) floor under adverse disturbance; SLOW violates the \(s_2\) floor; STAGED exhausts the reserve stock (\(x < 1\)); and NO-SWITCH fails to reach the destination \(G\).}

\emph{Proof.} 
\textbf{(1)} Typed admissibility requires that the worst-case tube remain within \(S_0\) and that the successor reach \(G\). NO-SWITCH is never admissible because its successor remains at \(q = 0\), failing to reach \(G\). 

For FAST, the worst-case \(s_1\)-tube is \([s_1 - 2, s_1]\), which remains non-negative if and only if \(s_1 \ge 2\). Its successor \((1, x, s + e)\) satisfies \(G\) whenever \(x \ge 0\). Thus, FAST is typed-admissible if and only if \(s_1 \ge 2\). By symmetry, SLOW is typed-admissible if and only if \(s_2 \ge 2\). 

For STAGED, both service floors increase monotonically and the successor lands in \(G\); the sole binding constraint is the physical reserve tube \([x - 1, x]\), which remains non-negative if and only if \(x \ge 1\). 

Combining these conditions, \(E_{\mathrm{typ}}(z) \neq \varnothing\) if and only if \(x \ge 1\), \(s_1 \ge 2\), or \(s_2 \ge 2\).

\textbf{(2)} Fix an arbitrary weight vector \(w = (w_1, w_2) \in W_+\). NO-SWITCH remains inadmissible as it never reaches \(G^w\). 

FAST is admissible under weight \(w\) if and only if its aggregate floor remains non-negative under the worst-case dip: \(w_1(s_1 - 2) + w_2 s_2 \ge 0\), which simplifies to \(w_1 s_1 + w_2 s_2 \ge 2 w_1\) (trivially satisfied when \(w_1 = 0\)). Symmetrically, SLOW is admissible under \(w\) if and only if \(w_1 s_1 + w_2 s_2 \ge 2 w_2\). STAGED is admissible under \(w\) if and only if \(x \ge 1\), as its service floors never decrease.

Consequently, if \(x \ge 1\), then \(z \in \mathcal{V}_w\) for every \(w\), placing \(z \in \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{weak}}\). 

If \(x < 1\), the state is weakly accepted if and only if every weight vector permits either FAST or SLOW. When \(s_1 \ge 2\) (or \(s_2 \ge 2\)), FAST (or SLOW) is typed-admissible by part (1), and therefore valid for all weights. 

It remains to analyze the case where \(x < 1\), \(s_1 < 2\), and \(s_2 < 2\). For weights with \(w_1 > 0\), define the ratio \(r = w_2 / w_1\). FAST is admissible if and only if \(s_1 + r s_2 \ge 2\), which rearranges to \(r \ge (2 - s_1)/s_2 = \rho_1\) (since \(s_2 > 0\); if \(s_2 = 0\), this collapses to \(s_1 \ge 2\), which is excluded). Similarly, SLOW is admissible if and only if \(s_1 + r s_2 \ge 2r\), which rearranges to \(r \le s_1/(2 - s_2) = \rho_2\) (since \(s_2 < 2\)). 

At the boundary weights: when \(w_1 = 0\) (\(r \to \infty\)), FAST is licensed whenever \(s_2 \ge 0\), while SLOW is rejected because \(s_2 < 2\). Conversely, when \(w_2 = 0\) (\(r = 0\)), SLOW is licensed whenever \(s_1 \ge 0\), while FAST is rejected because \(s_1 < 2\). 

Therefore, every weight \(w \in W_+\) admits an action if and only if the intervals \([0, \rho_2]\) and \([\rho_1, \infty]\) overlap to cover all of \([0, \infty]\). This occurs if and only if \(\rho_2 \ge \rho_1\). Evaluating this inequality:
\[\frac{s_1}{2 - s_2} \ge \frac{2 - s_1}{s_2} \iff s_1 s_2 \ge (2 - s_1)(2 - s_2) \iff s_1 + s_2 \ge 2.\]
Both denominators are strictly positive on this domain. Thus, for \(x < 1\), \(z \in \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{weak}}\) if and only if \(s_1 + s_2 \ge 2\). Combining this with the \(x \ge 1\) case yields \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{weak}} = \{ x \ge 1 \} \cup \{ s_1 + s_2 \ge 2 \}\).

\textbf{(3)} The physical operators evaluate only the reserve stock constraint \(x \ge 0\) and regime transition \(q = 1\). FAST and SLOW modify only \(s_1\) and \(s_2\), leaving \(x\) constant; their tubes remain in \(S^{\mathrm{phys}}\) and successors reach \(G^{\mathrm{phys}}\) for all initial states in \(X_0\). Hence, \(E_{\mathrm{tube,phys}}(z)\) is non-empty across \(X_0\), giving \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{phys}} = X_0\). Because endpoint evaluation relaxes trajectory evaluation, \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{end}} = X_0\) holds immediately.

\textbf{(4)} Subtracting the characterization in (1) from that in (2):
\[\mathrm{FP}_{\mathrm{agg}} = \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{weak}} \setminus \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{typ}} = \big[ \{x \ge 1\} \cup \{s_1 + s_2 \ge 2\} \big] \setminus \big[ \{x \ge 1\} \cup \{s_1 \ge 2\} \cup \{s_2 \ge 2\} \big] = \{ x < 1,\ s_1 < 2,\ s_2 < 2,\ s_1 + s_2 \ge 2 \} = I.\]
This set is demonstrably non-empty: the point \((x, s_1, s_2) = (\tfrac12, \tfrac65, \tfrac65)\) satisfies all criteria, and its interior corresponds to the stated open inequalities.

\textbf{(5)} By part (4), \(I = \mathrm{FP}_{\mathrm{agg}} \neq \varnothing\), proving the first inclusion is strict. For the second, consider \((x, s_1, s_2) = (\tfrac12, \tfrac1{10}, \tfrac1{10})\). Here, \(x < 1\) and \(s_1 + s_2 = \tfrac15 < 2\), placing the state outside \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{weak}}\), even though it belongs to \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{phys}} = X_0\).

\textbf{(6)} The licensing intervals follow from the proof of part (2). On \(\mathcal{Q}\), the condition \(s_1 + s_2 \ge 2\) ensures that \(\rho_2 \ge \rho_1\). On its relative interior, both thresholds are strictly positive and finite, leaving a region of disagreement: weights with \(r > \rho_2\) accept only FAST, while weights with \(r < \rho_1\) accept only SLOW. On \(I\), where \(x < 1\), STAGED is unavailable, meaning no single action satisfies all weights simultaneously: \(\bigcap_w E_w(z) = \varnothing\). On \(R\), where \(x \ge 1\), STAGED is viable for every weight.

\textbf{(7)} For any state in \(R\), STAGED's reserve trajectory remains non-negative (\([x - 1, x] \ge 0\)), its service floors improve, and its successor lands in \(G\). On \(I\), every action violates at least one non-negotiable constraint: FAST breaches \(s_1 < 0\) under disturbance \(\alpha\); SLOW breaches \(s_2 < 0\) under disturbance \(\alpha\); STAGED depletes the reserve stock below zero (\(x - 1 < 0\)); and NO-SWITCH fails to transition to \(q = 1\). \ensuremath{\square}

\emph{Boundary behavior.} The ratio expressions for \(\rho_1\) and \(\rho_2\) assume \(s_2 \in (0, 2)\). On the boundary segments (\(s_1 = 0, 2\), \(s_2 = 0, 2\), or \(s_1 + s_2 = 2\)), state classifications follow directly from the closed sets established in parts (1) and (2).

\textbf{Takeaway.} Weak sustainability requires only that the aggregate service budget satisfies \(s_1 + s_2 \ge 2\). Strong sustainability requires either that one service floor can absorb its entire worst-case deficit independently (\(s_i \ge 2\)) or that the system holds sufficient reserve capital to finance a controlled transition (\(x \ge 1\)). The gap between these two conditions represents the zone where compensatory assessment certifies transitions that noncompensatory assessment rejects.

\subsubsection{4.8 Propagation under hold-prefix extension}\label{propagation-under-hold-prefix-extension}

Does this dynamic gap persist when the planning horizon is extended? We examine this by prepending hold intervals to the single-interval model. Remark 6 shows that the gap survives under a clean hold extension, while Theorem 7 provides a counterexample demonstrating that downstream recourse can erase it.

\textbf{Remark 6 (propagation under hold-prefix extension).} \emph{Extend the witness system to \(m \ge 2\) review stages by prepending inactive intervals governed solely by a HOLD policy (where the state is stationary, the tube is \(\{z\}\), the successor is \(\{z\}\), and the safe set is \(S_0\)), with the final interval carrying the active transition. Assume:}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item \emph{(H1) HOLD is available at all prefix states;}
\item \emph{(H2) the hold trajectory tube is the identity \(\{z\}\);}
\item \emph{(H3) prefix safe sets contain the witness states;}
\item \emph{(H4) terminal targets and successor architectures are preserved; and}
\item \emph{(H5) no additional reset or disturbance branches are introduced.}
\end{itemize}

\emph{Then:}

\emph{(i) Structural hierarchy.} \emph{At every stage \(j\), the accepted sets satisfy}
\[\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{typ}}_j \;\subseteq\; \bigcap_{w \in W_+} \mathcal{V}^{w}_j \;\subseteq\; \mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{phys}}_j.\]
\emph{This chain holds for any multi-stage typed exact-tube system by backward induction on constraint sets.}

\emph{(ii) Preservation of strictness.} \emph{Initial accepted states at stage 0 correspond to the single-stage witness sets pulled back across identity hold steps. Thus, both strictness properties from Theorem 5 survive intact.}

\emph{(iii) Delimitation.} \emph{The gap is not an artifact of evaluating a single isolated interval. However, extending strict separation to arbitrary multi-stage systems requires explicit conditions: later stages can collapse the gap if every aggregate-feasible state gains access to a typed-safe continuation, if weak and strong target sets coincide on the reachable domain, or if actions are coupled across stages.}

\emph{Proof.} 
(i) By backward induction. At the final stage, target sets satisfy \(G \subseteq G^w \subseteq G^{\mathrm{phys}}\) by Remark 2. At each preceding stage, the set of viable states consists of those possessing an action that maintains tube safety while landing in the subsequent stage's accepted set. Applying Proposition 3(i) to the stage-level action sets and invoking the induction hypothesis preserves the three-way inclusion. 

(ii) Under a pure HOLD policy, an initial state is accepted if and only if it is currently safe and belongs to the downstream accepted set. Because HOLD acts as an identity operator on states, the stage-0 viable regions are the exact pullbacks of the sets from Theorem 5. Both witnesses (\(I \neq \varnothing\) and the point \((\tfrac12, \tfrac1{10}, \tfrac1{10})\)) remain valid. 

(iii) The three mechanisms noted in (iii) are each sufficient to collapse the gap, showing why multi-stage extensions must be evaluated conditionally. \ensuremath{\square}

\textbf{Theorem 7 (erasure witness).} \emph{The propagation of the acceptance gap across multiple stages depends on system architecture: there exists a two-stage transition system whose single-stage truncation exhibits an acceptance gap, but whose full two-stage execution erases it entirely via terminal coincidence (Remark 6(iii)).}

\emph{Construction.} Consider two capital stocks with floors at zero and no physical reserve stock (\(x \equiv 0\)), so states are pairs \(s = (s_1, s_2)\). 

Stage 2 (the final stage) imposes no trajectory constraint (\(S_0^{(2)} = \mathbb{R}^2\)) and features identical terminal sets for all weights: \(G^{(2)} = G^{w,(2)} = \{ s \ge 0 \}\). It contains a single action, REPAIR, which leaves the state unchanged along its tube and transitions directly to \((1, 1) \in G\). 

Stage 1 enforces the safe set \(S_0 = \{ s \ge 0 \}\) and provides two actions with constant tubes: A1 transitions to \((s_1 - 1, s_2 + 1)\), and A2 transitions to \((s_1 + 1, s_2 - 1)\). Stage 1 targets the accepted set of Stage 2. Consider the initial state \(z^* = (\tfrac25, \tfrac25)\).

\emph{Gap erasure in the two-stage model.} Because REPAIR is typed-admissible from any state in Stage 2, the accepted sets at that stage coincide completely: \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{typ}}^{(2)} = \mathcal{V}_w^{(2)} = \mathbb{R}^2\) for all \(w\). Backward induction to Stage 1 reveals that both A1 and A2 maintain safe tubes and land within \(\mathbb{R}^2\). Consequently, \(z^*\) is accepted under the noncompensatory doctrine at Stage 1, which immediately implies its acceptance under the compensatory doctrine. The acceptance gap vanishes.

\emph{Gap emergence in single-stage truncation.} Now consider Stage 1 in isolation, truncating the model by forcing its terminal targets to match standard terminal sets: \(G = \{s \ge 0\}\) and \(G^w = \{w \cdot s \ge 0\}\). 

Under typed evaluation, \(z^*\) is rejected: neither A1 nor A2 reaches \(G\), because \(s_1 - 1 = -\tfrac35 < 0\) and \(s_2 - 1 = -\tfrac35 < 0\). 

Under weak evaluation, let \(r = w_2 / w_1\) on the interior of the weight cone. Action A1 is admissible if \((s_1 - 1) + r(s_2 + 1) \ge 0\), which requires \(r \ge \tfrac{1 - s_1}{s_2 + 1} = \tfrac37\). Action A2 is admissible if \((s_1 + 1) + r(s_2 - 1) \ge 0\), which requires \(r \le \tfrac{s_1 + 1}{1 - s_2} = \tfrac73\). 

Because \(\tfrac37 < \tfrac73\), these two intervals overlap and cover all of \([0, \infty]\); boundary weights are covered directly (\(r = 0\) is satisfied by A2, and \(r \to \infty\) is satisfied by A1). Hence, \(z^* \in \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{weak}}\). The single-stage truncation exhibits an acceptance gap that contains \(z^*\). \ensuremath{\square}

The witness system in Section 4.5 is immune to this type of erasure. Its gap is driven by trajectory tube violations: across the impossibility region \(I\), every action in the menu violates the intermediate safe set \(S_0\) itself (due to service floor deficits or reserve depletion, as detailed in Theorem 5(7)). Because intermediate tube breaches cannot be undone by later recourse, the gap on \(I\) is structurally uncorrectable downstream.

\subsubsection{4.10 Menu convexification: the blend family}\label{menu-convexification-the-blend-family}

The baseline action menu \(\mathcal{A} = \{\text{FAST}, \text{SLOW}, \text{STAGED}, \text{NO-SWITCH}\}\) consists of discrete, deterministic controls. To examine whether the acceptance gap stems from this discreteness, we introduce control convexification. 

For each mixing parameter \(\delta \in (0,1)\), let \(\text{BLEND}_\delta\) represent the fractional allocation of control inputs:
\[u_\delta = \delta\, u_{\mathrm{FAST}} + (1-\delta)\, u_{\mathrm{SLOW}}.\]
By linearity of the system dynamics, the resulting worst-case trajectory tube and successor states are exact pointwise convex combinations of those of FAST and SLOW. This models continuous control blending—it is not an alternation of discrete actions over time.

\textbf{Theorem 8 (blend collapse).} \emph{On the witness datum of Section 4.5, over initial states \(X_0\):}

\emph{(i)} \emph{\(\text{BLEND}_\delta\) is typed-admissible at state \(z\) if and only if}
\[\delta \in \left[\,1 - \frac{s_2}{2},\; \frac{s_1}{2}\,\right].\]
\emph{This feasibility interval is non-empty if and only if \(s_1 + s_2 \ge 2\) (collapsing to a single point when equality holds), independent of \(x \ge 0\) and of the scalarization weight.}

\emph{(ii)} \emph{Augmenting the discrete menu with the blend family expands the typed accepted set to coincide exactly with the compensatory region:}
\[\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{typ}}^{\mathrm{blend}} \;=\; \{ x \ge 1 \} \cup \{ s_1 + s_2 \ge 2 \} \;=\; \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{weak}}.\]
\emph{Convexifying the action menu entirely eliminates the acceptance gap: \(\mathrm{FP}_{\mathrm{agg}}\) vanishes. Moreover, blended controls reach precisely the compensatory boundary and nothing further—across \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{phys}} \setminus \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{weak}}\), no blend is typed-admissible.}

\emph{(iii)} \emph{The admissible window for \(\delta\) depends solely on the service floors \((s_1, s_2)\). A single choice of \(\delta\) satisfies every weight \(w \in W_+\) simultaneously. On the impossibility region, continuous blending supplies the single unified action that the discrete menu cannot provide. The acceptance gap is therefore a consequence of action-menu geometry, not an inherent divide between sustainability doctrines.}

\emph{Proof.} 
(i) FAST's worst-case trajectory traces \([s_1 - 2, s_1]\) in coordinate \(s_1\) while holding \(s_2\) constant. SLOW's worst-case trajectory traces \([s_2 - 2, s_2]\) in coordinate \(s_2\) while holding \(s_1\) constant. Both actions leave the reserve stock \(x\) unchanged. 

The convex combination \(\text{BLEND}_\delta\) yields a worst-case tube spanning \([s_1 - 2\delta, s_1]\) in \(s_1\), \([s_2 - 2(1-\delta), s_2]\) in \(s_2\), and maintaining \(x \ge 0\). For the tube to remain within the safe set \(S_0\), both minimums must remain non-negative:
\[s_1 - 2\delta \ge 0 \implies \delta \le \frac{s_1}{2}, \qquad \text{and} \qquad s_2 - 2(1-\delta) \ge 0 \implies \delta \ge 1 - \frac{s_2}{2}.\]
This defines the stated interval for \(\delta\). Both original actions share the identical successor state \((1, x, s + e)\), so any convex combination lands at that same destination in \(G\). 

The admissible interval for \(\delta\) is non-empty if and only if the lower bound does not exceed the upper bound:
\[1 - \frac{s_2}{2} \le \frac{s_1}{2} \iff s_1 + s_2 \ge 2.\]
When \(s_1 + s_2 = 2\), the interval contracts to the unique value \(\delta = s_1/2 = 1 - s_2/2\), which is viable because \(S_0\) is closed.

(ii) If \(s_1 + s_2 \ge 2\), part (i) guarantees an admissible blend for any \(x \ge 0\). If \(s_1 + s_2 < 2\), no blend can satisfy the safe set, leaving only the deterministic menu options, which collectively yield \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{typ}} \subseteq \{x \ge 1\} \cup \{s_1 + s_2 \ge 2\}\). The typed accepted set under the blended menu is therefore precisely \(\{x \ge 1\} \cup \{s_1 + s_2 \ge 2\} = \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{weak}}\).

(iii) The feasibility interval for \(\delta\) depends only on \(s_1\) and \(s_2\), with no dependence on the weight vector \(w\). Selecting any \(\delta\) from this interval produces a plan that is typed-admissible, and therefore admissible under all weights. \ensuremath{\square}

\textbf{Remark.} The blend-collapse result shows that a single convexified control policy satisfies every assessor across the impossibility region \(I\), resolving the conflict between the weight-dependent plans identified in Theorem 5(6). 

The mechanism at work here is the geometric convexity of the \emph{action space}, rather than the traditional Pareto curvature that prevents linear weights from reaching non-convex frontiers (Das and Dennis, 1997). This mirrors the distinction between pure and mixed strategies in game theory (von Neumann, 1928; Sion, 1958): treating the planner as selecting an action and the assessor as selecting an evaluation weight, common-plan acceptance corresponds to the pure-strategy maximin value, whereas per-weight acceptance represents the mixed/wait-and-see value. The acceptance gap is the resulting duality gap, which convexification closes. This directly parallels the relationship between here-and-now and wait-and-see formulations in adjustable robust optimization (Ben-Tal et al., 2004).

\subsubsection{4.11 The converse: discrete time-sharing does not erase the gap}\label{the-converse-discrete-time-sharing-does-not-erase-the-gap}

\textbf{Proposition 9 (sequential time-sharing does not erase the gap).} \emph{On the witness datum of Section 4.5, consider a time-shared policy that alternates sequentially between FAST and SLOW during the review interval, such that its visited state set is the union of the two individual worst-case trajectory tubes. Such a policy is typed-admissible if and only if \(s_1 \ge 2\) and \(s_2 \ge 2\). Across the impossibility region \(I\) (where \(s_1 < 2\), \(s_2 < 2\), and \(s_1 + s_2 > 2\)), no sequentially alternated policy is typed-admissible. The acceptance gap therefore survives discrete time-sharing.}

\emph{Proof.} 
Any sequential policy that allocates any non-zero time to FAST visits the full deficit interval \([s_1 - 2, s_1]\) in coordinate \(s_1\). Similarly, allocating any non-zero time to SLOW visits \([s_2 - 2, s_2]\) in coordinate \(s_2\). 

The trajectory tube of the sequential policy contains the union of these visits. For this union tube to remain within the safe set \(S_0\), both coordinates must remain non-negative throughout their respective dips, requiring \(s_1 \ge 2\) and \(s_2 \ge 2\). 

On the impossibility region \(I\), both service floors are strictly below 2, ensuring that any sequential alternation violates the boundary of \(S_0\). Because deterministic menu options fail to provide a common safe plan on \(I\) (Theorem 5(6)), discrete time-sharing cannot extend the noncompensatory accepted set to cover \(I\). \ensuremath{\square}

\textbf{Takeaway.} Taken together, Theorem 8 and Proposition 9 clarify the precise mathematical nature of policy mixing. Continuous convexification—the fractional, simultaneous allocation of control flows—eliminates the acceptance gap across the entire compensatory region \(s_1 + s_2 \ge 2\). By contrast, discrete time-sharing—alternating policies sequentially in time—requires that both service floors survive their deficits independently (\(s_1 \ge 2\) and \(s_2 \ge 2\)). 

Across the intermediate regime where \(s_1 + s_2 \ge 2\) but \(\min(s_1, s_2) < 2\), the acceptance gap survives discrete switching and dissolves only under true control blending. The gap is therefore fundamentally driven by the \emph{convexity of the control menu}, rather than temporal flexibility alone.

\subsection{5. Interpretation}\label{interpretation}

\subsubsection{5.1 The doctrinal reading}\label{the-doctrinal-reading}

The scalarized operators \(\{E_w\}_{w \in W_+}\) formalize one specific interpretation of compensatory sustainability assessment: they model an evaluation regime characterized by a single linear index \(w \cdot s\), non-negative weights on distinct capital forms, full substitutability at those fixed weights, and robust protection against worst-case disturbances. We refer to this construction as the \emph{scalarized aggregate doctrine as formalized here}, rather than weak sustainability in its entirety. The broader weak-sustainability literature encompasses concepts not modeled by our static weights, including dynamic shadow pricing, consumption smoothing, intertemporal welfare optimization, and endogenous discount rates (Neumayer, 2013; World Bank, 2011; Boos, 2015; Dasgupta and M\"aler, 2000; Asheim, 1994). 

Similarly, strong sustainability is not always defined by strict, coordinate-wise non-negativity across all states at all times. Frameworks based on critical natural capital often rely on safe operating spaces, resilience metrics, minimum service thresholds, or systemic irreversibility constraints (Ekins et al., 2003; Doyen and Gajardo, 2020). The typed operator \(E_{\mathrm{typ}}\) serves as an idealized formal benchmark for noncompensatory, separately binding thresholds, mirroring how the scalarized operators formalize linear aggregation.

Within this formal setting, Theorem 5 demonstrates that weak and strong assessment doctrines can arrive at opposing verdicts on the exact same transition problem—sharing identical disturbance models, action sets, and robustness standards—across a robust, open region of state space. 

This disagreement is not driven by an unfortunate selection of weights. Every valid weight in the cone certifies a transition path; the issue is that each weight requires a \emph{different} physical path. The certified actions represent physically incompatible trajectories: FAST and SLOW incur deficits in different resources at different times. This divergence is the dynamic manifestation of inter-capital substitution. By Proposition 3(ii), the root of this divergence is the noncommutation of the existential quantifier over actions with the universal quantifier over weights.

Because full-cone evaluation along any fixed path is entirely lossless (Remark 2), the limitation of the aggregate doctrine does not lie in using an index per se. Rather, it lies in the \emph{policy dependence} of the certified paths: the aggregate index certifies that a safe path exists for any chosen valuation, yet none of these paths are acceptable under a noncompensatory standard. 

This noncommutation can also be interpreted through the lens of information and commitment timing. The order \(\exists a\, \forall w\) requires committing to a single robust transition plan before the valuation weights are known—reflecting the strong doctrine's demand for precautionary robustness. Conversely, the order \(\forall w\, \exists a_w\) allows the planner to adapt the physical transition after the evaluation metric has been fixed. On our witness system, the acceptance gap measures the informational value of knowing that weight in advance. 

Theorem 8 qualifies this information-value interpretation: because a single blended action satisfies every weight simultaneously across the impossibility region, the value of weight information is an artifact of restricting the system to a discrete deterministic menu. Proposition 9 refines this further: that informational value cannot be eliminated by simple temporal alternation; it requires true convexification of the control inputs.

Throughout this paper, elements of \(W_+\) are referred to as non-negative scalarization weights, reserving the term ``prices'' for broader interpretive discussions. True economic prices may be strictly positive, endogenously determined, time-varying, state-dependent, or dimensionally irregular—properties not assumed in our proofs. Our operators evaluate linear combinations with weights held constant across each review interval. Non-linear indices—such as constant elasticity of substitution (CES) functions or barrier penalties that diverge near zero-stock thresholds—represent different mathematical operators and are outside the scope of our theorems.

\subsubsection{5.2 Positioning against established theory}\label{positioning-against-established-theory}

\textbf{Established foundations.} The backward dynamic recursion in Section 3 builds upon classical constructions in viability theory, reach-avoid games, capture basins, and hybrid system verification (Aubin, 1991; Aubin, Bayen, and Saint-Pierre, 2011; Saint-Pierre, 1994; Lygeros, Tomlin, and Sastry, 1999). Proposition 3(i) expresses standard constraint monotonicity for viability kernels under nested constraint sets (Aubin, 1991; Frankowska, 1989). 

The thesis of weak comparability—that environmental values cannot be collapsed into a single metric without loss—was articulated by Martinez-Alier, Munda, and O'Neill (1998); our typed operators provide a dynamical systems formulation of their distinction between strong and weak comparability. The conditions under which sustainability goals can be captured by scalar indicators, along with the operationalization of strong sustainability via maximin and maximum sustainable yield criteria, are developed by Martinet (2011), Cairns and Martinet (2014), and Doyen and Gajardo (2020). In particular, Doyen and Gajardo show that multi-criteria maximin paths solve a static Pareto problem over a viability kernel—formalizing strong sustainability as constraint viability rather than dynamic optimization, precisely as our typed operator does here. 

The limitations of linear scalarization in static optimization are well known: weighted sums cannot access non-convex regions of a Pareto frontier (Das and Dennis, 1997). That limitation is a geometric property of trade-off surfaces under single-objective optimization, distinct from the dynamic quantifier noncommutation analyzed here. Finally, the role of compensability is well explored in multi-criteria decision analysis, which maps compensatory aggregation to weak sustainability and outranking relations to strong sustainability (Cinelli, Coles, and Kirwan, 2014; Sch\"ar, Pohl, and Geldermann, 2025).

\textbf{Contributions of this paper.} 
\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
\tightlist
\item The action-set identity \(E_{\mathrm{typ}} = \bigcap_{w \in W_+} E_w\), which uses full-cone scalarization to demonstrate that the divergence between weak and strong criteria is strictly dynamic (Proposition 3(ii)).
\item The general quantifier-separation principle (Remark 1).
\item The weight-family monotonicity theorem (Proposition 4).
\item An explicit rational witness system exhibiting an open region of strict separation and the rescue/impossibility partition (Theorem 5).
\item The proof that this hierarchy persists when prepending hold intervals (Remark 6).
\item The two-stage erasure model establishing the limits of multi-stage propagation (Theorem 7).
\item The blend-collapse theorem, showing that action-space convexification resolves the gap across the compensatory region (Theorem 8), paired with the proof that discrete time-sharing fails to achieve this collapse (Proposition 9).
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Novelty qualification.} To our knowledge, this work offers the first explicit exact-tube witness demonstrating this dynamic quantifier separation within sustainability assessment. We claim no priority over the underlying logical property \(\exists a\, \forall w \implies \forall w\, \exists a_w\), which is an elementary mathematical fact. Whether an identical dynamical separation has been noted in adjacent literatures (such as robust multi-objective control or viability theory) reflects a bounded review of those fields rather than an established universal absence.

\subsubsection{5.3 Scope delimitations}\label{scope-delimitations}

To ensure the theoretical boundaries of these results are clear, we highlight the following delimitations:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{No scalarization blindness along fixed trajectories.} By Remark 2, full-cone linear aggregation preserves componentwise non-negativity along any fixed trajectory without distortion.
\item
  \textbf{Separation is not universal.} If a system possesses a single action that is robustly safe for all weights, the compensatory and noncompensatory assessments agree. Our result is an existence theorem establishing an open region of divergence, supported by structural monotonicity properties.
\item
  \textbf{Model assumptions.} The formal statements apply to finite-horizon systems governed by exact-tube semantics and bounded disturbance sets. They do not address infinite horizons, stochastic dynamics, partial observability, or endogenous regime transitions.
\item
  \textbf{Weight cone generality.} We do not assume that the full positive cone is the only valid weight family. Proposition 4 proves that restricting the weight family only widens the compensatory accepted set; the full cone is simply the most conservative compensatory standard.
\item
  \textbf{Non-normative weights.} Scalarization weights model an assessment doctrine; they do not imply an endorsement of market prices or welfare assumptions.
\item
  \textbf{Theoretical, not empirical.} These theorems characterize mathematical operators on formal models; they do not provide empirical estimates for any specific ecological or economic system.
\item
  \textbf{Relationship to wealth accounting.} The operator \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{weak}}\) models a strict compensatory standard across all linear weights. It is not an exact implementation of genuine savings or inclusive wealth metrics, and the reserve stock in our witness model is excluded from the index by construction (Section 4.5).
\item
  \textbf{Action-set relativity.} The impossibility region \(I\) is certified as infeasible strictly relative to the defined four-action menu. Expanding the menu can reduce or eliminate this region (Section 5.5).
\item
  \textbf{No doctrinal ranking.} The chain \(\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{typ}} \subseteq \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{weak}} \subseteq \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{phys}}\) characterizes the mathematical behavior of these operators under shared disturbance classes, horizons, and tube semantics. It does not establish a universal normative hierarchy among sustainability paradigms.
\item
  \textbf{Disturbance structure.} The witness system relies on action-indexed disturbances (Section 4.5). If disturbances were structured to force simultaneous deficits across all service floors regardless of the chosen action, both FAST and SLOW would fail together, collapsing the divergence into universal rejection.
\item
  \textbf{Convexification and time-sharing.} Our baseline separation holds for discrete, deterministic action menus. Theorem 8 shows that continuous control blending eliminates the acceptance gap, demonstrating that the gap reflects the discrete geometry of the action space. Proposition 9 confirms that discrete time-sharing cannot replicate this collapse.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{5.4 Policy implications}\label{policy-implications}

The separation theorem yields four direct implications for sustainability governance and indicator design.

\textbf{First.} Aggregate indices cannot, on their own, certify transition safety when critical thresholds are non-negotiable. A scalarized assessment can certify aggregate viability; it cannot guarantee that individual service floors remain intact along the way unless bridged by an explicit structural theorem. As Theorem 5 demonstrates, the compensatory accepted set strictly contains the noncompensatory set, and the gap between them represents a region of dynamic failure.

\textbf{Second.} When a noncompensatory analysis identifies an infeasible transition whose failure is governed by a capital deficit, investing directly in that reserve margin is an effective remedy. Simply recalculating or adjusting the weights cannot resolve the physical deficit on the witness system (Section 5.5). This holds whenever an identifiable, resource-backed rescue action exists.

\textbf{Third.} Per-floor monitoring must accompany aggregate reporting to detect dynamic masking. While aggregate indicators summarize high-level trends, they cannot distinguish between states in the rescue set (which possess a viable physical transition) and states in the impossibility region (which do not). A transparent assessment architecture should document:
\begin{enumerate}[(1)]
\tightlist
\item typed floors and their legal, contractual, or physical provenance;
\item scalarization weights and their permitted ranges;
\item policy quantifier structures;
\item disturbance sets and environmental bounds;
\item observation and control models;
\item exact-tube versus conservative tracking semantics;
\item terminal maintainability guarantees;
\item the completeness of the considered action menu;
\item approximation tolerances and uncertainty bounds; and
\item underlying normative premises.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Fourth.} Assessment regimes that inspect conditions only at periodic reporting dates (such as annual audits) evaluate the weakest operator in our hierarchy (\(E_{\mathrm{end}}\)). Snapshot audits risk certifying trajectories that violate critical safety thresholds mid-interval without detection. On our witness system, the FAST policy satisfies terminal criteria from every initial state in \(X_0\), yet it breaches its safe service floor unless \(s_1 \ge 2\). Continuous or high-frequency tracking of separate floors is essential to identify these intra-interval deficits.

\subsubsection{5.5 Rescue as action synthesis, and data requirements}\label{rescue-as-action-synthesis-and-data-requirements}

\textbf{Rescue as action synthesis.} Rescue operations modify states within the impossibility region. Consider a resource augmentation mapping:
\[\mathsf{Aug}_\kappa : (x, s, \mathcal{A}) \mapsto (x + \kappa,\; s,\; \mathcal{A} \cup \{\mathrm{STAGED}_\kappa\}),\]
and define the minimal required rescue investment as:
\[\kappa^* = \inf\{\, \kappa : \exists a \in \mathcal{A}_\kappa,\; a \in E_{\mathrm{typ}}(z) \,\},\]
where \(\mathcal{A}_\kappa\) denotes the augmented menu. 

\textbf{Proposition (rescue threshold).} \emph{On the witness datum, deploying the STAGED action from a state with \(x < 1\) requires a minimal augmentation of \(\kappa^* = 1 - x\).} 

\emph{Proof.} STAGED is typed-admissible if and only if its reserve trajectory remains non-negative, which requires \(x + \kappa \ge 1\). The smallest non-negative increment satisfying this is \(\kappa^* = 1 - x\). \ensuremath{\square}

Injecting this capital margin converts an impossible state into a viable, typed-transformable one. States already belonging to the rescue set \(R\) require no augmentation, because STAGED is already feasible there; this explains why \(R\) lies outside the acceptance gap.

\textbf{Data requirements for empirical application.} Translating these formal operators to real-world resource systems requires augmenting the model with five operational components:

\begin{enumerate}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Action-set completeness.} An impossibility certificate is valid only relative to the evaluated action menu. Practitioners must clarify whether the action menu \(\mathcal{A}\) is exhaustive, an illustrative shortlist, a discrete sample, or an inner approximation. If incomplete, a negative certificate proves only that no listed policy is safe, not that safe transition is physically impossible.
\item
  \textbf{Calibration and identifiability.} Empirical models require parameter estimates, structured uncertainty bounds, observational error models, missing-data protocols, and sensitivity checks against structural alternatives. A formal disturbance set must be empirically justified, or the resulting certificates must be explicitly marked as conditional on model accuracy.
\item
  \textbf{Governance and execution constraints.} Real-world deployment depends on institutional authority: which actors control which control levers, what information they observe, decision latencies, enforcement fidelity, compliance behavior, and political legitimacy.
\item
  \textbf{Threshold uncertainty semantics.} Critical thresholds are rarely known with certainty. Analysts must distinguish between deterministic floors (\(s_i(z) \ge 0\)), robustly bounded thresholds (\(s_i(z;\theta) \ge 0\) for all \(\theta \in \Theta\)), and probabilistic chance constraints.
\item
  \textbf{Terminal maintainability verification.} Long-term viability within the destination set \(G\) must be supported by an explicit model—whether through physical analysis, dynamic simulation, or policy assumptions.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{6. Discussion}\label{discussion}

\subsubsection{6.1 Negative certificates}\label{negative-certificates}

In transition assessment, a negative certificate—a proof that every action in the menu violates at least one non-negotiable constraint—is a definitive finding, not an inconclusive analysis. Theorem 5(7) is an example: it systematically evaluates every available action, identifies an unavoidable constraint violation for each across region \(I\), and characterizes the resource threshold needed to restore safety. 

Similar formal frameworks have been applied to evaluate historical resource collapses and aquifer management, such as the Northern cod fishery and the Edwards Aquifer (Abaee, 2026b, 2026c). The mathematical results here are established independently by analytical proof and confirmed by the exact rational verification described in Section 4.9.

\subsubsection{6.2 Limitations}\label{limitations}

\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
\tightlist
\item
  The separation theorem is an existence result establishing an open region of divergence. It does not measure the volume of this region in any specific empirical system, and it provides no differentiation in settings where weak and strong criteria happen to coincide.
\item
  Literature positioning reflects a bounded review of adjacent fields (Section 5.2).
\item
  The formal operators are evaluated over finite horizons under exact-tube semantics and bounded disturbances. They do not model infinite horizons, partial state observation, chance-constrained stochastic systems, or endogenous event timing.
\item
  The paper makes no empirical assertions regarding specific ecosystems or economies.
\item
  Extensions to complex governance hierarchies, intergenerational planning, and composite system architectures are presented with partial status in the Supplementary Material and do not enter the formal proofs.
\item
  \textbf{Computational complexity.} On a finite state-action-disturbance graph with constant-time constraint evaluation, backward dynamic recursion runs in polynomial time relative to graph size and horizon length. However, for continuous, hybrid, or partially observed systems, the reachable set can grow exponentially or become undecidable, and grid discretization scales exponentially with dimension (\(N_{\mathrm{grid}} = \prod_{i=1}^n N_i\)). Our witness system avoids these computational bottlenecks because its structure is low-dimensional, finite, and rational.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{7. Conclusions}\label{conclusions}

Weak and strong sustainability are traditionally contrasted as philosophical doctrines—differing primarily in their normative tolerance for natural capital substitution. This paper shows that this divide is also mechanical: when translated into dynamic transition assessment over a shared action menu and disturbance model, the two paradigms diverge as a mathematical theorem about operator quantifier order. 

This result does not establish an inherent superiority of one doctrine over the other. The formal operators represent specific mathematical idealizations (Section 5.1), and Theorem 8 demonstrates that the acceptance gap between them is fundamentally a property of discrete, deterministic action menus (Sections 4.10--4.11). 

Under exact-tube semantics, compensatory evaluation (per-weight acceptance) and noncompensatory evaluation (common-plan acceptance) differ by a quantifier exchange that fails to commute across an open region of state space. Within this region, an aggregate index can certify feasible transitions for every individual valuation weight, even though no single physical transition is acceptable to all weights at once. The accepted states split into two distinct categories: those that can be safely navigated using a resource-backed policy, and those that represent structural impossibilities under the existing action menu. This failure is not driven by static index blindness—full-cone scalarization preserves all constraint information along any fixed path—but by the policy-dependent nature of aggregate certification.

This finding has direct consequences for the design of composite sustainability indices. An index may be methodologically sound as an accounting metric and yet over-certify safety as an assessment tool. Certification is not a static property of a composite score; it is an assertion about the existence of viable dynamic paths. 

Where non-negotiable safety floors are present, reporting disaggregated, per-floor trajectories alongside composite indicators is not a matter of stylistic preference—it is necessary to detect dynamic masking. A single aggregate index cannot distinguish between states that possess a viable physical transition and those trapped in the impossibility region. The vital bridge that any composite index requires—exhibiting a single physical transition that satisfies all admissible weights simultaneously—is precisely what reporting frameworks must be designed to demonstrate. 

Theorem 8 clarifies the nature of this bridge: such a unified plan may not exist within a discrete deterministic menu, requiring instead a convexified control strategy. The key question for transition design is whether a system's physical controls can be smoothly blended—because, as Proposition 9 confirms, simple temporal switching between discrete policies cannot close the gap.
