
# Part VI. Assessment Outputs and Research Programme

## 24. Output portfolio

A sustainability assessment should produce more than a binary label:

1. physical-feasibility result;
2. functional viability result;
3. normative-admissibility result;
4. relational and commons-allocation result;
5. current-state viability-kernel membership;
6. bottleneck and robustness margins;
7. transformation-path status;
8. boundary-interface adequacy statement;
9. unresolved assumptions and proof obligations;
10. evidence and confidence grade.

Possible classifications include:

- sustainable within the current architecture;
- dynamically resilient but normatively inadmissible;
- physically feasible but functionally nonviable;
- relationally noncompliant;
- locally infeasible but recoverable within the architecture;
- transitionally sustainable through a declared architecture path;
- structurally infeasible within known architectures;
- indeterminate because of unresolved interfaces or evidence.

---

## 25. Assessment maturity and certificates

The earlier certification hierarchy is retained but renamed so that preliminary accounting is not confused with sustainability.

- **Level 0 — Assertion:** no explicit model or evidence.
- **Level 1 — Accounting preflight:** principal stocks and flows are internally consistent.
- **Level 2 — Nominal constraint preflight:** the nominal scenario satisfies identified bounds.
- **Level 3 — Dynamic viability:** the actual initial state belongs to an estimated viability kernel under an implementable policy.
- **Level 4 — Robust viability:** viability survives a justified disturbance and model set.
- **Level 5 — Embedded viability:** boundary interfaces, dependencies, commons, distribution, and delayed liabilities are represented.
- **Level 6 — Empirically validated adaptive viability:** predictions, interventions, monitoring, and revisions have demonstrated performance over time or comparable cases.

Levels 0–2 are assessment-maturity levels, not sustainability certificates. Levels 3–6 support increasingly strong qualified certificates.

### 25.1 Certificate contents

A qualified certificate should include:

1. frozen \(\Omega\) and architecture index;
2. typed constraint register;
3. causal and behavioral models;
4. threshold provenance;
5. boundary-interface register;
6. typed dependency and commons map;
7. policy or meta-policy witness;
8. robustness and sensitivity evidence;
9. distribution and risk allocation;
10. identity-continuity result where transformation occurs;
11. monitoring and trigger rules;
12. unresolved proof obligations.

### 25.2 Impossibility and infeasibility certificates

A certificate may identify:

- persistent net depletion under a non-substitutable essential-stock signature;
- local or reachable-set corridor contradiction;
- sink load exceeding capacity and maximum abatement;
- response delay exceeding time to threshold without buffer;
- incompatible contract modalities or interface demands;
- insufficient provision to satisfy registered minima;
- empty implementable action set;
- no admissible transformation avoiding irreversible states;
- commons allocation inconsistent with aggregate capacity.

Such a certificate is conditional on \(\Omega\), the architecture registry, model set, and evidence. It does not prove impossibility under every conceivable architecture.

## 26. Proof obligations

A rigorous assessment should discharge, to the declared maturity level:

1. **Semantic obligation:** constructs and identities are operationally defined.
2. **Accounting obligation:** stock, flow, burden, and allocation records are consistent.
3. **Dynamic obligation:** trajectories follow the stated dynamics or behavioral correspondences.
4. **Boundary obligation:** interfaces and negligibility claims are justified.
5. **Robustness obligation:** results survive the declared disturbance and model set.
6. **Legitimacy obligation:** authority for normative constraints and risk tolerance is explicit.
7. **Composition obligation:** local contracts support the claimed global behavior.
8. **Transformation obligation:** cross-architecture maps preserve registered obligations and identity continuity.
9. **Commons obligation:** aggregate capacity and actor-level allocation are jointly represented.

### Box 13. Template — twelve-step diagnostic

1. Freeze the specification and current architecture.
2. Identify protected functions, values, populations, and higher-order identity.
3. Build the typed constraint registry.
4. Identify essential stocks and enabling capacities relative to protected predicates.
5. Map typed dependencies, boundary interfaces, and commons nodes.
6. Construct relation-level typed contracts.
7. Restrict control to the implementable action set.
8. Stress-test delay, correlated shocks, strategic defection, and alternative boundaries.
9. Estimate current-state viability and robustness margins.
10. If needed, evaluate architecture-transition paths.
11. Conduct adversarial counterexample and respecification tests.
12. Issue a typed result, maturity level, and unresolved-proof-obligation report.

## 27. Leading indicators

Candidate indicators include:

- declining minimum slack;
- falling capacity-to-load ratio;
- increasing recovery time;
- rising control effort required to maintain the same state;
- greater dependence on buffers;
- increasing \(T_r/T_c\);
- declining boundary-interface reliability;
- increasing burden transfer across populations, domains, or time;
- contraction of the current architecture’s viability kernel;
- narrowing of viable transformation options.

Their causal rationale is architectural: declining slack approaches a binding constraint; falling capacity-to-load ratio removes response room; rising control effort can reveal hidden deterioration; buffer dependence finances recurring load from finite reserves; rising delay ratio indicates dynamically inadequate governance; burden transfer converts local improvement into relational failure.

These are candidate monitoring metrics. Their predictive advantage over simpler outcome indicators is empirical, not guaranteed by definition.

## 28. Conjectures and hypotheses

### Conjecture 1. Compositional sustainability

Under identifiable compatibility, timing, robustness, and interface conditions, local typed contracts can establish jointly viable behavior without full monolithic verification.

### Conjecture 2. Transformability

For identifiable system classes, sustained contraction or emptiness of the current architecture’s viability kernel predicts need for architecture change earlier than current output failure.

### Conjecture 3. Capacity-leading failure

Declining regenerative, maintenance, or governance capacity predicts typed sustainability failure earlier than current output measures.

### Conjecture 4. Boundary-expansion reversal

Some favorable sustainability assessments reverse when imported resources, exported burdens, affected populations, and deferred liabilities are introduced through adequate boundary interfaces.

### Conjecture 5. Distributional dynamics

In some system classes, unequal provision and burden alter health, compliance, conflict, and governance enough to change the functional viability kernel, in addition to their normative significance.

### Conjecture 6. Correlated disturbance

Models treating ecological, economic, and social shocks as independent underestimate joint failure where shocks share causes or reinforcing feedback.

### Conjecture 7. Maintenance suppression

Persistent diversion of maintenance toward present output initially improves visible performance while reducing future viability and transformability.

### Conjecture 8. Efficiency–scale interaction

Efficiency improvements do not reliably reduce total burden where scale and rebound exceed intensity reduction.

### 28.1 Falsifiability restrictions

The conjectures may not be rescued by arbitrary post-hoc state augmentation. Each empirical study must preregister:

- system class;
- specification;
- candidate indicators;
- excluded variables;
- predicted direction or comparative result;
- acceptable model revisions;
- observations that count against the conjecture.

The former unrestricted claim that every sustainability failure can be represented at an “adequate scale and resolution” is retired because it is too elastic to falsify.

## 29. Falsification programme

### 29.1 Persistence test

Identify systems that persist while violating declared sustainability constraints. Test whether the typed vector distinguishes persistence from normative or relational admissibility without redefining identity after the result.

### 29.2 Sacrifice test

Identify a system that preserves aggregate function by sacrificing a subsystem. Test whether the typed dependency edge and frozen specification classify the sacrifice prospectively.

### 29.3 Transformation test

Identify cases where lower-level identity disappears while higher-level identity persists. Test the declared continuity relation and architecture translation map.

### 29.4 Innovation test

For innovations claimed to remove a limit, map new energy, material, institutional, distributional, and risk dependencies. Test whether substitution passes the registered criteria.

### 29.5 Conflict test

Represent incompatible normative specifications and authority claims without pretending dynamics derive a unique moral answer. Assess whether the architecture makes disagreements comparable and auditable.

### 29.6 Unknowability test

Use systems with uncertain thresholds or probabilities to test whether scenarios, adaptive monitoring, and precaution remain decision-useful.

### 29.7 Commons test

Use a diffuse burden for which no actor is individually pivotal. Test whether aggregate capacity plus allocation obligations classify free riding and collective failure.

### 29.8 Vacuity test

Attempt to explain failure without adding variables after the event. If the framework requires unlimited post-hoc augmentation, the relevant conjecture fails.

## 30. Empirical programme

For each case, compare four nested approaches:

1. output-only model;
2. stock-and-flow model;
3. disturbance-and-resilience model;
4. typed, embedded, transformation-aware architecture.

The expanded framework earns its complexity only if it improves prediction, diagnosis, intervention selection, boundary transparency, or identification of false local success.

A first test suite should include:

- a fishery, watershed, or grazing commons;
- a water, energy, or public-health provisioning system;
- a region dependent on imported resources and exported burdens;
- an institution facing overload and unequal access;
- a historical transformation with contested identity continuity;
- a global shared-sink case such as greenhouse-gas emissions.

Researchers should preregister \(\Omega\), architecture, constraints, boundary interfaces, contract modalities, and expected leading indicators. Competing models should be retained where uncertainty is material.

### 30.1 Future formal work

Priority formal developments include:

- a sound restricted composition theorem;
- hybrid viability across architecture graphs;
- responsibility allocation under commons constraints;
- methods for approximating typed viability kernels;
- interface confidence propagation;
- identity-continuity logic;
- strategic implementability modules.

### 30.2 Evaluation criteria

The programme should be judged by:

- scope without semantic distortion;
- nontrivial exclusions;
- operationality;
- predictive utility;
- intervention utility;
- transparency of values and assumptions;
- parsimony;
- robustness to respecification;
- falsifiability.

## 31. AI-assisted theory development

Large language models can accelerate synthesis, translation, decomposition, counterexample generation, code production, and interface drafting. They also favor familiar patterns, compressed summaries, plausible prose, and locally convenient repairs. The workflow should exploit the former and constrain the latter.

### 31.1 Roles

1. **Architect:** accountable researchers define the problem, specification, and acceptance criteria.
2. **Generator:** an AI translates claims into definitions, equations, contracts, or graphs.
3. **Adversary:** a separate context searches for omitted assumptions, counterexamples, boundary failures, and incompatible definitions.
4. **Verifier:** formal tools, data, domain experts, and normative authorities test claims.

The same model output is not independent evidence for its own correctness.

### 31.2 Layered rigor

\[
\text{intuition}
\rightarrow
\text{ontology}
\rightarrow
\text{typed claims}
\rightarrow
\text{formal specification}
\rightarrow
\text{module verification}
\rightarrow
\text{composition}
\rightarrow
\text{simulation}
\rightarrow
\text{empirical test}.
\]

A claim should not jump from intuition to theorem.

### 31.3 Tasks aligned with LLM strengths

Useful bounded tasks include:

- compressing raw concepts into candidate relations;
- mapping relations to established mathematical frameworks;
- filling a specified interface blank;
- debugging a broken contract;
- generating counterexamples;
- translating between natural language, equations, graphs, and code;
- checking consistency of a claim ledger.

An unbounded request to “invent a general theory” encourages shallow synthesis.

### 31.4 Controls on shortest-path behavior

- separate generation from evaluation;
- prohibit undefined abstract nouns in formal phases;
- require input, output, assumption, guarantee, and modality signatures;
- decompose claims until each has one principal inference;
- ask for counterexamples before repairs;
- prohibit silent new variables or specification changes;
- require units, limiting cases, and edge cases;
- preserve unresolved gaps;
- use independent adversarial review;
- verify with data, code, model checking, or proof assistants where applicable.

### 31.5 Axiomatic funnel, qualified

Highly certain physical or logical constraints may be fixed within their valid scope, and lower-level propositions required to inherit only permitted terms. Contested empirical claims and normative choices must not be relabeled as indubitable axioms. The funnel begins with a typed premise register.

### 31.6 Forest-to-trees and trees-to-forest validation

Top-down work decomposes the architecture into modules and proof obligations. Bottom-up work checks whether independently supported relations compose into the claimed result. Repeated movement in both directions is required. Top-down work alone risks elegant emptiness; bottom-up work alone risks disconnected detail.

## 32. Limitations

First, the architecture remains a candidate kernel rather than a validated general explanatory theory. Its conjectures require prospective tests.

Second, specification relativity is not eliminated. Competent assessments can disagree because they use different identities, horizons, authorities, or boundaries. The contribution is to expose and audit those differences, not to derive one universal moral solution.

Third, architecture registries will be incomplete. Unknown transformations may exist, and known transitions may fail.

Fourth, contract synthesis and robust composition can be computationally hard or undecidable for general nonlinear and strategic systems. Modularization is an architectural and sometimes computational aid, not a universal tractability solution.

Fifth, social variables require empirical, probabilistic, strategic, and interpretive models. A common interface grammar does not make social and physical mechanisms identical.

Sixth, boundary adequacy depends on evidence and sensitivity analysis. Omitted influences can invalidate certificates.

Seventh, aggregate commons budgets do not determine a uniquely just allocation. Allocation remains an explicit normative and institutional module.

Eighth, high-dimensional viability kernels may require approximations, conservative bounds, simulation, or local proofs.

Ninth, the typed judgment vector can clarify but not automatically resolve conflicts among rights, needs, risks, and identities.

Tenth, placeholders create a risk of indefinite deferral. Every placeholder must carry an owner, scope, evidence requirement, and proof obligation.

---

## 33. Conclusion

This manuscript proposes the architecture of a general sustainability theory rather than claiming its completion. The stable spine consists of:

1. a prospectively frozen sustainability specification;
2. a registry of system architectures;
3. one typed constraint registry;
4. a typed physical, functional, normative, and relational judgment vector;
5. within-architecture viability evaluated from the actual initial state;
6. a separate hybrid transformation operator;
7. explicit boundary-interface adequacy;
8. typed dependency hypergraphs with commons nodes;
9. deterministic, robust, probabilistic, strategic, and scenario contracts;
10. a disciplined taxonomy of definitions, principles, lemmas, conjectures, templates, placeholders, and proof obligations.

The central idea is not that ecological, economic, and social systems obey one equation. It is that sustainability claims about all three require a compatible architecture for possibility, function, value, relation, disturbance, action, boundary, and change.

In compressed form:

\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\text{Sustainability architecture}
={}&
\text{typed admissible viability}\
&+\text{architecture-sensitive transformation}\
&+\text{interdependent composition}\
&+\text{commons-aware responsibility}\
&+\text{auditable boundary assumptions}\
&+\text{prospectively declared identity and legitimacy}.
\end{aligned}}
\]

The architecture is successful only if later formal and empirical work can fill its boxes without redefining the primitive in every domain—and if doing so yields nontrivial exclusions, earlier warnings, or better interventions than simpler approaches.

# Appendix H. AI-assisted verification protocol

1. Freeze \(\Omega\) before generating conclusions.
2. Translate claims into typed registry entries.
3. Require every relationship to state its modality.
4. Use a separate adversarial context to search for omitted interfaces, groups, scales, and architecture alternatives.
5. Convert accepted relations into equations, code, contracts, or explicit placeholders.
6. Test dimensions, limiting cases, and edge cases.
7. Simulate counterfactuals and correlated disturbances.
8. Compare multiple models and boundaries.
9. Submit empirical and normative premises to the appropriate experts and authorities.
10. Use formal proof or model checking for claims presented as logical guarantees.
11. Record unresolved gaps rather than bridging them with prose.
12. Revise the claim ledger only after the relevant obligation is discharged.
