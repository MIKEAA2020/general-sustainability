In plain words:

Think of an orchard as having two things:

Flow: the fruit that grows back each year and can be picked without damaging the trees or soil. Ecologists call this annual regenerative supply biocapacity.
Stock: the trees and soil themselves — natural capital. The stock produces the flow.

If you pick fruit at or below the rate the orchard regrows it, you are living off the flow. That is sustainable. If you cut into the trees/soil stock faster than it regrows, you are liquidating capital — using up the thing that produces future fruit.

Now suppose there are two kinds of land:

Fast provisioning land — like cropland or intensively managed systems. It gives high yields quickly and recovers quickly, but its standing ecological value is small.
Ecological capital land — like forest, wetland, soil, or long-rotation systems. It has large ecological stock and flow, but it rebuilds slowly and is damaged if used faster than it regrows.

Demand is met first from the fast, renewable flow. But if demand is bigger than that sustainable flow, the extra comes from converting ecological capital into agricultural provision. That is land-use conversion — how agriculture has historically expanded by taking over ecological systems.

The key warning: a harvest can look stable, or a total capacity number can even rise, while the ecological capital account is quietly shrinking. In other words, you can appear to be doing fine because you are getting more from fast-yield land, while you are actually running down the slow-rebuilding natural capital that supports future flows.

what are biocapacity units


deepseek response

Biocapacity units are measured in global hectares (gha). Think of a global hectare as a standardized "currency" for nature's productivity[reference:0][reference:1].

Just as you can't directly add up a hectare of lush cropland, a hectare of dry pasture, and a hectare of ocean to get a meaningful total, you can't compare their biological output without a common denominator. A global hectare solves this by normalizing every type of biologically productive land and sea area to a single standard: one hectare with the world-average productivity for a given year[reference:2][reference:3].

🌍 How the Normalization Works

Not all land is equally productive. A hectare of intensive cropland produces far more usable biomass than a hectare of arid grazing land. To make them comparable, each land type is adjusted using equivalence factors and yield factors[reference:4].

Equivalence factors adjust for the type of land (e.g., cropland vs. pasture vs. forest).
Yield factors adjust for local productivity compared to the world average (e.g., highly fertile soil vs. poor soil).

After these adjustments, you get a number of global hectares. For example, if a country has 10 actual hectares of very productive cropland, its biocapacity might be expressed as 15 gha. If it has 10 hectares of marginal pasture, that might be only 3 gha[reference:5].

📊 What the Unit Represents

A biocapacity unit (gha) measures the supply side of the ecological equation: the amount of regenerative capacity—the "flow" from your orchard analogy—that an area can provide[reference:6]. It is the counterpart to the ecological footprint, which measures demand and is also expressed in gha. When a region's footprint exceeds its biocapacity (both in gha), it is running an ecological deficit, meaning it is liquidating natural capital rather than living off the sustainable flow[reference:7].

In short, the global hectare is a tool that lets ecologists and economists put a single, comparable number on the regenerative capacity of everything from a farm to the entire planet.

A global hectare is defined as a hectare with world-average biological productivity for a given year[reference:2]. Writing “gha per year” would be redundant in the same way that we usually say a car’s speed is “60 miles per hour,” but sometimes just say “60 miles” in context. There is also a practical reason. Biocapacity accounts are built from the physical area of productive land and water. You are conceptually right that biocapacity is an annual regenerative flow. The standard unit gha is simply a shorthand that already incorporates the “per year” through its definition of annual world-average productivity. So when you see “biocapacity in gha,” it is best understood as “global hectares of annual regenerative capacity.”

but if it's not fixed, then it keeps changing as avg global productivity fluctuates over the years. does gha choose a particular base year to address this?


deepseek response

You've pinpointed a real and important nuance. The value of a single global hectare is not fixed—it fluctuates because it's tied to world-average productivity, which changes yearly[reference:0]. The Global Footprint Network addresses this with two different approaches, depending on whether the goal is to measure a single year or to compare trends over time.

📊 The Standard Approach: Year-Specific Global Hectares

In the standard National Footprint Accounts, the global hectare is indeed a year-specific unit. A gha represents "one hectare of biologically productive area with world-average productivity in a given year"[reference:1].

This means the equivalence factors used to convert actual land into global hectares are adjusted on a yearly basis. The goal of this yearly adjustment is to ensure that the total number of global hectares on Earth always equals the total number of physical, biologically productive hectares.

The practical consequence is that the total biocapacity of the planet, when measured in these year-specific gha, only reflects changes in the physical area of productive land. It is "entirely insensitive to changes in yields"[reference:3]. If the world's average productivity rises, the value of a single gha rises with it, but the total number of gha stays the same as long as the physical area doesn't change. This makes comparing the size of the footprint and biocapacity in any single year straightforward, but it makes interpreting time trends difficult, because the "measuring stick" itself is changing.

🎯 The Solution for Time Trends: "Constant Global Hectares"

To solve the problem you identified—that a fluctuating unit makes historical comparisons tricky—the Global Footprint Network developed the "constant global hectare" method.

This approach normalizes all data to a single, fixed reference year. Instead of using factors that change annually, it uses a set of "world-average Intertemporal Yield Factors" (IYFs) to express results in hectares that have the world-average bioproductivity of one chosen base year[reference:6].

For example, in the 2011 edition of the National Footprint Accounts, the selected base year was 2008[reference:7]. By using this constant unit, analysts can clearly distinguish between a trend caused by a change in the total physical area of land and a trend caused by a change in productivity or yield over time.

💎 In Summary

So, to answer your question directly: Yes, the issue is addressed by choosing a base year, but only for the specific purpose of analyzing time trends.

For a single-year snapshot, the standard gha is a year-specific unit that floats with global productivity. This is useful for comparing footprint and biocapacity within that same year.
For time-series analysis, the "constant gha" is used, which is pegged to a fixed base year (like 2008) to provide a stable, unchanging measuring stick for tracking historical changes.

The choice of which unit to use depends on the question being asked: "How do we compare now?" versus "How have we changed over time?"

deepseek response

Is capital declining?

In the model: yes, under overshoot — and the decline is permanent without restoration

The model's answer is unambiguous and proven (Proposition 2, Fig. 1a):

When demand exceeds the harvestable flow, A_c falls: the demonstration run shows A_c: 1.500 → 0.808 over ~150 yr while B: 0.594 → 0.825.
The decline stops only when S = 0 (balance point) or A_c hits its floor A_c^min.
At equilibrium, conversion stops — because P = K = B/e implies E = B and S = 0. So the model does not predict perpetual decline at equilibrium.
But there is no return flow. A_c → A_f proceeds through conversion; nothing returns land to A_c except the explicit restoration R_rc. So a past overshoot leaves a permanent reduction in the capital book unless restoration is applied. The paper's Table 5 says "hysteresis / path dependence: No" — but that refers to the equilibrium being unique, not to the level being path-independent. The level is path-dependent: past overshoot scars the capital book.

So in the model: capital declines during overshoot, stays down afterward, and recovers only with explicit restoration. That is a strong, testable prediction.

In the data: not settled — and the paper says so

The paper is explicit (abstract, conclusions): "they are not a forecast and do not show a measured decline in the model's ecological-capital book." That is correct, and it should not be softened.

What the NFA data actually show:

NFA category 1961 → 2022 ratio reading
Cropland 2.85× fast book grew
Forest products 0.90× −10% — closest NFA analogue to capital
Grazing land 0.98× −2%
Fishing grounds 0.96× −4%
Built-up land 8.63× not ecological capital
Non-cropland total 1.008× net of the above

So there is measured decline in the NFA categories closest to ecological capital (forest products, grazing, fishing together fell ≈ 6.4 × 10⁸ gha), but it is offset by built-up land rising 8.6× and by equivalence-factor and re-basing effects. The aggregate is flat because the decline is masked — which is exactly the paper's point — but the masking categories (built-up land) are not ecological capital. So the flat non-cropland total is not evidence that capital is stable; it is evidence that the accounting aggregate cannot distinguish capital decline from built-up expansion.

Three reasons this still does not settle the question:

The NFA categories are not the model's A_c. Forest products is a flow (timber yield), not a standing forest stock. Fishing grounds is a flow, not fish biomass. Soil carbon, wetland, and long-rotation systems have no direct NFA category at all. So the NFA can be consistent with capital decline but cannot measure it.
The magnitudes are small and confounded. −10%, −2%, −4% over 61 years are within the range of re-basing and equivalence-factor revisions (the paper notes the two NFA vintages differ by 1.5 points on the aggregate).
The aggregate conflates yield, area, and equivalence. The paper's own Proposition 1(iii) says the aggregate cannot be inverted into components without independent data. That applies to the NFA series itself.

So the honest position: the NFA data are suggestive of capital decline in the categories that most resemble capital, but they are not a measurement of it.


What would settle it

Five things, in order of importance.

1. Measure the capital stock directly, in physical units

The model's A_c is area of ecological-capital land. The NFA does not measure this. What would:

Forest area and biomass — FAO FRA (area), and growing stock / above-ground biomass (FRA, or remote sensing: GEDI, ICESat-2, ESA CCI Biomass). This gives both area and standing stock.
Soil organic carbon stocks — SoilGrids, WISE, or national inventories. The model's capital land includes soil; the NFA does not.
Wetland extent — Ramsar, GIEMS, or national inventories. Also absent from NFA.
Fish stock biomass — RAM Legacy Stock Assessment database (already used in the SI's regeneration fit). This is biomass, not "fishing grounds biocapacity."
Long-rotation and regenerative systems — no global dataset; would need to be constructed from land-use and management data (LUH2, HYDE, FAOSTAT).

Without this, "capital declining" cannot be tested — only "NFA categories resembling capital declined modestly."

2. Measure b_f and A_f independently, and test the residual

The model's composition identity gives a concrete test. Since

C ≡ B − b_f A_f = Υ_c(q) A_c,

if you measure B (NFA), b_f (FAO yields), and A_f (FAOSTAT cropland area) independently, you get C. If C declines while B rises, that is the composition mask in the data — not just in the model.

The paper already does this as a proxy (X_t = b̂_f,t A_f,t, C_t = B_t − X_t) and finds the residual declining (−0.242 contribution). But the paper correctly labels this a proxy, not an identification, because b̂_f is a cereal-yield sentinel, A_f is FAOSTAT cropland (not the model's fast book), and C contains grazing, forest products, fishing, built-up, and equivalence-factor effects.

To settle it: a cleaner b_f (all-crops production index, or calorie/dry-matter basis), a cleaner A_f (cropland area from multiple sources, cross-checked), and a decomposition of C into its NFA sub-components so that built-up land is excluded. Then ask: does the ecological-capital residual decline?

3. Value standing capital independently (or abandon gha for capital)

The model expresses capital as Υ_c(q) A_c in gha·yr⁻¹ (or gha, depending on the unit convention). This requires a value b_{G,c} for standing capital per hectare — which the paper says is not identifiable from a cross-section.

Options:

Physical units. Measure capital as biomass (Mg ha⁻¹), soil carbon (t C ha⁻¹), fish biomass (Mt), wetland area (ha). Then "capital declining" is a statement about physical stocks, not about gha. This is cleaner and avoids the equivalence-factor problem entirely.
Explicit, defensible valuation. Use net primary productivity (NPP) or carbon as the common metric, with stated conversion factors. This is what the ecological footprint's equivalence factors attempt, but the paper's own critique applies: the weighting is a choice.
Abandon aggregation for capital. Report components (forest biomass, soil carbon, fish biomass, wetland extent) as a scoreboard, exactly as the paper recommends for biocapacity. "Capital declining" then becomes "three of four capital components declined," which is honest and testable.

4. Exploit the panel structure

The global time series is one observation. A country-level panel is much more informative:

Countries with documented cropland expansion into forest (Brazil, Indonesia, DRC) vs. countries with reforestation (China, Europe, India).
NFA land-type accounts exist at country level.
FAO FRA, FAOSTAT, and RAM exist at country level.

A panel would let you test whether B rises while A_c falls in the countries where conversion is documented — a direct test of the mechanism, not of the model. This is the most decisive empirical test available.

5. Distinguish transient from equilibrium

The model predicts conversion is a transient: it stops when S = 0, but the capital book stays down. So the empirical question is not "is capital declining now" but "has past overshoot left capital permanently lower, and is it still being drawn down?"

That requires:

A time series long enough to see the transient (the model's demonstration run is ~150 yr; the NFA series is 61 yr).
A test of whether A_c has stabilized (conversion stopped) or is still falling (conversion ongoing).
A check on whether restoration is occurring — in the model, A_c does not recover without it. Empirically, is there net reforestation, soil recovery, fish-stock rebuilding? In some regions yes; globally, forest area net loss continues in the tropics, soil carbon is still being lost in many regions, and most collapsed fish stocks have not recovered.

The paper's own regeneration anchors (forest t50 ≈ 30 yr, fishery t50 ≈ 5.6 yr, soil 17–23 yr) suggest that if conversion stopped today, recovery would take decades. So "capital declining" and "capital recovering" can coexist across components — which is why a scoreboard is better than an aggregate.


Bottom line

In the model: capital declines during overshoot and stays down without restoration. That is proven and demonstrated.
In the data: not settled. The NFA categories closest to capital declined modestly (forest products −10%, fishing −4%, grazing −2%), but they are offset by built-up land and confounded by equivalence factors, and they are not the model's A_c.
What would settle it: direct measurement of forest biomass, soil carbon, wetland extent, and fish biomass; independent b_f and A_f to test whether the residual C = B − b_f A_f declines; either physical units or an explicit valuation for capital; a country-level panel where conversion is documented; and a test of whether past overshoot has left capital permanently lower.

The paper is right to say the question is open. The decisive measurement has not been made — but the data to make it mostly exist. The gap is not conceptual; it is a measurement programme.

Yes—the data largely exist, and a direct measurement programme is feasible. The main caveat is that each of the four capital components is measured with different methods, at different spatial and temporal resolutions, and with distinct uncertainties. This means they can support a scoreboard of physical capital trends, but integrating them into a single, commensurable “capital stock” requires explicit valuation choices (which the manuscript already flags as non-identifiable from aggregates alone).

🌳 Forest Biomass: High feasibility, maturing global coverage

Availability:

ESA CCI Biomass provides global above-ground biomass (AGB) maps at 1 ha (100 m) resolution for epochs 2007, 2010, and 2015–2022, with per-pixel uncertainty layers[reference:0].
NASA GEDI and ICESat-2 provide lidar-based AGB estimates (circa 2020–2022) that are used to calibrate the SAR-based maps and can serve as independent benchmarks[reference:1].
FAO FRA 2020 provides country-level growing stock and biomass statistics, useful for validation and for longer historical context[reference:2].

Feasibility: High. The ESA CCI product is open, citable, and delivered in standard formats (GeoTIFF/NetCDF). A 2015–2022 change assessment is directly possible. Caveats: Radar saturation in very dense tropical forests reduces sensitivity at high biomass; cross-sensor harmonisation (ASAR/Sentinel-1/ALOS) requires care, and change maps should use the provided quality flags rather than simple differences[reference:3].

🌱 Soil Organic Carbon: High feasibility for stocks, harder for change

Availability:

SoilGrids 2.0 (ISRIC) provides global soil organic carbon stock (t/ha for 0–30 cm) and density at 250 m resolution, derived from machine learning on a global soil profile compilation[reference:4].
FAO GSOCmap v1.5 is a country-driven global SOC map, useful for cross-checking and for national inventory alignment[reference:5].

Feasibility: High for mapping the stock at a point in time. Detecting change over decades is harder: SOC changes slowly, and the uncertainty of global maps may be larger than the decadal signal in many regions. A defensible approach is to use SoilGrids/GSOCmap as a baseline and combine with repeated national inventories or long-term experiments where available. Caveat: the manuscript’s own regeneration anchor (soil re-equilibration in 17–23 years) implies that if land use changed, SOC change should be detectable over a 20–30 year window, but not annually.

💧 Wetland Extent: High feasibility for extent, moderate for condition

Availability:

GIEMS-MC provides monthly global wetland extent at 0.25° for 1992–2020, including separate layers for flooded/saturated wetlands and all wetlands/peatlands, built on passive microwave data and the Global Lakes and Wetlands Database (GLWDv2)[reference:6].
GLWDv2 provides a static baseline map of lakes, rivers, and wetlands[reference:7].
Ramsar site inventories provide higher-quality local data, but coverage is not global.

Feasibility: High for extent and its interannual dynamics. Caveat: GIEMS is coarse (0.25° ≈ 25 km), so small wetlands and narrow riparian zones are missed. For “capital” in the model’s sense, extent is a useful proxy, but wetland condition (e.g., peatland degradation, water quality) is not captured by extent alone and would require supplementary data.

🐟 Fish Biomass: High feasibility for assessed stocks, partial global coverage

Availability:

RAM Legacy Stock Assessment Database compiles stock assessment results (biomass, catch, fishing mortality) for commercially exploited marine populations worldwide. The latest version (v4.66) is openly downloadable via Zenodo[reference:8].
As of recent releases, it contains thousands of stock assessments, with particularly good coverage in the North Atlantic, North Pacific, Australia, New Zealand, and parts of South America[reference:9].

Feasibility: High for assessed stocks. Caveat: Coverage is biased toward well-managed, high-value fisheries in developed regions. Many small-scale, tropical, and unassessed stocks are absent. RAM is therefore a strong measure for a subset of fish biomass, not a complete global census. For a global picture, it must be combined with catch-based estimates or ecosystem models, which carry larger uncertainty.


🧭 Overall Feasibility and How to Use the Data

A direct measurement programme is feasible today as a multi-source reconstruction, not as a single integrated dataset. The practical path is:

Build a physical capital scoreboard. For each year (or epoch), report: forest AGB (ESA CCI), soil SOC stock (SoilGrids), wetland extent (GIEMS), and assessed fish biomass (RAM). Track each series separately.
Test the model’s composition prediction directly. For each component, ask: is the physical stock declining while the corresponding NFA biocapacity category is flat or rising? This is the empirical analogue of the manuscript’s C = B − b_f A_f residual test.
Use the panel structure. All four datasets have country-level or regional subsets. A panel of countries with documented land-use conversion (e.g., tropical deforestation) versus reforestation can test whether capital declines where conversion is occurring, which is a more decisive test than the global aggregate.
Be explicit about what is not measured. Peatland condition, soil depth beyond 30 cm, unassessed fish stocks, and small wetlands remain gaps. The scoreboard should state these limitations rather than imply complete coverage.

The data infrastructure is sufficient to move the manuscript’s central empirical claim from “consistent with” to “testable.” The limiting factor is not data availability but the integration and interpretation of heterogeneous physical stocks—which is precisely the kind of measurement programme the paper’s identifiability argument calls for.
