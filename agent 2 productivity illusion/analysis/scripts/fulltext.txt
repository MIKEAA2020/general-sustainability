Abstract:
Imagine an orchard. The trees produce fruit each year—this is nature’s regenerative
flow, what ecologists call biocapacity. Now imagine the harvesters take more fruit than
the trees can regrow. At first, nothing seems wrong. The trees still stand. The harvest
still comes. But the surplus is not free: it comes from cutting branches, depleting soils,
and eventually killing trees. This is the productivity illusion—the mistaken belief that a
stable or rising harvest signals a healthy orchard—and it lies at the heart of a long‐
standing debate in sustainability science between those who believe technology can
always substitute for natural capital and those who argue that substitution has hard
limits.,In this paper we will develop a minimal mathematical framework that resolves
this debate by showing that both positions are correct, but at different timescales. We
model the environment as a renewable stock with logistic recovery, depleted by the
human ecological footprint. Two distinct time lags—environmental response and
demographic inertia—are introduced, because ecological damage and social
adaptation both take decades to unfold. We will prove analytically that for a sustainable
baseline, neither delay alone can destabilize the system; instability requires both
delays to interact, with the threshold at a combined delay of approximately 80 yr.
During overshoot, an ecological debt variable accumulates and erodes environmental
productivity. Numerical experiments reveal that this debt feedback is the primary driver
of collapse when delays are small, while the interaction of both lags drives total
biospheric collapse by shrinking the basin of attraction around the locally stable
equilibrium. The central finding is that technology can temporarily mask environmental
decline by raising output per unit of natural capital faster than debt erodes it—the
weak‐sustainability regime—but this masking is bounded by the irreversible
accumulation of ecological debt and the limits of logistic regeneration, which reassert
themselves over the long run. Rising biocapacity need not signal improving
environmental health. It may simply be the productivity illusion that precedes a
fundamental reckoning.

Dear Editor, 
I am pleased to submit the manuscript “Overcoming the productivity illusion with delayed 
feedbacks and emergent carrying capacity” for consideration in Ecological Modelling. 
The paper develops a minimal coupled human–environment framework in which carrying 
capacity is not an externally imposed ceiling but an emergent property of environmental stocks, 
productivity, and resource needs. At its core is a two‑dimensional delay‑differential equation 
model with two distinct lags—environmental response and demographic inertia—and a 
cumulative ecological debt that erodes productivity during overshoot. 
I believe the manuscript aligns well with Ecological Modeling’s scope for three reasons. First, it 
contributes a rigorously derived, analytically tractable DDE model that advances the modeling of 
carrying capacity beyond static parameters or fitted logistics. Second, the framework directly 
engages with long‑standing debates about weak versus strong sustainability, showing formally 
that both positions are valid at different timescales—a finding with implications for how 
ecological modelers interpret biocapacity trends. Third, the model’s modular structure, with 
separate delays, a debt term, and an additive technology term, makes it a flexible platform that 
other researchers can adapt or extend. 
The key contributions are: 
 
An analytic proof that, for a sustainable baseline, neither single delay can destabilize the 
system; instability requires both delays to interact, with the threshold at a combined delay 
of approximately 80 yr. 
 
The identification of two distinct collapse pathways: debt‑driven decline when 
consumption exceeds the sustainable optimum, and basin‑of‑attraction shrinkage when 
both delays are present. 
 
A formal reconciliation of the weak‑ and strong‑sustainability paradigms through the 
concept of a “productivity illusion”—the observation that rising biocapacity can mask the 
depletion of natural capital when technology boosts yields faster than debt erodes them. 
 
Stylized numerical simulations, a stability map, and a comprehensive set of robustness 
checks, all independently verified by re‑derivation and re‑execution. 
The manuscript is a pure theoretical contribution; empirical calibration is explicitly deferred to 
future work. I have taken care to make the mathematical reasoning accessible without sacrificing 
rigor, using vivid analogies alongside formal derivations so that the model can be understood by 
a broad readership. However, I am willing to adjust the tone slightly if the journal prefers a more 
formal presentation. 
All simulation code, figure‑generation scripts, and a full independent verification report are 
provided as Supplementary Material to ensure complete reproducibility.

1. Introduction 
38 
Carrying capacity is one of ecology’s most enduring yet elusive concepts. In classical population 
39 
biology, it appears as a fixed number—a ceiling that caps logistic growth. When the concept is 
40 
imported into global human ecology, it is usually treated as a static boundary: the maximum 
41 
number of people the Earth can support (Cohen, 1995). Such treatments overlook a fundamental 
42 
feedback. If a population overshoots its resource base, it degrades the environment that sustains 
43 
it, thereby lowering the future ceiling. The ceiling itself becomes a moving target. 
44 
The Global Footprint Network (GFN) provides a quantitative language for describing this 
45 
interplay through two accounting variables (Borucke et al., 2013). Biocapacity is the annual 
46 
regenerative flow of biologically productive land and water—the apples from the orchard. The 
47 
ecological footprint is humanity’s annual demand on that flow—the apples harvested. When the 
48 
footprint exceeds biocapacity, an ecological deficit occurs, analogous to drawing down a bank 
49 
account. While the annual balance is a flow, its cumulative effect alters the underlying stock of 
50 
natural capital, which in turn determines future biocapacity. The very quantity that defines the 
51 
ceiling is eroded by sustained overshoot. 
52 
A further complication is that neither the environment nor human populations respond 
53 
instantaneously. Forest loss, soil degradation, and climate change operate over decades. 
54 
Demographic patterns and social norms exhibit considerable inertia. These features make the 
55 
system a delay differential equation (DDE) model, a class widely used to capture feedback 
56 
delays in ecology (Kuang, 1993). They also explain why civilizations can thrive for extended 
57 
periods while silently undermining their productive base, only to experience abrupt declines 
58 
(Tainter, 1988; Diamond, 2005). 
59 
Relation to existing literature. The most prominent global human–environment model is the 
60 
World3 system used in The Limits to Growth (Meadows et al., 1972, 2004) and documented in 
61 
Dynamics of Growth in a Finite World (Meadows et al., 1974). World3 contains numerous 
62 
interacting stocks and produces overshoot scenarios through complex feedbacks. Since then, a 
63 
rich literature on coupled human–environment and resource‑harvest dynamics has developed 
64 
(Brander & Taylor, 1998; Anderies, 2000). Estimates of the Earth’s carrying capacity span an 
65 
enormous range, from under one billion to over one trillion people (Van Den Bergh & Rietveld, 
66 
2004), and some scholars have argued that the very notion of a fixed carrying capacity is 
67 
inapplicable to humans (Simon, 1996). These diverging perspectives highlight the need for 
68 
frameworks in which carrying capacity is not an exogenous input but an emergent property of 
69 
the system.

Haberl and Aubauer (1992) first introduced time delay into human population dynamics, 
71 
demonstrating that a hyper‑logistic model with a single lag could reproduce historical population 
72 
trajectories. Meyer (1994) introduced the concept of bi‑logistic growth, showing that carrying 
73 
capacity could evolve not as a single smooth curve but as a series of overlapping logistic pulses, 
74 
each corresponding to a major technological or social transition. Meyer and Ausubel (1999) 
75 
extended this idea to model carrying capacity itself as logistically varying, laying the 
76 
groundwork for the emergent‑carrying‑capacity framework we develop here. 
77 
More recently, several authors have argued that Earth System models must incorporate 
78 
bidirectional feedbacks with human population, inequality, and consumption rather than relying 
79 
on exogenous projections such as those published by the United Nations (Motesharrei et al., 
80 
2016; Donges et al., 2017; Calvin & Bond‑Lamberty, 2018; Henderson & Loreau, 2018). The 
81 
United Nations population projections (United Nations, 2022), which inform most global 
82 
sustainability assessments, assume demographic trends unfold independently of environmental 
83 
constraints—precisely the assumption this paper challenges. 
84 
The present model is distinct from World3 in that it isolates a minimal mechanism: a 
85 
two‑dimensional delay‑differential system in which carrying capacity is not an exogenous input 
86 
but an emergent state variable driven by biocapacity‑footprint accounting, environmental 
87 
regeneration limits, and two distinct lags. Unlike earlier resource‑harvest models that assume a 
88 
fixed resource growth function and a constant harvesting effort, this framework treats both the 
89 
environmental stock and the per‑capita resource requirement as co‑evolving, with cumulative 
90 
overshoot feeding back into productivity. The concept of natural capital as a depreciating asset 
91 
has been formalized by Dasgupta (2021); the present model operationalizes that idea within a 
92 
dynamic, delay‑feedback structure. We will introduce logistic environmental recovery, a debt 
93 
term, and logistic innovation waves. The linearized system yields a transcendental characteristic 
94 
equation whose structure reveals how the two delays interact. The model offers a mechanistic, 
95 
analytically grounded alternative to static carrying‑capacity assumptions.

2. Model Development 
97 
2.1 Environmental dynamics with recovery limits and delay 
98 
Let M(t) denote the effective physical size of the environmental stock—the aggregated stock of 
99 
biologically productive land and water, measured in global hectares (gha). This is the orchard: 
100 
the trees, the soil, the water systems that generate biological production year after year. The 
101 
stock regenerates logistically up to a maximum potential size Mₘₐₓ, and is depleted by the human 
102 
ecological footprint E(t), which we measure in gha per year. The coefficient γ converts the 
103 
annual footprint demand into a net depletion of the productive stock; it carries the units required 
104 
to make Eq. (1) dimensionally consistent.¹ 
105

106 
resource extraction removes stock immediately; the delay here is a mathematical shortcut. It

2.2 Biocapacity and carrying capacity 
121 
Global biocapacity B(t) (gha yr⁻¹) is the annual regenerative flow generated by the environmental 
122 
stock, modulated by a productivity factor b(t) (yr⁻¹). Productivity here captures technology, 
123 
management practices, and ecological health—everything that determines how much fruit each 
124 
tree yields. 
125 
B(t) = b(t) M(t). (2) 
126 
Carrying capacity K(t) (population) is the maximum sustainable population given a reference 
127 
per‑capita resource requirement rₒₚₜ(t) (gha cap⁻¹ yr⁻¹). If the average person requires a certain 
128 
amount of biologically productive land to support their lifestyle, then the total population the 
129 
Earth can sustain is simply the total biocapacity divided by that per‑capita requirement. 
130 
K(t) = B(t) / rₒₚₜ(t) = b(t) M(t) / rₒₚₜ(t). (3) 
131 
Carrying capacity is emergent: it arises from the evolving state variables M(t), b(t), and rₒₚₜ(t), 
132 
rather than being prescribed externally. This separation reflects a core distinction: the 
133 
environmental stock M is the orchard that produces the harvest B, and technology can increase 
134 
the yield per tree without increasing the number of trees. 
135

ago.

2.4 Ecological debt 
153 
Persistent overshoot degrades the functional quality of the environment—soil fertility, 
154 
biodiversity, hydrological integrity—even if the physical area remains unchanged. We will 
155 
model this degradation as a first‑order process in which the accumulated deficit D(t) (measured 
156 
in gha) increases whenever demand exceeds supply. Think of D(t) as an odometer of overshoot: 
157 
it counts how much, cumulatively, we have taken beyond what the Earth can regenerate. 
158 
dD/dt = max(E(t) − B(t), 0),  D(0) = 0. (6) 
159 
We adopt a phenomenological specification for the resulting productivity loss: the effective 
160 
productivity coefficient is 
161 
b(t) = b₀ exp(−α D(t)) + T(t). (7) 
162 
Here α (gha⁻¹) scales the sensitivity to accumulated debt, and T(t) (yr⁻¹) represents technological 
163 
innovation, modeled as an additive contribution. The exponential form ensures b(t) remains 
164 
strictly positive, reflecting diminishing marginal damage: each additional unit of debt reduces the 
165 
remaining productivity by a fixed proportion. However, a positive b(t) does not prevent M from 
166 
collapsing to zero—if the depletion term γE dominates the regeneration term ρM(1−M/Mₘₐₓ), the 
167 
environmental stock shrinks regardless of how productive each remaining hectare is. A variant 
168 
with debt repayment (dD/dt = max(E−B,0) − ηD) is tested in the Supplementary Material; the 
169 
qualitative conclusions are unchanged for plausible η. 
170

172 
and Ausubel (1999). Each wave corresponds to a major historical transition—the agricultural 
173 
revolution, the Industrial Revolution, the Green Revolution, the digital era—and raises the

2.5 Policy extensions 
182 
The Half‑Earth proposal (Wilson, 2016) caps human‑usable biocapacity at half of the total:

3. Analytical results 
188 
3.1 Steady state in the constant‑parameter version 
189 
With constant b = b₀, no debt (α = 0), and constant rₒₚₜ and e, the non‑trivial equilibrium (M*, P*) 
190 
satisfies P* = K* and E* = e P*. Equation (1) gives 
191 
ρ M* (1 − M*/Mₘₐₓ) = γ e P*. 
192 
Using P* = b₀ M* / rₒₚₜ, we obtain 
193 
M* = Mₘₐₓ (1 − γ e b₀ / (ρ rₒₚₜ)). (10) 
194 
An interior equilibrium exists if and only if 
195 
γ e b₀ < ρ rₒₚₜ. (11) 
196 
Linear stability of the non‑delayed subsystem additionally requires the equilibrium to lie on the 
197 
upper branch of the logistic, i.e. M* > Mₘₐₓ/2. From Eq. (10), this condition is 
198 
ρ > 2 γ e b₀ / rₒₚₜ. (12) 
199 
When both conditions hold, the constant‑parameter, delay‑free system is locally stable. In the full 
200 
debt–innovation model, the effective productivity varies over time and the conditions apply only 
201 
locally.

3.2 Linear stability and characteristic equation 
203 
We linearize the constant‑parameter subsystem around (M*, P*). The full derivation is in 
204 
Appendix A. The result is the transcendental characteristic equation 
205 
λ² − a₁₁ λ + γ e a₂₁ exp(−λ τₘ) + r (λ − a₁₁) exp(−λ τₚ) = 0, (13) 
206 
where 
207 
a₁₁ = ρ (1 − 2 M* / Mₘₐₓ), 
208 
a₂₁ = r b₀ / rₒₚₜ. 
209 
The dimensional consistency of Eq. (13) has been independently verified; all terms carry units 
210 
yr⁻². 
211 
The structure of the characteristic equation reveals an important property. The two delays enter 
212 
through separate exponential terms with different coefficients—γ e a₂₁ multiplies exp(−λ τₘ), 
213 
while r (λ − a₁₁) multiplies exp(−λ τₚ). For the sustainable baseline (Scenario A, e = rₒₚₜ), setting 
214 
either τₘ = 0 or τₚ = 0 produces polynomial conditions in ω that have no positive roots for the 
215 
baseline parameter set. An analytic proof eliminating the delay via the identity 
216 
sin²(ωτ) + cos²(ωτ) = 1 yields, for τₚ = 0: ω²(10 000ω² + 2504) = 0, and for τₘ = 0: 
217 
ω²(2500ω⁴ + 1199ω² + 143.5) = 0. Both polynomials have strictly positive coefficients for ω > 0, 
218 
so neither can have a positive‑frequency root. This result holds under the condition 
219 
r² a₁₁² ≥ (γ e a₂₁)², which is satisfied for the baseline parameters but may be violated if the 
220 
depletion pressure γ e a₂₁ is sufficiently large relative to the stabilizing term r |a₁₁|. When the 
221 
inequality reverses, a single delay can in principle trigger a Hopf bifurcation even in the 
222 
sustainable case. For the present parameter choices, however, no single delay can destabilize the 
223 
system—instability requires the interaction of both delays. The threshold is located at a sum of 
224 
the two delays, τₘ + τₚ, of approximately 80 yr for the baseline parameters, with the exact value 
225 
depending on the ratio of the two lags. 
226 
For Scenario B (e = 1.15 rₒₚₜ, overshoot), a one‑delay Hopf bifurcation does exist at τₘ ≈ 83 yr 
227 
(with τₚ = 0), with the associated frequency ω ≈ 0.026 rad yr⁻¹. This reflects the destabilizing 
228 
effect of overshoot even in the constant‑parameter subsystem. The value is robust to the 
229 
numerical root‑finding method; additional Hopf crossings occur at intervals of approximately 
230 
242 yr, consistent with the dominant eigenvalue branch.

4. Numerical simulations (stylized) 
232 
Simulations use an Euler method with a history array and interpolation for delayed quantities. 
233 
This Euler approach follows the tradition of early DDE population models (Haberl & Aubauer, 
234 
1992) and is sufficient for the long timescales studied here; more sophisticated DDE solvers

318 
Reconciling weak and strong sustainability: the productivity illusion. A central theoretical 
319 
contribution of this model is its formal reconciliation of the weak‑ and strong‑sustainability 
320 
paradigms. The separation of environmental stock M and productivity b creates two channels 
321 
through which biocapacity B = bM can change: the stock can grow or shrink (an ecological 
322 
channel), and the yield per unit stock can rise or fall (a technological channel). These channels 
323 
can move in opposite directions, producing the productivity illusion. 
324 
When technological innovation T(t) raises b faster than the cumulative debt αD erodes it, 
325 
biocapacity can rise even as the environmental stock M slowly declines. This is the 
326 
weak‑sustainability regime: technology substitutes for natural capital, and aggregate output 
327 
masks the depletion of the underlying asset. The global data from 1961–2022 are consistent with 
328 
this interpretation: biocapacity grew modestly while ecological overshoot persisted, suggesting 
329 
that technology has been outpacing degradation. 
330 
However, this substitution cannot continue indefinitely. The system can bear overload for a 
331 
time—an elevator rated for 10 people will still manage 12, showing only subtle wear before the 
332 
cable suddenly breaks—but the underlying stock is quietly eroding. Liebig’s Law of the 
333 
Minimum states that biological productivity is limited by the scarcest essential resource. As 
334 
critical components of M—soils, aquifers, pollinators, stable climate—are depleted beyond 
335 
recoverable thresholds, the marginal productivity of technology must eventually saturate. The 
336 
debt term exp(−αD) continues to erode b from below, and if M falls below the logistic 
337 
regeneration threshold, the environmental stock can enter a self‑reinforcing decline. This is the

354 
Limitations and future work. The analytic results apply to the constant‑parameter subsystem; 
355 
the full debt–innovation model is studied numerically. The logistic recovery model neglects 
356 
hysteresis and irreversible thresholds. The candidate stability boundary is a preliminary 
357 
root‑tracking estimate; a rigorous DDE bifurcation analysis would be needed for a definitive 
358 
stability map. The Half‑Earth extension is included as a policy illustration; its quantitative effects 
359 
depend on the parameter choices and should not be interpreted as a forecast. The model currently 
360 
treats technology as purely productivity‑enhancing (acting through b only) and bounded 
361 
(logistic). A natural extension, motivated by the finding that even massive productivity boosts 
362 
cannot prevent delay‑driven collapse, would allow technology to also restore or expand the 
363 
environmental stock directly—representing reforestation, soil recovery, or carbon capture. A 
364 
structurally richer model distinguishing natural from managed capital, each with its own 
365 
dynamics, would further sharpen the empirical predictions. The modular structure—separate 
366 
delays, a debt term, and an additive technology term—makes it straightforward to replace any 
367 
component with a more detailed formulation, such as an age‑structured demographic model or a 
368 
different damage function. These extensions are left to future work.

Appendix A: Derivation of the characteristic equation 
391 
We linearize the constant‑parameter subsystem (α = 0, b = b₀, constant rₒₚₜ, e) around the 
392 
equilibrium (M*, P*). Define deviations m = M − M*, p = P − P*. 
393 
Environmental equation (1). At equilibrium, dM/dt = 0. For small deviations, 
394 
dm/dt = ρ (1 − 2M*/Mₘₐₓ) m − γ e p(t − τₘ). 
395 
Define a₁₁ = ρ (1 − 2M*/Mₘₐₓ). Linear stability of the non‑delayed subsystem requires a₁₁ < 0, 
396 
i.e. M* > Mₘₐₓ/2. 
397 
Carrying capacity. K = b₀ M / rₒₚₜ, so ∂K/∂M = b₀ / rₒₚₜ. 
398 
Population equation (4). At equilibrium, P* = K*, so the term 1 − P*/K* = 0. We expand the 
399 
logistic term to first order in the deviations m and p. The term P(t−τₚ) / K(t) is a quotient. 
400 
Applying the chain rule, its partial derivative with respect to M is 
401 
∂/∂M [P(t−τₚ) / K(M)] = − P* / (K*)² · ∂K/∂M = − (1/K*) · (b₀/rₒₚₜ). 
402 
The logistic term is 1 − [P(t−τₚ) / K(M)], so the derivative with respect to M flips the sign: 
403 
∂/∂M [1 − P(t−τₚ)/K(M)] = + (1/K*) · (b₀/rₒₚₜ). 
404 
Multiplying by the outer factor r P* = r K* yields the coefficient (r b₀ / rₒₚₜ) for m. The derivative 
405 
with respect to the delayed population is straightforward and gives −r p(t−τₚ). Putting these 
406 
together: 
407 
dp/dt = (r b₀ / rₒₚₜ) m − r p(t − τₚ).
