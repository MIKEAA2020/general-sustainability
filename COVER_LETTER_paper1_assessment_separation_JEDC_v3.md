# Cover Letter — Paper 1 (JEDC)

**Title:** Aggregate Indices and Transition Safety: A Quantifier-Order Separation Between Scalarized and Coordinate-Wise Feasibility
**Author:** Amin Abaee (Independent Researcher)
**Journal:** *Journal of Economic Dynamics and Control*

---

Dear Editor,

I am submitting the manuscript **"Aggregate Indices and Transition Safety"** for consideration in the *Journal of Economic Dynamics and Control*.

**What the paper is about.** Many sustainability rules require several separate minimum standards to hold at the same time — for example, a service that must never fall below a legal floor, and another service that must never fall below its own floor. An authority that has to carry out a transition can be judged in two ways. One judge adds the standards into a single weighted score and asks whether *some* plan keeps the score above zero. The other judge asks whether a *single* plan keeps both standards above zero along the entire path. The paper shows that the two judges can genuinely disagree: on a small, explicit example, every weighting of the score approves some plan, yet no single plan keeps both standards safe. The weighted score is not wrong at any one moment — it is misleading at the level of the decision, because it approves a change that no available plan can actually carry out safely.

**Why it matters.** This is the formal version of a familiar situation: an aggregate looks healthy even though no workable plan exists. The paper shows exactly where the disagreement lies, proves that a reserve budget fixes it when one is available, and shows that running two plans at once as a fractional mix removes the problem, while merely switching between the plans over time does not. The practical lesson is that, where separate minimum standards matter, they should be reported separately — the aggregate alone cannot tell a fixable case from a hopeless one.

**An everyday picture.** Imagine two hospital wards, each with a legal minimum staffing level. A fast reorganization briefly draws down one ward's surplus; the other fast plan draws down the other ward's; a gradual plan avoids both drawdowns but needs a reserve budget. A regulator who reads only a weighted total of the two staffing surpluses may find that, for every weighting, some plan keeps the total positive — while no single plan keeps both wards above their legal minima, and the reserve budget is too small to phase the change gradually. That is the gap the paper formalizes.

**Scope and method.** The results are stated precisely and proved in full, with the proofs collected in an appendix. A small worked example is additionally checked by exact computer arithmetic (no rounding), and the code and results are archived. The paper makes no claims about any particular resource system; the companion studies are cited only for context, and the manuscript is self-contained.

I confirm that the manuscript is original, is not under consideration elsewhere, and that all authors have approved the submission. There are no competing interests.
