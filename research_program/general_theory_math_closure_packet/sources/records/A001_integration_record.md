## High-value direct contributions to the current architectural manuscript

### 1. Operator hierarchy

The article sharply distinguishes quantifier structures:

- controlled viability: existential control;
- robust viability: one causal strategy against all nonanticipating disturbances;
- epistemic viability: one observation-based strategy over every compatible state;
- institutional viability: restriction by authority, enforcement, allocation, and information.

This should refine Operator I rather than create a parallel definition of sustainability.

### 2. Actual-state and attainability semantics

Definition 2.3 explicitly states that kernel nonemptiness does not establish sustainability from the actual state. Section 3 then distinguishes viability from attainability and recoverability. This directly supports the architectural correction already adopted.

### 3. Recovery requires an emergency envelope

Proposition 3.1 shows that capture into the viability kernel while remaining inside the same constraint set is trivial: the capture basin equals the kernel. Nontrivial recovery therefore requires a larger emergency envelope. This is a strong, non-obvious result and should replace generic recovery language in the flagship.

### 4. Epistemic viability

Belief-state kernels, common-action conflicts, hidden modes, delayed information, observer margins, and the value of information provide rigorous content for the manuscript’s observability and information-capacity placeholders.

### 5. Barrier and obstruction certificates

The article supplies a reusable calculus for sufficient invariance and finite-time exit. This can turn the architectural certificate section into a mathematical programme rather than a checklist alone.

### 6. Explicit resource and sink kernels

The order-minimal control principle and scalar kernels provide actual computed viability regions. These can replace or supplement the flagship’s illustrative ecological ODEs.

### 7. Resource–capital and distributional coupling

The joint viability results directly connect ecological limits, productive capital, consumption floors, and distribution. They provide formal substance for the typed constraint registry and sustainability corridor.

### 8. Substitution thresholds

The dimensionally correct CES treatment provides a rigorous bridge between strong/weak sustainability and economic substitution. Its distinctions among essentiality, finite substitution, and unbounded substitution are especially valuable.

### 9. Coupling can destroy or create viability

The two-patch counterexample and rescue example demonstrate that subsystem viability does not imply system viability and that coupling is not uniformly harmful. This is central to the compositional architecture.

### 10. Networks and commons

The spectral-radius counterexample, dynamic cascade theorem, Nash over-extraction results, and quota mechanisms fill the typed-hypergraph and commons placeholders with formal cases.

### 11. Institutions as implementation operators

The representation of institutions through information, admissibility, enforcement, and allocation closely matches the architecture’s implementable-control hierarchy and contract modalities.

### 12. Horizon and stochastic distinctions

The generation-structured constraints, discounting counterexample, p-viability, and finite/infinite stochastic split give rigorous content to horizon typing.

## Required harmonization bridges

### Bridge B1. Typed sustainability semantics

The article generally places physical, functional, consumption, distributional, and institutional constraints into one set \(\mathcal V\). The master architecture permits this geometry but requires typed provenance and a judgment vector.

**Action:** map every article constraint to the typed registry. Theorems remain conditional on the declared \(\mathcal V\); they do not validate the normative authority that selected consumption or distributional floors.

### Bridge B2. Notation registry

The article uses:

- \(C\) for carrying capacity;
- \(K\) for sink burden;
- \(\mathcal V\) for constraints;
- \(A\) for productive capital;
- \(H\) for harvest;
- \(\mathfrak I\) for institutions;
- \(\mathcal I\) for information.

The architectural manuscript has used \(K^*\) for the constraint geometry and \(\mathbb A\) for architectures. Direct merger would create collisions.

**Recommended canonical choice:** retain the article’s domain notation in technical modules, use \(\mathscr C\) for the constraint registry, \(\mathcal V_\Omega\) for its generated target set, and \(\mathbb A\) for architecture registry. Avoid \(K^*\) as the flagship’s generic constraint symbol because \(K\) is a substantive sink state in the formal article.

### Bridge B3. Finite- and infinite-horizon operators

Much of the article uses infinite-horizon viability. The master architecture indexes claims by \(T\).

**Action:** label every theorem as finite horizon, infinite horizon, stationary, or transition-time. Keep infinite-horizon theorems intact rather than silently generalizing them to finite \(T\).

### Bridge B4. Recovery versus transformation

The emergency-envelope capture basin remains within one fixed dynamical architecture. Operator II changes architectures.

**Action:** define recovery as a subcase of Operator I with relaxed temporary constraints; define transformation as a hybrid path whose segments may each use emergency envelopes. Do not identify capture with architecture change.

### Bridge B5. Information state as an architectural state

Belief sets are genuine state variables for epistemic viability.

**Action:** permit an augmented architecture \(Z_q^{\mathrm{epi}}=Z_q\times\mathfrak B_q\), or treat belief dynamics as a linked epistemic module. State clearly which projection produces physical, epistemic, and institutional kernels.

### Bridge B6. Physical primacy and architecture change

The article’s physical-primacy theorem is valid for fixed physical actuators and information. It distinguishes governance restrictions from technology that adds actuators.

**Action:** map actuator-changing technology to Operator II. Within one architecture, institutions restrict or select from physical possibilities. Across architectures, institutions and technology can alter \(U_{\mathrm{impl}}\), information, enforcement, and even the physical actuator set. Avoid the overly broad slogan that governance only restricts in every sense.

### Bridge B7. Contract modality

The article’s control, observation, game, and institutional results imply deterministic, robust, epistemic, and strategic modalities.

**Action:** translate each theorem into a typed contract entry rather than presenting the whole social module as deterministic.

### Bridge B8. Composition

Theorem 16.1 provides a restricted sufficient composition theorem, while Section 10 gives coupling counterexamples.

**Action:** present Theorem 16.1 as one proved box inside the broader Compositional Sustainability Conjecture. State that general contract synthesis and nonlinear circular composition remain open.

### Bridge B9. Commons allocation

The article models Nash extraction and quota enforcement but does not fully address alternative fair allocations of a global commons budget.

**Action:** embed the game results beneath the master commons node. Separate physical aggregate safety from normative allocation among actors.

### Bridge B10. Architecture constructors

The article’s constructor algebra changes \(U\), \(F\), observation, effort rules, and realized harvest without introducing new state spaces.

**Action:** classify each constructor as within-architecture parameter/control modification or as an architecture transition. A constructor that changes the official tuple \((Z,F,U^{impl},B,I,\partial)\) should generate a new architecture index even if it introduces no new state variable.

### Bridge B11. Intergenerational normative inheritance

Generation-specific constraint sets may encode changing values as well as physical conditions.

**Action:** tag the provenance and authority of each \(\mathcal V^{(k)}\). A shrinking sequence is not automatically legitimate merely because it is mathematically defined.

### Bridge B12. Quantitative margin alignment

The article’s dynamics-aware value \(V_T(x)\) is stronger than the flagship’s geometric minimum slack.

**Action:** retain both. Geometric slack is a transparent diagnostic; \(V_T\) is the preferred dynamics-aware safety value when computable.

## Targeted verification queue before direct theorem integration

The user reports that the mathematical content is mostly verified. The following items nevertheless require focused review because they contain visible notation issues, scope ambiguities, or proof steps that could affect integration. These are review flags, not final judgments of invalidity.

### V1. Controlled-system notation

Section 2 writes \(\nu(t)\in U(x(t))\) after defining control \(u\). Confirm and correct the symbol.

### V2. Robust tangency semantics

Theorem 4.5 invokes Nagumo-style viability while concluding robustness against every disturbance. Confirm that the assumptions establish strong invariance for the closed-loop disturbance inclusion, not merely existence of one inclusion trajectory.

### V3. Order-minimal control wording

Theorem 6.1 correctly supports an existential viability equivalence, but the proof says that if the minimum-harvest trajectory remains viable, “all trajectories” remain viable. Higher-harvest trajectories are worse under the stated order and need not remain viable. Correct the sentence while preserving the existential conclusion.

### V4. Pollution-feedback comparison sign

In Theorem 6.6(b), \(\partial g/\partial K\le0\) and \(K\le K_{\max}\) imply \(g(S,K)\ge g(S,K_{\max})\), whereas the proof text appears to say the slice lies below the frozen slice. Confirm the intended inequality.

### V5. Capital lemma and higher consumption

Lemma 7.1 only needs the viable choice \(c=c_{\min}\). The proof additionally suggests every larger consumption remains above the lower kernel boundary, which is generally not guaranteed. Remove or qualify that sentence.

### V6. Generic non-polyhedrality

Proposition 10.1’s open-dense algebraic argument should be checked carefully. The existence of slope/intercept parameters and projection of algebraic conditions may require a more rigorous transversality or elimination argument. Consider relabeling as a conjecture if the genericity proof is not complete.

### V7. Rosen diagonal strict concavity

Lemma 12.1 permits heterogeneous \(d_i\), but the proof appears to replace all \(d_i''\) by one common \(\gamma\). Verify sufficient conditions for the heterogeneous game or restrict the theorem.

### V8. Commons obstruction definition

Theorem 12.2 uses \(H_{\mathrm{safe}}\) without a fully local formal definition in the theorem statement and then invokes a uniform negative drift. State sufficient drift conditions explicitly.

### V9. Quota rescue and harvest floor

Corollary 12.1 uses \(\sum_i h_i^{quota}\le H_{\min}\) but then applies a theorem with a required lower harvest floor \(H\ge H_{\min}\). Equality or an admissible safe interval may be required.

### V10. Sanction sufficiency

Theorem 13.3 proves that payoff declines above quota but does not by itself prove quota is the unique best response rather than a lower action. Add conditions below quota or weaken the conclusion to “no profitable upward deviation.”

### V11. Ostrom sufficiency and obstruction

Theorems 13.5–13.6 should be checked for consistency with output floors, collective-choice behavior, and the later statement that individual necessity remains open. Distinguish “each principle can be necessary in some constructed case” from “each is universally necessary.”

### V12. Implementation lattice

Proposition 13.1 calls the sets downward closed only when the smaller correspondence still contains a viable selection. That condition effectively assumes membership. Clarify the actual order-theoretic property; arbitrary contraction of a viable control set can destroy viability.

### V13. Theorem and cross-reference audit

Check theorem numbering and references, including the conclusion’s hierarchy, references to Theorem 13.2 versus 13.5, and use of EViab/ERViab/IRViab.

### V14. Institution notation and architecture distinction

The article uses \(\mathfrak I\) for institutions while the master architecture uses related calligraphic symbols for information and identity. Harmonize without obscuring the original results.
